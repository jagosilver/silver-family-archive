# Social and contextual analysis of *'When The Wind Blows'* by Raymond Briggs

Raymond Briggs', *'When The Wind Blows'* (Hamish Hamilton 1982), is a
shocking and horrifying account of the realities of nuclear war told
through the eyes of a naïve and trusting elderly couple.

The story follows the last few days of Jim and Hilda's life as they
struggle to survive a nuclear strike. The book tells how, hearing on the
news of an impending nuclear war, Jim collects all the 'official'
leaflets from the Council and Government in order that they know what to
do. Jim follows the instructions for building a survival shelter and
takes all the 'correct precautions'. Hilda isn't that concerned about
the whole thing and neither of them really realise why this war will be
any different from the last (WWII). The fact that Hilda wouldn't have
known there was a war, if not for Jim highlights how little some of the
general public at the time knew about the realities of the nuclear
threat.

Neither of them have any idea of the scale of a nuclear blast or the
devastation it causes. They have been told of 'fall-out' but don't
realise that you can't see it, or understand what it is and how it
affects them. They are completely unaware of the real implications of
radiation sickness, and of the fact that there is no real hope of
survival. They are very much reliant on the Government to tell them
what's going on and to sort things out afterwards. Little do they
realise that in the aftermath of the nuclear strike there is no
Government. Both characters base their reactions to nuclear war on their
experiences in WWII, with disastrous consequences.

They have almost fond memories of the last war and really can't see how
this one will be any different. They remember the war cabinet of
Churchill, the caricatured enemies, Hitler, Mussolini and expect the
current Government to be similar.

Following the nuclear strike the couple wonders whether or not they've
won and of course here the book highlights how ridiculous the idea of a
nuclear war is, because no one can possibly win it. The book also
mentions the idea of '*mutual assured destruction*' whereby it was
thought impossible for Russia say to launch a nuclear missile at
America, because the US would instantaneously retaliate with one of
their own and both countries would be wiped out. This was intended to
provide comfort to survivors of an attack; at least 'the enemy' was
annihilated too.

Having read Briggs' 'Ethel and Ernest' it seems likely that the
characters of Jim and Hilda were based on his parents. Through his use
of sympathetic, believable characters Briggs manages to humanise the
extremely inhuman concept of a nuclear war.

The style of the book is and would have been familiar to many people,
Briggs was already well known for his children's books, *The Snowman*
and *Father Christmas*, when this came out in 1982. This fact would have
meant that he already had a large audience who were likely to buy the
book simply because he'd written it. The book is presented as a comic
strip, in common with other Briggs books; in fact all that differs is
the subject matter. Although comics and the comic strip were firmly
established in the US, in this country, graphic novels were not so
prevalent and the book would probably have been assumed a children's
story on first sight. This in itself makes the book's statement all the
more powerful because it is unexpected. By using a familiar style and
friendly characters, Briggs manages to deliver a hard-hitting message to
people who might otherwise have ignored it and because comic strips are
more usually associated with books for children, the topic of a nuclear
war being depicted in one was shocking.

Visually, his characters in *'When The Wind Blows'* are very similar to
those in his children's books, friendly, almost cuddly looking people.
They are portrayed as an old married couple of the sort you wouldn't
mind as neighbours. His use of colour through the book is interesting.
The illustrations start off colourful and jolly, identical to other
children's books of his. In the aftermath of the bomb the colours slowly
fade to a sickening pallor following the characters decline and eventual
death. There is something chilling about the way the originally comic,
rotund figures are gradually changed into the bewildered, dying
characters of the end of the book.

The book works simply as a story and carries a frightening message about
nuclear war, but to most people reading it at the time it was far more
than that. The book was published in a climate of growing tension
between American and Russia, with Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan
embarking on a fundamentalist anti-Soviet crusade.

The threat of nuclear war was very real, and as in the book, leaflets
were issued by the government, giving instructions on how to survive a
nuclear strike. Although these leaflets were ridiculed at the time, for
providing pointless information, Jim and Hilda find comfort in following
their instructions; at least then they feel they are doing something.
Some people at that time felt that they really were living under a
three-minute-warning and that at any moment war could break out and so
for them this book has greater significance. The book highlights the
fruitlessness of any of the Governments 'civil-defence' strategies, at a
time when underground nuclear bunkers were being built; not for the
general populace (and certainly not for working class elderly couples),
but for specially selected politicians and civil servants.

Throughout the 80's CND was campaigning for nuclear disarmament and was
considered such a threat by the government that a major part of Micheal
Heseltine's job as Minister of Defence was to lead a well-funded
counter-CND propaganda unit. However, CND was never going to reach
everyone and many people who would never have dreamed of supporting a
'pressure group' *would* have bought Briggs' book. This is perhaps
connected to the fact that Jim and Hilda seem to be quite definite
working class characters with working class attitudes to life and
politics. This may have attracted working class readers to the book, who
would have been put off by the CND by its predominantly middle class
membership.

The original audience of the book is quite hard to define. Though as a
comic book it would seem to appeal to children and is certainly simple
enough for most to follow, it is likely that many parents at the time
would have considered the topic to frightening and the story too
distressing for their children.

Personally I think that Raymond Briggs intended the book to be read by
children, as a warning against a possible future, after all what adult,
having read '*When The Wind Blows*' as a child would support the idea of
nuclear weapons? I know that since reading it as a young child I have
always been horrified at the thought of them.

This book probably has a secondary audience by now, as those who bought
it or read it when it came out, now buy it for their own children.
Though the message may not have the same topicality it did for the first
generation of readers I think the book will still move people and change
their perceptions of 'The Authorities'.

The obvious message of the book is that nuclear war is 'a bad thing',
but the story also promotes an idea of people thinking for themselves.
The characters Jim and Hilda have total trust in 'The Authorities' and
believe everything they say. This story tells us to think for ourselves
and not assume that the Government knows best. It is warning us that by
following the Governments orders unthinkingly Jim and Hilda have ended
up bewildered and dying in a poisoned, pointless world. This message
would have had extra poignancy at the time of publication because of the
huge power commanded by Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan. Decisions
were being made by these two leaders that affected the lives of
millions, and perhaps too many people were just accepting,
unquestioning, what they were told.

It could be said that this book is propaganda, and indeed in some senses
it is. The view it gives is very one sided and only shows the negative
side of things. However when you think about it there are no positive
sides to nuclear war, all Briggs is doing is being brutally realistic.
He is saying through the book, what the Government at the time was
afraid to admit. That in the event of a nuclear strike there is no hope
of survival. It is this brutal realism teamed with his usual humorous
touches and the naivety of his characters that make the book so moving
and it's message so powerful.

Having read many different reviews of '*When The Wind Blows*' they all
agree on one point; some say it is harrowing, others say it is moving
but all say that this book is essential reading. That, it seems is the
crux of the issue with this book; Raymond Briggs has managed to bring an
unpleasant, unpalatable and inhuman subject to the masses. By using
subtle combinations of black humour and horrific realities, combined
with all too human characters and a children's book format he creates
the ultimate contribution to the anti-nuclear movement.

## Conclusion

Does this book still have the same social relevance it did when it was
first published? With the new Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev,
negotiations to remove nuclear missiles went ahead and a treaty was
signed in 1987. The reforms of Gorbachev led to a calming of the
situation and the eventual collapse of the Soviet Union. With this, the
threat of a Nuclear Holocaust receded and ever since has gradually faded
in the public's mind. However with a new US President backing the 'Son
of Star Wars' ballistics plan and a British government apparently happy
to go along with it, the threat may yet return.

As for now, the book retains it's horrifying nature and deftly delivers
it's message, though it may not seem as close to home as before.
