From [Skegness](Skegness-Letters), [David Silver](David-Silver) went in January 1944 to **RAF Cosford**, near Wolverhampton, for an eighteen-week course as a Flight Mechanic (Engines). These thirteen letters to his parents at **Tresillian** in Exeter cover the whole course.

He files steel plates to a thousandth of an inch, learns Whitworth threads, carburettors and magnetos, and admits "I should never make an engineer." He tries hard to escape: he applies to become a meteorologist, is turned down, and is told by his Wing Commanding Officer that he is too young for a commission and must "LEARN TACT". Around the course: an afternoon in Wolverhampton with a hut-mate who finds everything "too dear", visits to his brother James at the Oxford Group's house in Cheshire, the International Ballet, evening classes at the School of Art, a midnight stranded in Birmingham, and tea with a pilot officer whose mother would not let David move to another table. In March he predicts the invasion "within 3 days of April the 8th or May the 8th" from the moon, and in May he passes his final board. A few weeks later he wrote from [Little Horwood](Little-Horwood-Letters) on D-Day.

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. Only three letters are dated, so the others are dated from his leave passes and the news; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. Missing pages are marked. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it.

## 1944

### 01. Sunday, c. 9 January 1944 (just arrived)

*Undated ("Sunday"). He travelled from Skegness "on Thursday" (6 January 1944, the week after his twentieth birthday), and leave rules changed "until January 1st this year".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver
> [Circled, with an arrow to the address:] 179 Entry — most important it goes in between G.10 and 4 wing.
> G.10 4 Wing
> R.A.F Station
> Cosford
> Albrighton
> Sunday Nr Wolverhampton.
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I am so sorry that it's such a long time since I have written to you a proper letter. Well here I am in the West Midlands about 60 miles south of Chester and 90 miles North of Bristol. I travelled here from Thursday at 6.30 am till about 5.30 in the evening.
> Well before I go any further with describing what has happened since I arrived, I will tell you about leave.
> Until January 1st this year weekend leave was obtainable almost every weekend. Of course they must go and change that now that I've arrived.
> Leave is evidently a very weak point in the
>
> *(p. 2)*
> R.A.F. There are people here who haven't had any for 10 months (seven days privilege I mean)
> Well I should get mine about the last week in May. (seven days).
> That sounds bad but it is made a little better by allowing me two 36 hrs passes and one 48 hr.
> Normally I should add the 48 hrs to the seven days but it is a very long time to go without any so I don't know whether I shall take the 48 when I get it.
> I should be allowed a 36 hr pass in four weeks time. This leaves me free from lunch time Saturday to 7.am Monday.
> I have found out about the trains and there is one that would get me in to Exeter at 10 pm. on Saturday night and another at about 10 pm Sunday which would take me back by 7 am on Monday morning.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> The same would apply to the forty eight only it would give me from 10 pm Friday night till 10 Sunday night.
> Well I could take the two thirty six hour passes (which I lose if I don't take) and come home after four weeks and after 12 weeks and keep the forty eight for my seven days to make nine days. Or I could take the forty eight when it comes in the eighth week and either miss the thirty sixes or possibly how far is Uncle Herbert away, I wondered whether I could see him. I can get a pass any time I like at the weekend for as far away as I like providing I come back to camp by 11.59.
> I don't ever have to be in before 23.59 but there is nothing to go out for so they are pretty safe in giving us that privilege.
> I like the idea of taking the two
>
> *(p. 4)*
> thirty sixes or at least trying the first one and saving the forty eight for my seven days.
> ~~Well~~ Now here as they say in the R.A.F. is the "pukka gen" (reliable information) on the camp.
> How much I can tell you I don't know. It's a large permanent camp with wood huts centrally heated linoleum floors, well lit, double decker beds [tiny sketch of a bunk] wash basins baths conveniently adjacent hot or warm water, a cinema, roller skating rink, swimming baths, billiards table tennis, Ensa concerts, boxing, choral society, Toc H, Dancing Badminton, libraries, - technical educational and for pleasure. Schools of maths and other subjects.
> ~~Well, As you may as well know~~
> I've just noticed that I put 'Well' at the beginning of almost every paragraph.
> As you can see from these entertainments although a lot of them don't suit me, it's a pretty good camp.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I would go further and say that this is one of the de luxe camps of the service. (this sounds a bit like a public speech)
> You don't get away from the R.A.F. housemaid duties here. I emptied a G.W.R. truck of coke into a lorry yesterday afternoon (not me alone, two others). in fact we emptied three trucks.
> What has my work consisted of so far? filing. I don't think it's very secret, just a piece of iron to be filed accurately to a certain size and shape (not as easy as it sounds)
> I have just chosen Flight Mech Engines in preference to airframes. It's quite a concentrated course (18 weeks) and will require quite a lot of intelligence thank goodness.
> I am going to have a shot at doing Architecture in my spare time.
> My bed is comfortable, the room is warm, the water is warm, and the food is good and plentiful.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> That means it's a good camp.
> It has to be a good camp as it's out in the country. It has to be self sufficient. I don't much want to see Wolverhampton.
> I came through Peterborough Coventry Birmingham Wolverhampton. The last two were the most filthy depressing smoking places I have ever seen in the whole of my life. Mile upon mile of scrap metal, smoke, cabbages, dustbins and chimneys.
> — — —
> I've just had lunch which consisted of roast beef potato Brussel sprouts apple tart and synthetic cream.
> As you can tell by my letters I place a great stress on meals and warmth but after the first few weeks at Skegness had you
>
> *(p. 7)*
> seen what it was like I don't think you'd have been surprised.
> You ought to see my hands, I wonder if I'll ever get them back to look respectable. They are cracked all over and I find it very difficult to get the dirt out of the cracks.
> I am much fatter than I was in the face.
> Last night I went to "Five Graves to Cairo" it was a story built round Rommel's advance to Alamein and how he cached food and supplies before the war in Egyptian graves ~~and again~~.
> As regards washing, I shall send my shirts vests etc to the camp laundry but I'll still post my collars and socks handkerchiefs and my own underclothes. This address is all right though it may be changed slightly later.
> The country round here is not bad nicer than Lincolnshire.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> Lincolnshire was quite beautiful sometimes but relied on the light effects to make it so. There seemed an enormous amount of sky. Now in Staffordshire, at least I think that's where I am, there are very slight hills and over to one side which I think is the West there is a high range of hills, very high ones like mountains.
> I should like to know what you think of the idea of taking the 36 hrs and leaving the forty eight. I am quite equal to travelling all night.
> I thought perhaps one Sunday I might be able to see James just for the day.
> I had a dental inspection and passed fit. It was a very brief inspection in fact he never ever used a [tiny sketch of a dental probe] he just looked. He had a chair like Mr Lawson's a runabout table on wheels a nice engine I believe like yours though I am not sure. [Tiny sketch of a dental engine arm.]
>
> *(p. 9)*
> I reported sick to get some more glasses this morning. I shall get them on Friday I hope.
> My hut is full of Scots. I can't understand a word they say and they can't understand me.
> I should be quite handy with tools by the time I've finished here, I never knew there was a knack in fitting filing before. James sent me a tooth brush and nail brush and a lovely sort of sponge bag idea made of waterproof material. [Drawing: a roll-up toilet case with pockets, and a soap dish.]
> Well that's all for now I can hardly hear myself think in here, we've got a wireless and it's such a treat after having none that they have it full on to celebrate
> With love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* Cosford was one of the RAF's great permanent technical training stations. He has chosen "Flight Mech Engines in preference to airframes" and means to "have a shot at doing Architecture in my spare time".

### 02. Sunday, c. 16 January 1944 ("not too dear")

*Undated ("Sunday"). His first 36-hour pass is "the fifth of February", "only 3 weeks!" away, and he had been on coal-heaving fatigues "last week end", as the first letter describes.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 SILVER D.R.
> G.10, 4 T.T. Wing,
> Royal Air Force Station
> Cosford, Albrighton
> Sunday. Nr Wolverhampton.
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you very much for your letters which seem to get better and better. That sounds awful but what I mean is I seem to be able to visualize what's going on at home better and better from them.
> Thank you also Mummy for the laundry and the pre-war chocolates. How on earth did you get them, they are lovely. I haven't seen any real chocolates like that for years.
> Fancy old Kit saying he would rather stay out in Italy to the end of the war. He must have an exaggerated idea of how bad the billets are in England.
> Do you see those peace proposals of Germany put to Britain through the neutrals. It's in to-day's Sunday Times?
> I hope you enjoyed thanking the donor for the 'cellophane sack' as you describe it Daddy. (that's in code you see that's why it's worded like that.)
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I should … to popularity among the other officers when he inoculated the battalion including officers. I wonder if he did himself.
> I had realized I was nearer Exeter by the distinctly warmer climate, though I suppose that may have been the East coast and the sea.
> I seem to remember Daddy you said you could do with some of the fresh bracing air at Skegness. Well as regards bracing air the place is a flop, it's no more bracing than Dawlish Warren. It is simply cold and occasionally windy.
> By the way à propos of Skegness I saw "rime" there for the first time. I think it's called "rime" an ice coating which forms overnight about ¼" thick all over the roads and pavements. It was awfully difficult to march on it.
> This place is a hundred times better than Skegness.
> Now for this burning question of leave thinking of which and devising railway routes to Exeter occupy my spare moments.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> Your boiling down of my notes had one mistake in it which I think was probably a misprint.
> You said
> " A one 36 hour pass at the end of 4 weeks
> B . . . . . . . . 8 weeks
> C one 48 . . . . . . 8 weeks or add to (D)
> D one 7 day leave"
> Erratum! for 'B' pass at the end of 8 weeks read 12 weeks. Otherwise it's the same.
> It's one 36 at the end of four weeks one 48 at the end of eight weeks and one 36 at the end of twelve weeks.
> I shall do as you say I think. I'll take the one at the end of four weeks that makes it the fifth of February. and I shall take the one at the end of twelve weeks, keeping the forty eight to add to my leave if I am allowed to do that. [there is a slight chance that I might save the 48 and then not be allowed to add it on I'll have to find out about that].
> The twelfth week one comes about April 2nd.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> Of course it's no good absolutely ~~on~~ counting on these short leaves as anything may happen but those dates I've given you are as near as possible correct.
> By the way Mummy if it's ever occurred to you I might be going abroad you can rest assured that it is impossible before June. I don't mean that I shall go after that but I merely mean it's possible after then. Uncle Herbert seems rather far away to get at though I suppose if the trains are suitable I might be able to see them on a Sunday just for the day.
> I just visualized James ~~then, or rather~~ his friend and his companion, and your consternation on having them sprung upon you so suddenly. I am glad they turned out quite nice.
> Yes, Auntie Lillah, did send me a blue envelope thing for an identity card which was very nice of her and I've been trying to remember to thank her for ages, in fact it preys
>
> *(p. 5)*
> on my mind at night so that I wake up and find myself writing letters of thanks on my tin hat!! No, actually I did have it but really I get so little time to spare after I've finished work that I don't ~~really~~ want to write letters all the time. I do ~~really~~ intend writing to her but she must wait her time as I've already written twice to James (he sent me a present for my birthday which I didn't expect just after I'd thanked him for a Christmas present) twice to Wiggs I think, once to Martie. The funny thing is that you haven't said anything about Martie getting a letter from me. Do ask her next time you see her as I'm positive I wrote one while I was at Skegness.
> I must write to Nanny, that is essential.
> I've written to Auntie Muriel ages ago now Getting moved and so on has mucked me up. This weekend is the first one I've had off for a fortnight, last week end here I was on coal heaving fatigues.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Well now one more thing about leave. The train times:—
> I seem to have left them in the hut so I'll put them in at the end of the letter. Anyway they give me 24 hrs on the thirty six pass and 48 on the 48 hr pass.
> This, I may say, is not what you get on most camps which just goes to show what a good camp this is.
> If I had been at Locking or Lockett near W. S. Mare. I should have not done much better as they are very strict there about going out of camp.
> This really is a very good camp. I washed in hot water this morning. I haven't been able to wash in hot water in the morning for weeks. (No Jokes Daddy)
> All the toughening-up course muscles I gained at Skegness are fading away and I feel much more like the me of earlier days pre-Cardington.
> Next I'll tell you about my work which this past week has been of a really useful nature - I hope!
>
> *(p. 7)*
> I can't outline how many weeks I have on this or that although I know. I can tell you however that I finish up by being able to service ("keep in order") a bomber or fighter's engine(s).
> This last week I have done 'basic'. Basic consists of learning the ordinary tools of what they are made and how to handle them. The "Job" I had to make consisted of a metal plate which had to be filed to a rectangular specified size ~~or~~ extremely accurately, sawn in two and fitted together one piece on the other with two bolts made by me and a locking plate also made by me [Drawings: the plate, with a dotted line marked "sawn"; then the two halves bolted together.] that isn't a very good description as I can't remember the names of one or two things even now but it gives you an idea anyway.
> I had eight files, a scriber, three hammers a hacksaw a number of chisels reamer etc.
> I found it difficult to make accurate (1/1000 inch is what they call accurate)
>
> *(p. 8)*
> I have learnt also many mysteries about British Standard Whitworth threads, Duralumin (tougher than steel standing a pressure of 26 tons per sq inch) and hundreds of other things. To keep up with it I had to do 1½ hrs work in the evenings.
> If I pass out high enough I go up two ranks and might get put on a fitter course. Don't hope for this though as it's an unheard of thing for anyone to pass out L.A.C. (leading aircraftsman) from scratch. Most of them have been garage mechanics, tool manufacturers carpenters etc. I was the only one in my class who hadn't done any engineering or mechanical work before. It's child's play to a lot of them, I had never seen a tap or reamer before some of them have been using them all their lives. So don't expect too much.
> By the way in case you are a bit vague about the ranks in the Air Force below officers I'll give them
>
> *(p. 9)*
> [A table:]
> Air Force — Army Equivalent
> A.C.2. — Private
> A C 1. — no equivalent
> L.A.C. — no equivalent
> no equivalent — L/Corporal
> corporal — corporal
> sergeant — sergeant
> Flight sergeant — Sgt Major
> W.O. — W.O. (Sgt Major is then a Battalion S.M)
> Officer — Officer
> The LAC in the airforce although next to corporal is not an N.C.O. He has higher pay than AC1 and more authority but he is not an N.C.O. like a Lance Corporal: He wears a propeller on his arm [tiny sketch of the propeller badge].
> In Technical Training you can only be promoted by the courses you've done. If for instance I passed out L.A.C which is not even a remote possibility I should then go on a fitter course from which it would be possible to pass out corporal and so ad infinitum. But it stops at Sgt as a matter of fact and it's only possible for civilian engineers to get as far as that.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> That's enough about work.
> Now about Wolverhampton. Yesterday I took the afternoon off and leaving everything just as it was I went out of camp for the first time since my arrival, boarded the train with ~~also~~ a station full of others and set off for "WOLVES" as it's called here.
> The station is small and smoky the city is also smoky. It's a dirty place, sooty appearance. Blackened houses, roaring trolley buses, packed with people and overhead the same sky as I had seen when we passed through in the train, yellow and sooty with smoke. The shops about as good as the Exeter ones before the Blitz only more of them. Do you know what the population is? Do you think you would look in the RAC guide and tell me please as I'd be interested to know. Many cinemas and theatres. People going everywhere but somehow nowhere to go when you get there.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> We sleep in double decker beds and I sleep on the lower deck. Seeing that I was going out 'upper deck' who comes from Birmingham inquired where I was going. I had to tell him and he said that he would come too. This was not my plan as I had intended looking in bookshops and he is a dull motor mechanic sort of boy. However he tagged on and we went to Wolverhampton together.
> I will never go again with anyone.
> It was awful. I wanted a sketchbook a little one with hot pressed Whatman. It was 3/4½ and I never heard the last of it. First every single thing in the shops he looked at was too dear, not that he wanted them, and then not content with not allowing himself to buy anything, because it was too dear, because I wanted this sketchbook he nearly went mad. I bought it and he lamented the wastage of my money for the rest of the day. At the end of the day I discovered that he thought it was a writing pad!
>
> *(p. 12)*
> He lead me further and further away from the decent big shops towards the sort of 'South Street' shops. He said one might pick up a bargain in a small shop but never in a large one!
> He had no definite objective in view. I thought how extraordinarily his mind must work. He was obviously out to buy the first article that he thought was not "too dear" whatever it was.
> He tried to buy a padlock of very cheap design as he thought it a bargain and then dropped it again like a red hot coal when he found that all the keys were alike for the padlocks. Once again it was 'too dear'.
> I steered him skilfully back to the larger shops and we went into a sort of "Wheatons". There there was a book which would have done admirably for our basic course at 10/6. It was a large nicely arranged book, I noticed that it
>
> *(p. 13)*
> was American and would therefore be useless to us as the screws etc are all different sizes and names to our own. He picked it up and looked at it. He was obviously interested and looked it through, he looked at the price and put it down. It was "too dear".
> I remarked that it would have been of no use anyway as it was American. He picked it up to check my statement and satisfied that I had spoken the truth he put it down contented. 'Pity it ~~was~~ is American" he said "I would have bought it otherwise"!!
> We had tea at an enormous store rather like Colsons only not as good quite. It was called Beattie's you may know the name. It was better than Waltons or anywhere like that.
> I really wanted to go to a decent restaurant, however this was not bad. Being in the Midlands they didn't know what tea was but had a sort of light supper.
>
> *(p. 14)*
> We sat down at a table half of which was occupied by two women in enormous fur coats who held cigarettes at fashionable sort of angles and talked in bad midland accents about their immediate neighbours.
> I felt rather ~~uncomftable~~ uncomfortable about crashing in on their table but the place was nearly full and my companion had the longing for a "fish and chip shop" look in his eye and so we anchored.
> We looked at the menu all teas were one and ninepence. "Not too dear" said he in a loud voice, picking the card up. After that followed a long period in which we couldn't make each other understand what we were saying.
> When tea finally arrived he said in front of the waitress "Oh that's why it's not so dear". I nearly died.
> I guided him to a canteen where mercifully he fell asleep. I read the papers and waited till 7.30
>
> *(p. 15)*
> when I woke him up and we went to the station. On the way for some reason he suddenly insisted that we were going the wrong way and pointed out a turning going in the wrong direction as the one to the station. Luckily I had noted the different turnings on my way into the centre of the town. I in turn insisted on my way and won. We went along my route. All the time he grumbled that we were going the wrong way that we should miss the train and that it was my fault. He nearly worked in that it was "too dear" only it didn't seem applicable.
> We arrived at the station as I had forecast much I think, to his disappointment where we waited on the platform.
> He felt that it would be unwise in fact definitely wrong to spend any money on getting a sandwich at a services canteen for supper and so we sat on the station till the train came in.
>
> *(p. 16)*
> Well that's what happens if you let somebody else "come along too".
> I shan't make that mistake again I would sooner be on my own.
> It's funny I never make friends with them, I suppose it isn't really, I have nothing in common with them. There aren't any people from - well the sort of schools like Bruton here.
> There were two at Skegness, quite nice but they were posted elsewhere.
> Anyway I get on all right with my companions in my hut although I think we have a mutual feeling of not understanding each other.
> I am sure they think me very queer but not half as queer as I think them.
> If I am ever short of cash I shall go out again with my companion of yesterday, I shall be pretty safe.
> I am sorry this letter has degenerated as it went on.
> With love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] only 3 weeks!
>
> *(p. 17)*
> Leave Train.
> Depart Cosford 1.5 G.W.R.
> Depart Wolverhampton 1.55. L.M.S.
> Depart Birmingham 3.6 L.M.S. via Cheltenham
> Bristol 5.38 L.M.S. G.W.R
> Taunton 7.35 G.WR
> Exeter 9.45
> Return Journey.
> Exeter 10.15 pm Sunday.
> Taunton 11.2
> Bristol 1.30
> B'ham 4.35.
> I definitely know there are trains from Birmingham to Cosford within the time limit but I don't know their exact times yet.
> Mummy, have you got "The death of the Moth" Virginia Woolf? I am just reading it. There's a library here with 17 armchairs.
>
> *(p. 18)*
> It's rather funny the Scotch people in my hut laugh at the North country people as they say 'Nut' as in 'look'. And the funny part is that the Scotch themselves are talking so broadly you can hardly understand them.
> I never knew before that Scotch people said their "U"s like we do.

### 03. Monday, c. 31 January 1944 (visiting James at Tirley Garth)

*Undated ("Monday"). His 36-hour pass is now "next weekend" (5–6 February), and he visited James "yesterday" (Sunday).*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Overlapping, top:] are running very late so I might not get in till midnight. If the drawing room door or even the landing windows were left open like Timmy I could hop in
> Good News This Week.
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> G.10. 179 Entry.
> Monday. 4.T.T. Wing.
> Royal Air Force Station
> Cosford, Albrighton
> Nr Wolverhampton
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> Lots and Lots of News and only a little time in which to tell it. First concerning a certain thirty six hours pass. Well of course because I've come here they've cut the pass to 2359 hrs on Sunday night instead of 7.0 am Monday morning.
> So instead of having the Sunday evening with you I have to leave at 4.20 in the afternoon which is a swindle. Theoretically I should arrive back at Cosford before midnight but as a matter of fact in practice my train will probably miss the connection at Birmingham however that doesn't matter as the R.T.O will sign my pass.
> Well now the good part of the news
>
> *(p. 2)*
> You have been imagining that leave was coming in a fortnight. Well that's been changed — It's next weekend.
> Don't wait up for me as though I am due in at 9.45 or something like that, the trains are running very late so I might not get in till midnight. If the drawing room door or even the landing windows were left open like Timmy I could hop in.
> I know first hand that the trains are running late as I didn't go to bed till quarter to one this morning because of a train.
> Well I went visiting yesterday [now I am going to keep you in suspense as to who I visited by putting the name on the next page]. It was a longish journey and very expensive but well worth it. I had to start at 7.5 on Sunday morning in order to see →
>
> *(p. 3)*
> James. Yes I went off up to Tarporley, To the Oxford Groups H.Q.
> I was very much impressed by both house and grounds. I was also pleased to find that the group, at any rate at home, are not in the very least "groupey" as we describe it. It struck me as a rather select country house party with a few very awkward shy but happy tradesmen and workmen amongst them who kept very much in the background. I really did enjoy seeing them and I must say they certainly seem to live on the fat of the land so why James is always thin I don't know. How did he look? The same as he always seemed to look to me Mummy. I looked at him specially as I knew you'd want to know. He has a lot of lines under his eyes which he had when I last saw him and he looks very pale but he says
>
> *(p. 4)*
> [Added at the top:] I am getting so excited about coming home to see you that I can't think of anything but next Sunday.
> he is very ~~good~~ well. Anyway once before when he came home looking exactly as he did look yesterday, I thought how pale you look James and Daddy ~~[struck]~~, you rushed forward and said "James you look very well" so I am not a good judge.
> I had a lovely time but I am purposely not telling you any more so that I can tell you myself when I see you.
> Well I shall really be seeing you at last. I shan't bring much with me, just a tooth brush soap and toothpaste as I believe I have some pyjamas at home.
> Well that's all till Saturday night or Sunday morning. I've got to find out the trains back at Exeter according to the R.T.O here. You'll see a difference in my appearance. I shall be wearing R.A.F glasses which make me look quite different
> with lots of love your loving son [David]

*Notes:* His brother James lived and worked with the Oxford Group (Moral Re-Armament) at its headquarters, Tirley Garth, near Tarporley in Cheshire. The first sheet was scanned with the foot of the second overlapping it.

### 04. Tuesday, c. 8 February 1944 (back from his first 36-hour pass)

*Undated ("Tuesday"), on RAF crested paper. He got back "by the last train from Wolverhampton" after his first pass home via Bristol (5–6 February); "48 hrs on March 4th" is noted at the head and foot as the next leave. This letter was scanned in the Oulton folder.*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] You Must Not let you know who IN. [?]
> [Below:] 48 hrs on March 4th
> [Top right:] Please address Kit's airgraph and post it next time you get a minute to do so. Cosford.
> Tuesday
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> I got back here in time by the last train from Wolverhampton.
> I arrived at Bristol about a quarter of an hour late and not knowing which platform my train left from enquired of my old friends the R.T.O. there being no porters about.
> Never again! must be my motto. There were two or three air force people all enquiring about trains to Cosford. I could scarcely believe my ears as I came in on hearing them told that they would not be able to reach Cosford until the next morning.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> They went out and just to make certain I asked again. They told me the same. The 6.55 for Birmingham would leave No 12 platform but there would be no train leaving for Cosford until morning. I began to experience a slight sinking feeling but determined to ask the railway officials. At the Booking Hall I was stopped by the R.A.F. service police. To my relief it was only to ask whether I was the chap they had told to go to No 14 platform because if so, it wasn't No 14 it was No 12. Poor chaps!
> I patiently explained that I wasn't although it struck me the policeman looked as if he didn't believe me.
> I asked if we were allowed to use the civilian enquiries office and I was [?] we are not.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> He became quite genial and directed me to it, asking me later whether I had found out all I wanted there.
> The railway official was of the opinion that life was one big joke and the R.T.O had been invented to make him laugh. He informed me that I could reach Cosford by 12.35 a time I had never heard of before. I told him about the Exeter Enquiry Office and he looked it up again. This time he got a different result and mentioned the trains I already had in mind. Much relieved I caught the 6.55. It was nearly a near thing at B'ham as the other train was waiting.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> however after everybody in our train had panicked and rushed across the bridge to the other side and got into the train, it waited about ten minutes for the train I would have come on at 4.20. Whether or not this made its connection I don't know but anyway I was glad I had taken the early one as it eliminated a certain amount of nervous strain.
> The train to Wolverhampton was very fast and I changed stations there and caught the next train to Cosford having forty minutes to catch it in.
> Over and over again it was worth coming home. I am so glad I did and thank you very much Daddy for paying for the fare and also for the money.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> All the people in my hut were confident that I should not get the connections and that I should not have any time at home. They thought I was mad to go - but that's because they are all Scots. I ~~[struck]~~ think that the feeling about madness is mutual.
> I keep thinking of it all and how lovely it was and being so pleased that I dare think about coming home again as it is not so far off. I still think it's four weeks Daddy, but I'll find out definitely before I post this letter.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I bet you've noticed this crest isn't in the middle of the page by now Daddy! It's only a Naafi block.
> Mummy, everything was lovely and I am enjoying thinking about the hot water bottle so don't be sorry about that as now I shall always remember that incident and the occasion will stand out in my memory because of it.
> I shall have to cut that hedge again Boss. The soil here is light and strangely, red. James says it new sandstone or something and that Devon is clay on old sandstone.
> I am on magnetos they are awful. I feel twice as good as I did before I went home, as if I'd had a lovely holiday, well I have really!
> with love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] 48 hrs on March 4th

*Notes:* The R.T.O. is the Railway Transport Officer. "They are all Scots" — his hut was full of Scotsmen. "I am on magnetos": his course had moved on to ignition.

### 05. Sunday, c. 13 February 1944 (tea with a pilot officer)

*Undated ("Sunday"); "It's only 12 days to Friday", the start of his 48-hour pass (Friday 25 February).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> G.10. 179 Entry. 4TT Wing
> R.A.F. Cosford.
> Albrighton. W'hpton.
> Sunday:
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> Thank you very much for all your letters. I am glad you are better, Mummy. I had half an idea I might get my forty eight moved forward if you were in an awful muddle, that's why I telephoned but evidently you have the cooking situation in hand chief so I decided it wouldn't be much help if I came. Of course once more James must arrive at a bad moment, poor chap he seems to get done out of his holidays at home.
> I am so glad you are better and do be careful and not lift things or do too much Mummy. ~~[struck]~~
> DON'T HAVE ANY UNWELCOME GUESTS. It would be terrible and you must
>
> *(p. 2)*
> not whatever happens. It would be too much for you and I should go stark staring mad and come home and throw them out.
> If there's anything I can get you in Wolverhampton I'll do it, but it's a rotten shopping place for the kind of things I buy. It's all right for shoes and that sort of thing but all the shops are so poor not like Ross's and Elands, they are like Waltons and the City Cycle Works ("City Cyclery" to you)
> I find that the 'Sunday Times' is worth about a shilling to me every week end. I read all the criticisms of films books music and art and enjoy James Agate and also the articles by Scrutator. They seem to have stopped putting those rather good pen drawings of London in each week.
> They are playing Boccherini's Minuet now
>
> *(p. 3)*
> on the wireless and it makes me feel I should like to be listening to it in the drawing room with you.
> It's a pity the telephone didn't work very well for you Daddy. Your voice was quiet but clear I expect mine would have been if I spoke more clearly.
> I treated myself to "The Lamp Still Burns" just released from London yesterday. You may or may not remember that it was the last film to be directed by Leslie Howard. It was about nursing, not only as a war time career and was very good indeed.
> My best way of describing it is to say that it was a 'quiet, efficient' film like the nurses it portrayed. There were no dramatic scenes, it might almost have been a "documentary" without a story except the life of a hospital nurse, which
>
> *(p. 4)*
> of course was its principal aim.
> Before the film I had tea in the Cinema Café. I sat down at the only place available, a single seat at a four seat table reserved for three. The three had not yet arrived so I planned that if I hurried I might finish before they came.
> The first to arrive was short lady dressed in black. Before long she had engaged me in conversation, informed me that her son and husband would presently arrive from a football match between the R.A.F and the Army (which incidentally seems to be the talk of the camp)
> She told me that he was in the R.A.F. and it appeared that he had done his training at Torquay. From this I realized that he must be aircrew and the horrible idea occurred to me that he might be an Officer!
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Just before he arrived it turned out that he was and so I suggested that perhaps it was incorrect for an AC2. to sit at a table with an officer who ~~[struck]~~ he didn't know when there were (now) lots of empty tables elsewhere.
> She would have none of it and I had to stay. I couldn't get anyone to bring me any tea as they thought I was with her and she had apparently ordered tea for later.
> The two football enthusiasts arrived and I felt hemmed in. He was a pilot officer just commissioned, a bomb aimer.
> I wondered how we should feel if we had reserved a table at Dellers and Kit had come in uniform and we had found a private sitting at the table.
> I tried to think of an escape but was unable to. At first we eyed each other
>
> *(p. 6)*
> rather in the manner that fish and spectators watch each other in an aquarium.
> I couldn't unfreeze until he did. The father and mother were the big hearted Company Floaters type who came from the Midlands.
> The mother as usual ran the conversation and seemed determined to include me in it. I felt the P/O looking about to see if any of his seniors or friends were in the café watching him having tea with me. I may say that if I were an officer I certainly wouldn't sit at a table with an AC2 as indeed as an AC2 I don't sit at a table with another AC2.
> He unfroze and it appeared that he was the same age as me and was going to Birmingham University after the war to study engineering.
> I said that I wouldn't mind being stationed at Torquay as I only lived at Exeter.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> The Mother asked me if I knew Whimple and wondered whether Whiteway was going to feature into the conversation.
> However it was a Mrs Edmonds the wife of the vicar at a place near by that she wanted to know if I knew. I didn't not surprisingly I wonder if you do Daddy?
> I hope these peculiar adventures I have don't bore you as I am afraid I am rather long winded and they may seem quite pointless.
> This morning we had a real fried shell Egg, (stone cold but nevertheless an egg) for breakfast with a large rasher of bacon.
> I was on "Halt Guard" the night before last. This sounds very sinister but it's only the same sort of halt as Lyons Holt Halt.
> It means seeing that people coming into camp don't fall off the platform or break the fences
>
> *(p. 8)*
> as there are such large numbers they are liable to be a bit heavy on the station property.
> It has been announced over the "Tannoy" (if that's how it's spelt), which is a loud speaker like that on Railway Stations that all central heating will stop forthwith in camp.
> That means virtually that all airmen will freeze forthwith and will have only cold water to wash in.
> Did you mean that Wiggs would be at home on my forty eight? It's only 12 days to Friday. I still feel a bit uneasy in case they might make it a week later but can't get much sense out of them. I shall be able to let you know definitely I hope next week end.
> If Wiggs is at home we'll do the breakfast on Saturday and Sunday.
> Fancy all these goings on, pulling
>
> *(p. 9)*
> ceilings down. 70% is a jolly good percentage for a course if I got 70% I should be terrifically good on this course. I can't get on with the practical very well but think I am fairly sound on theoretically.
> A Frenchman asked me the way to the "Aospeetahl" I couldn't think what he meant but when I had found out that it was the hospital I didn't know which one he meant and couldn't think how to ask 'which one?' as he didn't speak English. Finally since I didn't know where the hospitals were, there are several I called in a Corporal. He knew where they were but didn't know which one was wanted. For some reason he wanted me to ask where the Frenchman had come from. After much thought I did so and was delighted to see that he understood but his answer was useless and
>
> *(p. 10)*
> he was plainly puzzled, as I was, to know why I had asked him. He replied that he had been at the cinema which wasn't much help. I left the corporal with him as I had to go on parade.
> I am reading the "Green Mirror" by Hugh Walpole but haven't got very far yet. I've just finished "the Surgeon's Log" which I enjoyed. It's by a man called Abraham I believe we have it as a Penguin.
> Well I've lots to tell you but one can't very well write a book so I suppose I'd better stop.
> I had SIX oranges issued to me, I regret to say I've eaten them all. I do hope you got some.
> Well I am glad you are better Mummy dear. Well Chief it isn't so long now, twelve days.
> Cheerio till next week
> With lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> P.S.
> I had a wonderful conversation completely in French with a Frenchman in the Naafi for about a quarter of an hour. It was most entertaining and he could ~~[struck]~~ understand quite well what I meant & I understood what he meant.
>
> *(p. 12)*
> It started when he asked me what time the billiard room was opened.
> He was quite nice and I should think a different type to his companions as his French was like the French I learnt at school.
> I think the others probably speak with the equivalent accents to our various North and South and cockney dialects.

*Notes:* On lined paper. *The Lamp Still Burns* (1943) was the last film produced by Leslie Howard, who was killed in 1943. Whimple and Whiteway: the Whiteway's cider works are at Whimple, near Exeter. The small slip at the end (written sideways) seems to belong here. "Lyons Holt Halt" is a joke on the camp's railway halt.

### 06. Sunday, c. 20 February 1944 ("133 hours" to leave)

*Undated ("Sunday"). Home on his 48-hour pass "next Friday night" (25 February), "a hundred and thirty three hours" away; Kit is in Italy and "we don't seem to get on with that beach head" (Anzio).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R
> G.10. 179 Entry
> 4.T.T. Wing
> R.A.F Cosford
> Sunday Albrighton Nr W'hampton
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> Thank you very much for your letters. I do hope you are quite better now Mummy dear, and not doing too much. I wonder if you sent those socks, if you did they must be lost in the post as I haven't had them. If you didn't don't worry I am getting on all right.
> So far as anything is certain I shall be seeing you round about 10 o'clock next Friday night. As for last time, don't wait up if I am late, if it looks as if I am going to be very late indeed I can always get something to eat so please don't bother too much about whether I am starving, because I won't be.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> The trains will be slightly different this time but I should get in about the same time, as they are the same from Bristol downwards. I'll write or telephone if anything should come up and they should cancel our leave.
> For the past three days it's tried to snow and I am told that it snowed hard in Bristol on Friday.
> It's now only a hundred and thirty three hours until I see you if I get in at 10 pm.
> Once again my work is very interesting and not too arduous. When I get on well I enjoy it when I get on badly I hate it. I should never make an engineer. I think it must be a terrible job. All your ideas must be cut and dried into bevel gears and pinions. However I feel that this training may be of indirect use to me as in fact most training is.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> On air cooled engines I got 60% percent. The top mark was 70% so I felt encouraged as I believe my carburettors were very weak.
> With the amount of training I've had now I should be able to take down a car engine but of course I don't know anything about the clutch or gears. (I never said I could put it together again!)
> I had some more french practice yesterday. I was just going into the Flight Office to book out of camp. A Frenchman rushed out and said a long stream of unintelligible french to me. How he supposed I could understand I don't know. It turned out when I had got him to speak slowly that he wanted the sergeant. Well the sergeant happens to be away on leave and his substitute, - well I don't know where he hangs out.
> So I had all the business of explaining
>
> *(p. 4)*
> where he was. Unfortunately though the Frenchman could understand what I said I couldn't understand what he said. He would keep speaking quickly and waving a small ticket in front of me with "Collect 1400hrs" on it. I couldn't advise him what do as I didn't know what it was that he was to collect. In the end he seemed satisfied and went away still talking like a machine gun.
> I received a letter from James on Friday. Saying that if I wanted to come over this Sunday he was in bed with "internal upsets". As a matter of fact I hadn't thought of going over this Sunday.
> He evidently enjoyed writing the letter and I must reply to him in the same strain. It occupied 1½ pages with various diagnoses
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I am sorry for him of course, but it struck me as rather funny in parts, as I don't think that he has got any more than a chill.
> He says "I am unlikely to be up on Sunday, as the doctor is anxious that I should go slow. At present (this is typical James) the doctor is not certain that I am actually on the road to recovery. I am not seriously ill, but just at present, unable to get at what is wrong!" — "One doctor thinks it is colitis" —
> How many doctors does he have and isn't colitis the fashionable illness that Axel Munthe told his patients they had when he knew they wanted to be ill but had nothing wrong with them.
> Did any "Dolphin" ever come at Christmas as I didn't ever get it?
> I sit it lovely to think that I shall have all Saturday. I wonder if you have
>
> *(p. 6)*
> any walks or anything in mind for Saturday or Sunday morning.
> I wonder how old Kit's getting on. We don't seem to get on with that beach head very much, do we?
> But just you wait we're going to give them an awful knock soon, this is just one of those intervals we have.
> Everybody here always goes on his forty eight, nobody ever saves them in case they lose them. I am sitting in the little common room of "G" lines huts writing this at a table with three "Chauvelins" at least that's how they look, like Raymond Massey in the Scarlet Pimpernel. There's a chance I may yet see Pimpernel Smith it will be due at this station soon I should think. We only get very very old films and as far as I know it hasn't been yet.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> This is quite nice paper, isn't it, for war time I mean. It's from a YMCA. The YMCA is much better than Naafi. NAAFI are rotten compared to any other forces canteen. The Americans have lovely places in America for their forces, I wish we did as you get a bit sick of drinking horrible tea and eating stale buns. The tea is really horrible much worse than the tea the (RAF) station supplies. It tastes terribly of chlorine.
> I am glad I am doing engines not airframes they really are very interesting. It won't be so good when I stop learning and have to put it into practice though.
> I never got Phil a birthday present, I wrote however. It's so very difficult to get anything. Wolverhampton really has rottener shops than Exeter.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> Birmingham is a shopping paradise.
> By the way you could easily get a pump for that shelter if you could get a second hand electric mains motor. All you need is two spur gears a case and some piping and driving wheel of some sort. [Drawing: two meshed spur gears, A and B, inside a close-fitting oval case, with pipes in and out top and bottom and arrows showing the water carried round the outside of the gears.] The gears are meshed together in a metal container which fits fairly closely, to either side of the container is connected a pipe, take an axle through one of the gears to a driving wheel of some sort driven by the motor. The two gears revolve, in doing so they create a decrease in pressure which draws the water up one pipe into the case round the outside of the gears and out the other end. Perhaps you knew that already.
> Well goodbye till - Friday Night.
> with love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] 9.15 now 132 hrs.
>
> *(p. 9)*
> STOP PRESS
> I am not coming home at 10 pm any more. I am coming in on a much earlier train with any luck.
> I am sharing a taxi with four others to take us at 12.15, when we are let out of the workshop, at full speed to Wolverhampton to catch the next train (about 12.35) to Birmingham to catch the 1.30 express to the South. It will take me direct to Exeter.
> It should get in about six I think. But this is all in the air still. I haven't checked any of their times or connections. If it's all right I'll send you a postcard with all the details to reach you about Thursday. If it isn't, the ~~time I'll~~ times I've already given you still hold good ie arriving at 10 pm. ~~and~~ I'll let you know either way.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> The international ballet is coming to the theatre at Wolverhampton. It will play Les Sylphides, Endymion, Twelfth Night ballet and all sorts of other things.

### 07. Tuesday, c. 29 February – Saturday 4 March 1944 (late back from leave)

*Undated ("Tuesday", continued "Saturday"). Back late from his 48-hour pass because of the snow ("the same reason that Auntie Muriel can't move from her house"); he posts Lynette's birthday sweets on the Saturday for her birthday on the 5th.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> G.10.179 Entry 4 Wing RAF. Cosford
> Albrighton Nr W'hampton
> Tuesday.
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> I meant to write this yesterday but had rather a long day of it or a short night and so was glad to get to bed as soon as I could.
> I didn't get in until after three for the same reason that Auntie Muriel can't move from her house at present.
> The first two stages were done in company with Whistler in corner seats in comfort.
> I slept a certain amount between Exeter and Bristol which was lucky as things turned out. I reached Birmingham at about 10.35. My train was due out for Wolverhampton at 10.15. unfortunately. Old Whistler was a good travelling companion
>
> *[One or more pages missing here.]*
>
> *(p. 2)*
> In fact I have only a page or two more to read. I was very much interested, Daddy, and so glad that you offered it to me. It touches on the some of the same characters as there were in a biography you had Mummy about a friend of Keats who was an artist. What was his name? The one whose father got into a fury and nearly killed him.
> Auntie Muriel wrote to me offering me some hard boiled eggs, wasn't it kind of her. I am going to write back to her and thank her but decline! Besides being an awful nuisance to her they wouldn't be very handy to take a bite out of at an odd moment and might arouse alarm or despondency in my companions if displayed in public.
> I always write my letters without a fixed plan of campaign and this one is no exception so here we go back again to the train journey.
> Arrived at Birmingham you may remember
>
> *(p. 3)*
> [Overlapping, top:] … platform then and I thought it was time he stopped inventing these trains for his amusement. So I told him that I couldn't help it but the Railway People couldn't possibly be all three wrong …
> [Main sheet:]
> that I had just lost the W'hpton Train.
> The Station was crowded, more so perhaps than in daytime, the larger part of the crowd being forces. I hurried off to the Rotten Transport Office to get my pass signed, as the 10.15 had been the last train scheduled to reach here before midnight. Here was I, in a hole and feeling very civilian and not going to be sat on sort of feeling. So I said I wanted my pass signed and an American Corporal whom I later took a dislike signed it, to say that the next train was at 10.35 and told me it was in No 1 Bay.
> Now I knew A. that it was after 10.35 now and B that there wasn't another train till 11.10 anyway. So I went up to three railway officials in turn after practically having to send out a search party to find them and had the words from their lips that there wasn't a train until 11.10
>
> *[One or more pages missing here.]*
>
> *(p. 4)*
> are so obvious that any spy reading this could guess them we had to wait for half an hour or so.
> We reached Wolves about quarter to one, an hour late. I got the R.T.O. to sign my pass again and inquired about the next train fully expecting it to be the 4.10. However Fortune was good to me the 12.37 had not yet left and was even now waiting for the London Train. I thought this called for a certain amount of hurry on my part but was calmly told by the ticket collector that there wasn't any hurry as we shouldn't be leaving for another hour or so.
> Then my words of only a few hours previously were proved. Looking round the station I saw the welcome sign board of the Y.M.C.A.
> It's jolly good, I don't believe there's another organization to beat it. There it was open and warm. Every where were sleeping airmen and soldiers.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> There were three voluntary workers at the counter dishing out tea and coffee, ginger beer and sandwiches cakes and toast. (This sounds a bit like a sort of recommendation of a hotel).
> I had already consumed the vast supplies of sandwiches etc which you made me Mummy, very good they were too. However this had been over six hours before so I fell too on two bottles of ginger beer and a sandwich.
> This done I made my way between the snoring forms out to the platform and into the train. I had the misfortune to go into a compartment were there were four unfortunates who had been to Manchester without passes and had just been picked up by S.P's. who were it appeared now on the train.
> This was a new one on me S.P's on the train and the pass in my pocket seemed to be burning with the words 2359.
>
> *(p. 6)*

> with love

> your loving son David

>
> *(p. 4)*
> 4.

>
> *(p. 5)*

> It's a lovely day with great big bumpy clouds and a very blue sky in between.

> But how much did you want to pay for it and do you still want it. This one costs 45/- but looks worth it.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> 6.
> There is one snag. It could be eliminated. If you could fix ~~[struck]~~ it on a wooden post it would be all right. I wonder about the post on the back of the blast wall. It is hand operated like a village pump and looks roughly like this
> [Drawing: a rotary pump about 4" across on a post, with a two-armed handle swung up and down, labelled "handle", and an arrow to "Lever on screw or with".]
> I could do one of two things if you do want it, send it by rail or bring it myself ~~by~~ on my 36 hrs.
> It would be absolutely no trouble to do if you do want it. It has hose pipe on the inlet but not on the outlet.
> The only thing is the price. Would you want to pay forty five bob.

### 09. Monday, c. 27 March 1944 (James again, and the ballet)

*Undated ("Monday"). He visited James at Tirley Garth "yesterday" and saw the International Ballet at Wolverhampton on the Saturday; "a faint possibility" of a 36-hour pass "next Saturday" (1 April).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R. G.10. 179 Entry
> 4 Wing
> ROYAL AIR FORCE STATION, COSFORD, WOLVERHAMPTON
> Monday.
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> Thank you very much indeed for the letters and the lovely 'hankies'. They are the nicest I have ever seen, particularly the very blue one.
> I feel as if I can hear you making a joke Daddy like "that's all right my boy I chose them myself" or something like that as you probably don't know I ever had any sent me.
> I am feeling very optimistic about the war and in fact everything in general. I suspect the reason to be that lots of nice things have been happening lately.
> I went to see James yesterday!
>
> *(p. 2)*

>
> *(p. 3)*

Last time I didn't get in till about quarter to one as far as I remember. This time it worked out much better and I was in my hut making my bed by nine twenty five.

>
> *(p. 4)*
> That would have been all right but as I did it by mistake I didn't "set" my brain to wake me up at the right time. I woke up and thought I was in bed and I couldn't wake up properly then realized I was in a train and couldn't think why. Then realized why and thought I was at Stafford. Enquired of my next door neighbour and found to my horror it was Wolverhampton. Couldn't believe it as it seemed only five minutes before that I had been staring at the americans.

>
> *(p. 5)*

> I got the train which of course had to be late as I'd run all the way there to be on time.
> The revue at Tirley Garth really was quite good, it made me laugh but whether at relief at having something funny to laugh at or what I don't know.
> How do you like this paper? Somebody is playing "the man who broke the bank at Monte Carlo" very loud in the next room.
>
> *[One or more pages missing here.]*
>
> *(p. 6)*

The dancing was not as good as that of either the Anglo Polish or the Jooss Ballets.
> Everyman was almost more dramatic acting than dancing in parts though it should have been dancing. I hope to enclose the programme.

I felt that Priestley wrote a very good description of the stage in "The Good Companions" There they all were like people from a foreign country with suitcases and dyed hair standing in groups the men with baggy trousers and cigarettes hanging in special attitudes, and the women talking furiously as if afraid the world would end were they to stop.

>
> *(p. 8)*

>

> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David

### 10. Easter Day, Sunday 9 April 1944

*Headed "Easter Day" (9 April 1944), "only a week since I was at home" (the 36-hour pass of 1–2 April).*

> *(p. 1)*
> Easter Day.
> Dear Mummy + Daddy
> Thank you very much for the letter & the money. It's only a week since I was at home yet it seems an eternity. Monday and Tuesday dragged by, but I had a rotten night on Tuesday and woke up on Wednesday feeling wretched. As I had to see the C.O. at lunch-time about an application I'd sent in to get off this course, I went on with my work. Lunch time came and I went to see him feeling quite unequal to an interview or anything at all but bed.
> He was unsatisfactory, but the trouble is, you can't do anything about it if that's how he chooses to be.
> He said that I must go on to
>
> *(p. 2)*
> the end of the course when if as I said my lack of practical ability was obviously going to be a drawback he would see what he would do for me about getting me into another trade. I might have been quite pleased with this answer when I first came into the Air force, but I've seen too much of this now. When I come to the end of my course if I pass I shall go as Flight Mech to a station, If I fail I shall be automatically made maintenance assistance (one lower than flight mech, a sort of petrol pump man who oils the external parts of aircraft and re fuels). Anyway, I came out of his office determined to go on a Special sick report. I went to the Wing Office and Medical Inspection room and finally saw the MO. I had a temperature of 101.2
> I went straight back to my hut to bed where I stayed during Wednesday afternoon and the whole of Thursday.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> It seemed to me extraordinary that on the Thursday, the second day, I had to report sick again at 6.45. This seems a stupid system - I was marched about and waited 2½ hrs. This time I had a temperature of 99.4. As I've said I spent the whole of Thursday in bed and once more reported sick on Friday morning. This time I was normal and was astonished to find that I was on full duty again immediately with a gargle three times a day. Organized games were due for the afternoon in which I might have to run seven miles. Well I felt as if I had no go left in me whatever and when organized games came I told the ~~[struck]~~ sergeant in charge that I had been in bed with 101.2 and that I didn't feel
>
> *(p. 4)*
> that I could go rushing about. So, he being, thank goodness, quite human said "all right report to the NCO i/c your flight and he'll probably give you a job."
> This I did but the N.C.O being out and the sergeant obviously knowing this I was able to clear off and rest on my bed. Yesterday, Saturday, I got hotter and hotter as if I had caught the sun but when my temperature was taken at the M.I. room when I went for gargle they said it was all right.
> Well this is a nice letter I must say. I don't seem to have much to interest me, as I don't want to have any more to do with engines, as I feel now, and I have nothing to do.
> As I've been in bed most of the time or off colour I haven't any news really.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> As I feared, the worst has happened about my approaching holiday. That will be sufficient for you to know what I mean but still there is a slight hope. It's lucky that I saw you when I did. They told me when I was in bed with that temperature and it made me feel worse. I hope this won't prove as cryptic as Auntie Muriel's snow.
> As regards your buzzer Daddy, I went to Wolverhampton yesterday for an hour to look round the shops for one. Most of the shops were shut but there was one which looked very likely to have of in.
> Somebody here who has been in the manufacturing side of electrical parts all his life says that the thing
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I ought to look out for is a G.E.C. transformer buzzer, which would not be expensive but which is rarely sold as it is more profitable to sell a number of parts separately, than a self contained unit. He says too that you can run a 230 buzzer off a 210 v circuit. What you think about all this I don't know, but if I can get one at all I shall get one at 210 as that's our voltage isn't it?
> I went to Communion this morning and wondered whether you were there or not Daddy as well.
> I haven't had the 'strength' to go to look for Boscobel or whatever it is yet. Well it's a bit late to wish a Happy Easter but I do.
> I'd like a turn round the garden just after it had stopped raining and the sun was out.
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* "The worst has happened about my approaching holiday" — leave was being stopped before the invasion. Boscobel House, where Charles II hid in the oak, is near Cosford.

### 11. Thursday 13 April 1944 (meteorology refused)

*Dated "Thursday 13/4/44".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1,
> 1587757 AC2 Silver. D.R.

> 4 Wing. Cosford.

> Dear Daddy & Mummy

> 8.
> with my work I suppose. The trouble is that I don't want to be indispensable as a ~~[struck]~~ Flight Mechanic or they'll never let me get out of it.
> It's a rotten job - the rottenest in the air force. If only I was used to handling tools and that kind of thing it would be better but I am absolutely useless.
> However don't let it depress you as it doesn't me.
> As regards your buzzer Daddy, the chap who told me that I ought to get a G.E.C. Mains Buzzer, made enquiries at his old ~~factory~~ works where he used to work. He wanted to talk to the manager anyway, so he asked him over the 'phone whether they had any. The manager said they hadn't any but he would try to get one. He rang him up later and ~~[struck]~~ the manager said that there are no mains buzzers made now for civilian use, that he had none and knew of nowhere where one could be bought -
>
> *(p. 10)*
> 9.
> He said that with luck a shop with a large stock might by chance have one. I couldn't go to Wolverhampton on Saturday (that's yesterday, I am sorry I keep putting this letter down and picking it up and writing some more). I was on Bees Duty. But I will try next Saturday at the shops I have noted down.
> I am sorry I don't seem to be very successful. I am having, Mist. Ferri. Arsenic. which I gather must be a tonic of iron + arsenic. The dose has been the same every time and the first day I had it, I may have imagined it, but I felt a bit funny.
> Mummy, Tong Church is the one where "Little Nell" and her grandfather (?) rested in the porch, when Dickens was looking for them. I've got a guide book. That's all for now
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David
> R.T.O.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> P.S.
> I think that the second front will start as you say Daddy in May I put it at between the 30th April and the 15th of May. So bang goes my leave.
> ~~Well~~ We got the afternoon off on Easter Monday and I went and painted a picture of Albrighton Church. It was not good but it's a good subject to paint and I'll have another go at it if I get the chance.
> I went to see "Anthony Adverse" at the Station Cinema last week. ~~Isn't~~ Isn't it a novel, I seem to know the name. It was about 2¼ hrs long. It was quite good, you'd have liked it Daddy.
> My work is getting very complicated so that I get my brain tied in knots.

*Notes:* His application to remuster as a meteorologist is turned down, and the Wing Commanding Officer tells him he is too young for a commission and needs "to LEARN TACT". Kit had been wounded or injured in Italy ("laid-up for a long time"). Tong Church, with its Old Curiosity Shop associations, is near Cosford.

### 12. Sunday 30 April 1944 (before the final board)

*Dated "Sunday 30th April".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> G.10. 179 Entry..
> 4 Wing.
> R.A.F Cosford
> Nr W'hampton.
> Sunday 30th April.
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> How lovely it would be if they could fly Kit home. Aeroplanes of the type he would come in, average about 250 m.p.h. so if you look at the back of that red paper covered war atlas where the distances are marked to all parts of the world on a map you can see how long it would take.
> The range of an aircraft of that type might be anything from 1900 - 3000 miles (without "refuelling") according to the sort used.
> I know it's only just a chance that he might be able to come and that he may not have meant that at all but it is nice to half imagine it's possible.
> I should say you were up last night or rather this morning judging by the
>
> *(p. 2)*
> wireless news. They got three down so there must have been a lot of banging I should think. Well they won't be able to do it for very much longer.
> I think we are going to squash them by August and the war with Germany will be finished by next February. They are going to be so knocked about by our offensive they'll have to give in.
> Next Thursday I go on the Final Board. It's a viva voce lasting anything from about half an hour to four hours. I shall be under a hail of questions all the time. After that I await posting. The RAF ban has not been lifted and I am afraid will not be, but there are ways and means of getting a day or so if I am in the right part of the country. I bet you they either pop me off by boat or up to Scotland -
> I went to "The "NELSON TOUCH"
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2
> James Agate recommended it as a good film. He's been wrong once or twice in my opinion but generally he's fairly good. It was about the Canadian Corvettes escorting the convoys across the Atlantic. It made me feel that only the acted hollywood war films are depressing as it was a fairly accurate almost documentary film. It was very good and I enjoyed it.
> As a rule I am fed up with war films however. "Arsenic and Old Lace" is on at Birmingham but not with its original cast. I can get a day pass 20 miles which would take me to Birmingham but it's such a nuisance that you have to book for things like that weeks in advance
> It's a most beautiful day and I would go out for a walk if you were here to go with, Daddy, but as that's impossible I don't know what I shall do.
> I wonder if a person can be fully trained as an for two jobs or whether his brain won't hold that amount as by the time I've finished with the RAF I shall have quite a fair knowledge
>
> *(p. 4)*
> of Aeronautical Practical Engineering. I wish I wanted to do it after the war as it would be ~~so~~ convenient.
> If as time goes on I find that there is no hope of being ~~able~~ anything but a flight mechanic which I think may happen, I shall work like mad at that and try and get on a fitter II course after which all being well I shall try for a fitter I's course that will be as far as I can get without a degree in physics chemistry and maths.
> The northern people in this hut mostly seem to think that Devon is somewhere near London.
> I am glad Wiggs has got leave and I should like to call to her attention to an advertisement in to-day's Sunday Times Page 4 column I the fourth advertisement down. "Mary Poppins Opens the Door" I felt like buying it myself!
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3
> ~~The flors~~ ← (that's the effect of Scotch accents)
> The flowers outside my hut must be ordinary wall flowers as they have a strong and pleasant scent. They are yellow, Crimson lake, and vermillion.
> I have had a smashing hot bath this morning and consequently that and the hot weather make me very pleased with life. We had fried eggs and bacon (real eggs) for the third Sunday in succession for breakfast, What has come over the cookhouse?
> Every Sunday evening, surrounded by vast Tomes written on Aeronautical Engineering and empty cups and stale buns I sit in a corner of the Naafi and survey the scene of the 4 Wing Naafi Dance. This consists of a large space cleared on the linoleum floor (which isn't very slippery). to the accompaniment of an accordeon piano, saxophone and drums
>
> *(p. 6)*
> people perform the most astonishing gymnastic feats which are known as dancing. I have never taken part in this, since I feel that the dancing taught me by Miss Coleridge would appear rather staid and solemn in comparison to that of the other members of this Jamboree.
> I never get a minute to do any more painting somehow as it takes so long to walk to a secluded place to paint that by the time I get there it's time to start back.
> Hatchard's of which I spoke, in Wolverhampton, is a bookshop by appointment to the King. It is magnificent with vast books of pictures, Bonington, Sickert, Velasquez, all mixed up. I select them for my library of the future and spend a happy afternoon looking through them unmolested.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> You know the National Park scheme put forward by Sir Noel Curtis Bennett, ~~[struck]~~ Daddy. Well I thought it a bit queer to have playing fields and hostels and I see other people writing in the paper to day think so too.
> There are lots of books on post war architecture but having a lot of engineering and machines buzzing round in my brain I can't think straight any more.
> This letter is gradually getting deteriorating. Presumably it was James birthday yesterday anyway I wrote to him and wished him many happy returns so I hope it was. How old is he, 34?
> I can't think what he would like. I never got ~~one~~ a present for Phil either Poor Kit I've not written to him now as I don't [know] where to send it, while he's shifting about.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> 4
> I should like to come home for a spot of leave but it gets further away instead of nearer.
> I was glad to hear that you'd been out for a walk the other day Mummy I should like to take you out in the car up to Woodbury Common.
> I can imagine myself sitting down in the driving seat and going through a cockpit check to see that the under carriage was down etc and then looking for the boost gauges on the dashboard, and warming up prior to taking off.
> I may get a little bit of flying later.
> Here's a joke I saw in the Tatler. by Anton the man who draws eyebrows like this [tiny sketch]. There's a theatre with a poster outside with "The Great Julietta and her performing dogs" on it. There's a man of the above type standing outside the stage door with a large bouquet and behind him in the same attitude are two dogs with bones in their mouths.
> With love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] It doesn't seem very funny without the picture.

*Notes:* *The Nelson Touch* is the American title of *Corvette K-225* (1943). James Agate was the *Sunday Times* drama critic. The National Parks scheme was being debated. Anton was the *Tatler* cartoonist.

### 13. Sunday, c. 7 May 1944 (after the final board)

*Undated ("Sunday"); the Thursday after the 30 April letter he sat his final trade board, and he now awaits posting. (He was at Little Horwood by late May.)*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver
> J9 179 Entry
> 4 Wing
> Cosford etc.
> Sunday.
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> As you see I've moved, the huts we were in are being ~~[struck]~~ used for either wounded or the officer cadets according to two rumours circulating.
> Anyway we were shoved out at ½ an hours notice and I am now about three hundred yards from where I was. So if you write again before I move which may be some time towards the end of this week, I am in J.9. 179 Entry the rest as before.
> Thank you very much for your letters. I seem to be in a muddle about Wiggs leave as I thought she was home for seven days when she was only home for the day, the other day.
> That's why I pointed out that advertisement in the S. Times.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> 2
> Well I suppose you are waiting to hear how I got on on the board.
> ~~Well~~ I don't know how badly I did yet, but I do know that I passed the two main sections.
> We did it in three ~~main~~ sections of which I had better not mention any details, suffice it to say, that two were semi practical and one theoretical. I was very nervous on one section at least I wasn't nervous, but due to being keyed up waiting for it my mind was a blank and I couldn't answer a lot of questions. On the other section the one I least expected to do well at I had an examiner whom I immediately liked and I answered practically all the questions as far as I know correctly.
> They have cut out the rank of L.A.C. for flight mechs passing well on the course, so you are either AC.2. or AC.1. (the difference in pay is 9d). I shan't be AC1 in fact I shall be lucky to get through as I
>
> *(p. 3)*
> made such a muck up. On one subject I only had 3 questions and I couldn't answer any of them although ten minutes after I could easily have done so.
> The time passes very quickly and the time has been cut down so the figures I gave you last week are wrong. About the minimum on one subject is 10 mins and the maximum 45 mins at the outside and you have 3 goes altogether
> I was about a quarter of an hour on my worst and about 35 minutes on my best. The length of time on the other depends on certain conditions outside your ability. I did most of it on Thursday but some yesterday afternoon.
> I shall be on fatigues now till I leave here. Saturday morning I spent sweeping out No 1 Sgts Mess, cleaning knives, and removing the salvage to the salvage centre on a barrow.
> Alexandra Rose day got a hundred and something pounds I think it was 116
>
> *(p. 4)*
> on this camp.
> We have the Tannoy system of loud speakers here inside and out of doors and often there's a crackling sound and a voice says "This is the Station Commander" and then follows a message from Air Commodore Allen.
> Other air force stations are getting leave but our group aren't. I do hope I come South when I move I hope it isn't fourteen days ~~too~~ as you remember what that means too.
> I suspect that they tried to see whether I was trying to fail the board and I was a marked man due to asking to re-muster as they asked me all sorts of queer questions on and off after the board.
> I think it was touch and go whether I got through and I really don't know now, whether I did.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I think the second front will open within the next fortnight, you remember I said either April 7th or May 7th.
> Don't be surprised to receive peculiar parcels addressed to me as I am going to send some of my books home and painting things.
> On the ones marked "please open" please follow the instructions as they will contain empty tins I expect. On the unmarked ones please leave them as I might ask you to send them on to my next station again.
> I can't carry them with me as it makes such a lot of kit.
> I shall get most peculiar hours from now on I shall be lucky if I get a day off in a week, very lucky, and it may be any day. I may be working all ~~day~~ night or anything for which I shall earn the princely sum of 4/6 a day if AC2
>
> *(p. 6)*
> that's 1/6 more than I get now.
> I shall know what's happening and how I got on next Tuesday.
> I'll not be writing again until next weekend I expect so you may get my next letter from my next station or you may get it from here as some people don't get posted for ages on some courses at some stations. Very security minded this week!
> It certainly would be lovely if Christopher could come home and I'd try to make them give me some leave.
> I hope nobody takes it into their heads to send me to the outer Hebrides, or the North pole or anywhere miles away.
> How good you are getting at recognition Daddy, recognising Dakotas!
>
> *(p. 7)*
> My saving campaign is very slow and laborious but I may do better on 4/6 a day. I am still going to try to get onto a Met course but my plan has altered a little.
> The Air Force have War Office spare time courses for post war ~~planning~~ occupations. They are designed very carefully for servicemen and you have a proper tutor and they send your course by post. Meteorology is one of the jobs and I think that having done a War Office course on it (free) they might pay attention to me. It is awful they keep asking for volunteers for Meteorology and they won't let me go. Did you see the very nice photograph by Dixon Scott, of Exeter Cathedral in the S. Times and the little paragraph beside it. Very nearly your view Daddy if he moved a hundred yards to the left.
> Well that's all for now
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* He passes out as a Flight Mechanic (Engines), AC2. The Alexandra Rose Day collection, the Tannoy and the second front ("within the next fortnight") all feature. He plans a War Office correspondence course in meteorology.
