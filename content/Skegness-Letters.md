*← [Silver Family Archive](./)*

In October 1943 [David Silver](David-Silver), nineteen, arrived at **No. 11 Recruit Centre, RAF Skegness**, as **AC2 Silver**, for eight weeks of basic training in a seaside town whose hotels and boarding houses had become billets. These are the earliest of his RAF letters home to his parents at **Tresillian** in Exeter.

He is up at 5.25 and drilled until he is "one big ache all over". He polishes the back of his cap badge twice a day and scrubs floors "with a scrubbing brush with no bristles no hot water no soap and a leaky bucket". He throws a live grenade, fires a Sten gun, and has to crawl across a rope over the ornamental lake. A fever puts him in hospital, and for weeks he is moved "from pillar to post", back to the start of his training and from flight to flight. He finds almost no one to talk to: "I met somebody the other day who spoke and was literate, it was quite a treat." The bright spot is his brother Philip, a doctor holding a clinic in the town every Thursday, with fried plaice and "two milds please".

His Christmas letters have pen drawings of the Lincolnshire fens, and on **2 January 1944** he writes: "Well to-day I am twenty. It seems absolutely incredible to me, I don't feel twenty, I feel as if I stopped growing up when I left Bruton and am stuck at 18½."

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. Few of the letters are dated, so most are dated from what he says and from postmarked envelopes; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it. His later RAF letters are from [Cosford](Cosford-Letters), [Little Horwood](Little-Horwood-Letters), [Oulton](Oulton-Letters), [Oakington](Oakington-Letters), [Henlow](Henlow-Letters) and [Wellesbourne Mountford](Wellesbourne-Letters).

## 1943

### 01. Thursday 14 October 1943 (just arrived)

*Dated "Thursday 14 October" (a Thursday in 1943). Written in bed on his first night, after travelling from 2.50 a.m.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC.2. Silver D.R.
> No 2 Section
> 26 Flight
> "F" Squadron
> No 3 Wing
> 11 R.C.
> Skegness.
> Thursday 14 October.
> Dear Daddy and Mummy.
> I've arrived here as you see and this address will be good for the next eight weeks while I do my "square bashing". It is ten past ten at night and I am writing this in bed just before lights out. I have been up since 2.50 am this morning and am pretty well all in having carried full kit for about a mile and a half in all and travelled for ten hours by train.
> Life here is going to be very tough indeed so I must get to sleep quickly now as I have to be able to wake myself in time to be on parade at 6.10 am ~~[struck]~~ for inspection to-morrow
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I never knew the R.AF had such a tough time. Please could you let me know if the ATC certificate is coming to the address I gave you and also whether you got my parcels of clothes.
> I am in a converted private house in a room with four others (carefully selected by me) we have a basin with cold water which is a great luxury and a few nails as coat hangers. Drill is maintained even when you are exhausted at a very high standard. However I am still alive and kicking.
> The course starts to-morrow!
> This is all for now, I am not far from Phil with love your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* His first letter from Skegness: "square bashing" for eight weeks. He had come from a reception centre (later mentioned as Cardington, near Bedford).

### 03. Sunday 17 October 1943

*Headed "Sunday"; inoculated "yesterday" (Saturday 16 October). He asks his parents not to say where he is because of raids on people under training.*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] The Brierley must be back by now, don't let her interrupt your rests Mummy.
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> No 2 Section
> 26 Flight
> "F" Squadron
> No 3 Wing.
> 11. R.C.
> Skegness.
> Lincs
> Sunday
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> At last I can settle down and write you a proper letter.
> First of all though will you please let no one know where I am exactly or what I am doing.
> You can say I am training for the ground staff in the North East. But you mustn't say where as there have been raids here, on people under training, due probably to knowledge gained by the germans about the place.
> Of course it is impossible to keep it secret in a letter with the name of the place and R.C. (recruiting centre) as an address.
> Please tell ~~[struck]~~ no one where I went to before as the Germans don't know what it is being used for.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I'll tell you what I am doing each week when I write as P.T. and drill isn't secret and I shan't have anything to do with my ground job for a while.
> We are billeted in a red brick house in a road of similar houses. It corresponds to those new houses on the right as you go up Pennsylvania hill before you get to the nice white houses (one on either side, an architect or a lawyer or someone lives in the one on the left I think)
> It has three storeys and I am on the top floor in a room about the size of the railway room with three other people. As you can imagine there is very little space. The thing which perhaps is most irritating of all is the lack of anywhere to put your things except on or under your bed and the fact there is nowhere to sit. There is a basin in the room thank goodness.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2
> There is no hot water and no heating unfortunately. The next eight weeks will probably be the most strenuous in my life. The first part is for foot drill and rifle drill etc. Then we get a commando course (like we see these Yanks doing) then we get field exercises and all sorts of army stuff, it is terribly tough, and you can't report sick unless you are unable to stand up or some equally good reason (I don't believe this at all but the N.C.Os say it is so) We were innoculated and vaccinated yesterday. The vaccination won't hurt for ten days but the innoculation made me feel rotten. I couldn't move my arm much without great pain and it swelled rather. To-day however it is better. Everybody here has an awful cold but I think I came out top with a chesty sort of cough as well.
> I took two aspirins last night which
>
> *(p. 4)*
> [eased] my arm I think.
> [W]e get two more lots altogether the [last] of which I believe is a much larger [dose] although it doesn't affect you much more. Several people fainted and I had to hold a chap up in front of me in the queue as we were innoculated.
> One boy went to the hairdresser later in the day and suddenly fainted quite unexpectedly and fell on his nose, they had to throw water on him.
> But don't let this worry you about me as I didn't feel in the least faint and my arm has not been half as painful as most people's.
> I wish I had my paints here but if I had there would be nowhere to keep them and no time to do it in. To day is the first spare
>
> *(p. 5)*
> moment I've had.
> We are allowed to miss breakfast on Sundays if we like the first two Sundays. I missed it to day. I was able to sleep till 10.30 am without stirring, that shows you how tired I was. We ~~don't~~ didn't have to get up till 11.0 am today if we missed breakfast. Later we shall be doing field exercises and church parades so this luxury will not be repeated.
> You can't imagine what a luxury it was to be able to sleep as long as you liked. Normally we get up at about five twenty five and go to bed at about 10.5.
> We work the whole of the time between. We are supposed to be off duty and able to go out from 6.0 pm till 9.0
>
> *(p. 6)*
> 3.
> But as a matter fact as you have to have all your equipment spotless for kit inspection at 6.40 am and you have to get dressed and have breakfast between 5.30 and 6.40 you don't get any spare time at all.
> We have to keep our room spotless with a scrubbing brush with no bristles no hot water no soap and a leaky bucket. This afternoon I have been scrubbing hard for an hour and a half.
> Don't let this depress you, it is quite possible to do it all though it is very tiring. I can almost see my face in my boots, when I reach that state they are passable.
> This place isn't a bad little place at all really, I had a bit of time yesterday to go round and buy things so I saw the town. ~~[struck]~~
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4
> Out of our window there are back gardens full of cabbages allotments and fields. In the distance are trees but as there are no hills at all the horizon is only about a quarter of a mile away.
> It's lovely now with great white bumpy clouds, though it's been raining all day.
> Fatigues will start shortly for me. There is one fatigue I hope I don't get. That is being runner for an officer all night. It would mean a full day's training a night of carrying messages and another full day's training before going to bed.
> . . . . .
> 8 pm later on Sunday.
> Having done my scrubbing I have only to sweep the hall floor, clean my buttons polish my boots + make up my bed So now I am more or less free and have been since about six.
> There is a boy here in my room
>
> *(p. 8)*
> who persists in accompanying me every time I go out which as the Raf would say is a bit of a bind (bore). He isn't very intelligent but means well enough. Nobody ever goes out alone here for some unknown reason. Often they go out in fives and sixes.
> I am sitting in the Y.M.C.A. rest room which adjoins the canteen. It is in the main street. I have just been to the evening service at St Matthews which so far as I can make out is the only C of E Church here. It was a very enjoyable service and put me in mind of the times we've been to church in country churches round Exeter. {I feel that sentence sounds as if Hawthes had written it.}
> This room is not a large room, it is filled with small writing tables basket chairs and there is a
>
> *(p. 9)*
> 5
> piece of carpet on the floor. Round about me are sitting soldiers, sailors, and airmen, in various stages of repose, leaning across the tables half asleep, writing letters, asleep and so on.
> It is very interesting to me that these canteens are lit by neon tubes, at least that is what they appear to be like this [Drawing: two fluorescent tubes hanging from a ceiling flex, glowing.]
> They give quite a good light. For some reason the back of my hand is very painful indeed at the moment as if it were bruised. I think it must be the innoculation.
> I have just had to treat my companion, the same watchdog I have already referred to, to a cup of tea a sandwich and a piece of cake.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> This is a swindle but it had to be done. He had only the price of a cup of tea and I wasn't going to have one cup of tea instead of supper so I had to get us both a piece of cake and a sandwich as well.
> Funds are not running out yet but I shall be glad when they give us some pay as so far they have only given us 10/- to replace some equipment they couldn't issue and which will later be subtracted from our pay.
> I shall be very glad when I get some leave but it doesn't look as if I shall get any for a good many months yet.
> I have sent my suitcase by rail so I should think you ought
>
> *(p. 11)*
> 6
> to have received it by now together with a very peculiar letter.
> You should also have received first a parcel containing my raincoat and second a registered parcel containing my washing. If any of these have not arrived could please let me know at the first possible opportunity.
> There is no law allowing airmen to wear civilian shoes in the evening, the old regulation about wearing boots is still in force, but nobody seems to pay any attention to it here.
> The sea front here is quite long with a pier and some hideous iron switchbacks.
> Looking out to sea, we seem to be at the top end of a great horseshoe of land, I wonder if it's the Wash. I haven't seen a map of this place yet.
>
> *(p. 12)*
> I wonder how those tomatoes got on, ripening in my old bedroom. I hope that they didn't keep on developing the disease.
> I wonder about little Auntie Grouser and Martie and so on.
> Any news of Christopher? I should be very glad if you could let me have all the family's addresses or else re-direct on my letters.
> I hope the Aeroplane Spotter are being delivered by Wheatons for me one a fortnight each Saturday.
> I should like something to read but have little time to read and not much room to keep a book.
> Well that's about all I can manage for to-night
> With love
> your loving son
> David.
> Are you making the coffee still? Daddy.

*Notes:* He numbers the sheets 1–6; scan 0 65 is the back of sheet 2. The red-brick billet is compared with the houses on Pennsylvania Hill, Exeter. St Matthew's is Skegness parish church.

### 04. Monday 18 – Sunday 24 October 1943

*Begun on a weekday in the Naafi, continued "Wednesday night" and finished "Sunday (on a seat in a garden on the sea front)", in his second week of training.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1. [Top:] Don't tell anyone how long I shall be here.
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> [Left:] It is AC 2. (two) {Aircraftsman second class}
> Section 2.
> 26 Flight
> "F" Squadron
> No 3 Wing
> 11 R.C.
> Skegness
> Lincs.
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I am writing in a Naafi canteen writing room.
> To-day we have drilled almost continuously, without much rest in the various breaks we have had, from it. We have had an Aircraft Recognition ~~exam~~ lecture at which they explained to us we should have to learn the twenty five most common aircraft flying, this is a bit dull as I know them.
> My boots are beginning to look fairly professional and the ~~[struck]~~ tin in which Mrs Tredgett put the polish is about two thirds empty.
> Perhaps you think I am writing
>
> *(p. 2)*
> rather a lot but I find it the next best thing to reading and it is rather like having a one sided conversation with you.
> I am one big ache all over but it's isn't hurting me at all really. We've got the most marvellous N.C.Os and Commanding Officer and I think they could make us march all night and all day if they wanted and we wouldn't bear them any ill will.
> They must be specially picked, they may shout and swear and half kill you but they are always dead fair and they won't swear at you for ignorance of rules. They are fully aware that they are there to teach you them. It's only if you know the rules and don't observe them that you get into trouble.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2.
> They say we shall be very happy here for all the hard work they give us. They tell us each day that towards the end of our training (which incidentally was not intended to be fitted into such a small space of time) we shall be so tired that we shall crawl into bed directly we come off parade but that ~~[struck]~~ we shall enjoy it. It has been proved apparently over and over again.
> The difficulty is to wake at ~~[struck]~~ 5.30 every morning without being called.
> Wednesday night - same time and place.
> To-day I received your letters at tea parade. Thank you very much for them. Please make certain that you always address it exactly as you did last time as it is most important in the R.A.F.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> For a start the number is essential as they call it out for you to come up and receive your letter.
> I've been paid a pound.
> We heard planes and had a warning the night I arrived at the last place I was at. I hope the last letter I posted to you was readable as I carried it through driving rain to post in the dark and I should think it was badly battered.
> How awful about that primrose but it's lucky it was Auntie Lillah who gave it you.
> I am glad your cold is better I think either you didn't tell me that your voice was bad or else it was after
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3.
> I went away as I don't remember it being husky although of course I knew you had a cold. Well, Mummy dear, you seem to have been having a good many route marches with the Boss don't overdo it or anything will you?
> I should think Martie needed a truck to carry those 424 lbs of blackberries. I hope you are keeping up your Mouse attack course Daddy. Did you underline Scotland in the letter because you thought he was there when he started off.
> Wiggs seems to be popping home all right, I wouldn't mind doing the same. I might try to remuster as a compass tester, it requires more
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Gardener seems to be always falling off ladders or being bowled over by something or living out of a tin.
> I should think the glass front door is a disadvantage luckily little miss Grouser is short and the windows are high in the outer door.
> I hope Wiggs isn't such a fierce N.C.O. as our ex corporal. Please give her and everybody my love.
> I wonder if you have heard what Nanny is doing. I hope she gets on all right. I am quite happy if very tired, Mummy, so there is no need to worry about me. I've decided to have the smartest turn out in the flight so I am having my uniform properly tailored.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4.
> Now about this A.T.C. certificate, I am going to write a stinker to the A.T.C. people and tell them to hurry up as they are holding up Records Office. I shall tell them to send it to you. Please address it to the address on the slip, put the slip inside, seal it and post it. More than that neither you nor I can do, whether or not it will arrive at the other station too late is beyond me. I can only hope that it will come in time.
> I should like to put that A.T.C. chap on the square here and give him a spot of our drill.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> You are right about it being bracing. It isn't very cold though as yet, ~~though~~ touch wood, and our clothing is very warm. I know the best way to make an RAF bed which affords me great satisfaction as I am always tucked up comfortably and warm enough, though goodness knows, we have little enough time in them. I went for a two mile run to-day, by the end of my training I have to do it in full battle kit, tin hat fixed bayonets rifles respirators and all the rest in seventeen minutes.
> I met somebody the other day who spoke and was literate, it was quite a treat
>
> *(p. 9)*
> 5.
> They talk about going to get the MILE here, they mean MAIL but I never understand them at first. Torty needs a great coat like mine to wear in his cardboard box. [Drawing: a tortoise.]
> I must write to Kit and thank him for his airgraph. Poor chap I expect he feels a very long way from home. Phil seems to be making a mistake to go to Polzeath if he wants to go away from the war, there's plenty of barbed wire and aircraft round there. There's a man here rather like Darlan, Daddy he looks like this. [Drawing: a small figure in a long greatcoat.]
> Don't forget Mummy's library book if you change yours if she doesn't go out yet.
> The letter you posted on to me is
>
> *(p. 10)*
> from a great friend of mine at Bruton he was sixteen days younger than me. He is now a Bomber Pilot flying on raids, he volunteered for the University air course at the same time as I did at Oxford. He passed his medical. I failed mine. He's been trained in Canada and I've lost track of him till now.
> Meals here are not as good ~~[struck]~~ as at the last station.
> You got the Hugo Station Grammar all right? It's something about Hugo's simplified system and it has graded exercises in it.
> I've been doing masses of drill.
> I have ~~two~~ real complaints against the air force. They didn't give
>
> *(p. 11)*
> 6.
> us clean blankets when we got here (we don't have sheets) they gave us filthy pillow slips and only to-day they marched us down to get clean blankets and then marched us back without them. My other complaint is that we can't get a bath during our eight weeks training. We can get ~~get~~ an occasional shower if we are off parade in time and miss our tea. We get cold baths if we like. My arm has recovered from the first stage but the vaccination pain has not yet begun. It's more a tickle I believe than anything.
> Have you had (a) my two lots of civilian clothes. (b) my RAF washing? (I had knives in my pocket)
>
> *(p. 12)*
> please let me know as soon as possible.
> We have civilian hair cuts, very very short. I got ticked off mildly for not polishing the back of my cap badge twice a day. Ask Wiggs what she thinks of that for a high standard of efficiency. Everything is polished both sides twice a day. I didn't know about the cap badge so they didn't mind.
> I'm two thirds of out of brasso so could you hold some in readiness for when I say the word, as you can't get it here except in enormous cans. Mail is quite safe, registered mail and parcels are collected by me personally from the Post Office Rat Section.
>
> *(p. 13)*
> 7.
> I bought some stuff called Boots foot comfort for my feet which eases them when they are tired. My cold is much better, though I have a rather chesty cough. It is an experience common to most people u/t (under training) I understand.
> I dropped my tin hat on my mug (china, not my own mug) and smashed it so I had to buy another one in the NAAFI, I believe my pay will be eventually 3/- a day less 6d for old ages pension (for when I retire from the RAF at 50.!!!) and less so much else for general breakage, this and that and what not.
>
> *(p. 14)*
> I am two pounds to the good at the moment so I am all right, I don't know how I shall stand after I've finished with the tailor.
> I am only about forty miles from Phil I believe, but my limit from here is two so I can't get very near you see. Well that's about all for to-night. I hope these long letters aren't a bore to read only I can let off steam, because there is no one intelligent here who enjoys anything like books of any kind whatever, so there's no one to talk to.
> Sunday (on a seat in a garden on the sea front)
> As you can see I have amended various parts of the letter. It is in black ink as I have bought some so that
>
> *(p. 15)*
> I can write airgraphs.
> Thank you very much for your letter and the parcel Mummy.
> The chocolate was very good indeed. I have a sweet ration but the coupons may be only spent in the Naafi and the Naafi hasn't any sweets so I am thoroughly swindled.
> I hope that the washing isn't an awful bother. If it is please say so.
> There is no need for the pyjamas to be sent back till the week after next if you are busy.
> My cold is improving though it has not gone. I ~~am~~ hope that you are both free of colds now.
> As regards dancing, which you mentioned, I don't dance with anybody Mummy as I don't go to the dances.
>
> *(p. 16)*
> I never have any time for dances or the cinema or anything like that. As a matter of fact I am going to have a shot at going to "Lost Horizon" next week. Whether it is the same one as we saw years and years ago or not I don't know.
> All the Naafi and Y.M.C.A people when they answer a question say "yes love (pronounced as in look)" or "no duck" (also pronounced as in look) which is a bit startling at first.
> Your letters are the chief thing to look forward to in the week. They call out our names and numbers and we go forward to get them.
> There is a picture in one of the Naafi's here which reminds me of one in your waiting room I should like to know whether it's a copy.
>
> *(p. 17)*
> Well Mummy you certainly seem to have kept up to your usual standard as regards the soap box and Mrs B.
> I wrote to her and thanked her for it exactly as if it was the box I have, but I do hope I didn't say "it has been most useful" by accident.
> I didn't know how I ought to end the letter so I put "yours very sincerely".
> I often think of you in the evenings and particularly for some reason when you are cooking the breakfast. You see I've been up two hours by then. I go to bed about ten fifteen but the other night there was to be an inspection of billets next morning and I scrubbed and cleaned from when I came off parade at six till about 10.45 pm.
> I am glad about Wiggs's notebook but Mummy dear you put in your
>
> *(p. 18)*
> letter "now she has some vicar's notes" It is Vickers. Actually you didn't put an apostrophe.
> I am dying for the time when I can come home for a spot of leave however short. Please may I have the family's addresses.
> Well that is more or less all and so I may as well finish off.
> With love
> your loving son
> David
> Thank you very much for the P.O.
> Please, if you have a soft leather cloth, like those gloves are made out of, sort of chamois leather I think could I have it as it is ideal for shining boots. [Drawing: a boot.]

*Notes:* Sheets numbered 1–7. The friend from Bruton who became a bomber pilot had been on the Oxford University air course with him; "He passed his medical. I failed mine." Torty is the family tortoise; Darlan is the French admiral. "Vickers" is the shorthand system. Martie, Wiggs and Nanny recur throughout.

### 05. c. Wednesday 27 October – Monday 1 November 1943 (the steeplechase; taken to hospital)

*Undated. Begun in the Y.M.C.A., continued "Thursday", and finished in purple ink on "Monday. Station Hospital" after he was taken in with a fever. An envelope postmarked SKEGNESS 4 NOV 1943 (scan 0) fits.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC.2. Silver D.R.
> No 2 Section
> 26 Flight
> ~~[struck]~~ "F" Squadron
> 3 Wing
> 11 R.C.
> Skegness
> Lincs
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> This is a Y.M.C.A. pen as I've left mine in my billet and so please forgive the blots.
> I've just had supper for nine pence in the Naafi: I had egg and bacon pie (an imitation, and a poor one at that of the Bruton egg and bacon pie.) peas and mashed potato, one custard tart, that looked as if Nanny might have stepped in it and "coffee".
> The uniform cost me nine shillings but it was well worth it as it looks as if it was made for me now.
> I wear khaki battle dress denims now for work. They are enormous (for a man 6'2" and 42 round the chest). It doesn't much matter though.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I'm sorry this is so blotchy. The paper is poor although it was quite expensive.
> The other day we had organized games all the afternoon. You could choose tug of war, five mile cross country, one mile steeple chase or various others.
> I thought I'd better prepare for the commando course so I decided on the one mile steeple chase.
> There were fifty of us in it and most of them were all just finishing their training here, not beginning it like me.
> I regret to say that I got in thirty eighth. It was very hard going. I had to cross three dykes (how do you spell it?) [pencilled above, another hand: "ditches or dykes"] full of muddy water about nine feet across with precipitous banks on the side for landing. [Sketch: a dyke in section — banks, 20' across the top, 9' of water.]
> Most people fell right in. I reached the spot like this [arrow] each time and slid in.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> I had also to cross two miniature ravines about eight feet across and twelve feet ~~[struck]~~ deep. Of my group no one would go first and I wasn't too anxious to do it but time was precious and so I shut my eyes and jumped and no one was more surprised than me when I landed safely on the other side.
> I cut my wrist and saw my first red arterial bleeding. It can only have been a capillary or something as though it was bright red it didn't bleed much.
> I was rather surprised as when I came in two P.T. instructors rushed up to me and asked me what I'd done to my arm. I looked down and it was covered with blood. I knew I hadn't done much though. They wanted me to go to the MI (medical something or other).
> I said it was all right and when I had washed it in the billet we discovered
>
> *(p. 4)*
> that it was only a small scratch which has since healed.
> Thursday
> Thank you very much for your letter, Daddy which I received this afternoon at tea parade. I was sorry to hear that the safe has not come up to your expectations. What a long time Mr W. is taking to get well.
> I could just imagine the scene from your surgery window that you described to me. Many times a day isolated scenes and incidents at home spring up in my mind. I had a strong impression of Auntie Lillah drinking tea with Mummy in the dining room the other day in the morning so I don't know what she can have been saying to be so vivid in my mind. How awful about
>
> *(p. 5)*
> her getting up to her old tricks with Miss Hamilton. According to expressions here "She hasn't got much common" {common sense} or "She doesn't use her loaf {brain. head. When I come home Mummy I shall speak about as badly as I did when I tried to talk cockney.
> [In purple ink:] Monday. Station Hospital
> a good deal has happened since I wrote the beginning of this letter. On Friday night we had "Barrack Room Sports" scrubbing floors etc for inspection. The best billet to get a late night pass which would be no use as we are too tired to use it.
> We sleep all night in rooms with soaking floors. It is forbidden by the medical authorities to scrub rooms in the evening but as we have no other time and we have to scrub them we have to do it.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Anyhow I think that may be one of the reasons I am here in bed, with five others from my billet. On Saturday morning I found my rifle to be very heavy and found drill very difficult to do efficiently.
> On Saturday afternoon (which is free - unless you are on fatigues or billet orderly) I found that I hadn't any energy to clean my equipment. I had fixed up with somebody else to go to see a film in the evening - I couldn't let him down so I went I felt awful, hot and cold, and ached all over. I had a bad cough.
> I bought some aspirins and took two that night. Somebody had asked me to accompany him to early service at eight on Sunday morning. I had agreed and my name had been taken down on Saturday morning. I went, and felt awful
>
> *(p. 7)*
> We have our passing out exam from one part of our training to the next next Thursday - If I miss it, I shall be put back three weeks. I shall also be in another billet. So I thought that if I just lay down in our room till lunch I might feel better.
> Unfortunately another boy in my room had not got up that morning and unknown to me while I was at church they had sent for the medical officer. And so when she - it was a woman, came she found me in bed.
> I explained when she asked me that I had felt a bit queer in church She listened to my chest took my temperature and pulse and carted both of us off to hospital by ambulance. I am certain I am normal now, this morning, Monday.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> I can't eat anything though as I keep being sick, I can't write any more for now as my arm aches. Don't worry I feel fine lying down in a lovely comfortable bed with sheets.
> Feel better this afternoon post just going so must stop.
> P.S. What about my washing it hadn't come back when I left my billet, did you get it?
> With tons of love
> your loving son
> David

*Notes:* On thin, blotting paper with a Y.M.C.A. pen. The "passing out exam" he would miss was the end of the first phase of training.

### 06. c. early November 1943 (in hospital)

*Undated, "Usual address". Still in bed in the station hospital a few days after the previous letter.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Usual address
> Skegness.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> I am still in bed but am much better. Thank you for the parcel containing the letter and also for forwarding Wigg's letter, and the Dolphin which I was very pleased to have (as a reminder that I was once a member of respectable society and haven't always sat at table with people who eat peas off their KNIVES!)

> I hope I come out of here within a week or I have to begin all over
>
> *(p. 2)*
> again in another billet.
> The Station Medical Officer sees us every day. He is a wing commander and is about fifty six. He looks a bit stale to me as if he had been doing this job till his heart had broken.
> You should have had the ATC certificate now Daddy as I have had a letter from the ATC CO saying he's sending it.
> In the opposite bed to me is a man who has just arrived. He makes us all feel sick with tales of his internal disorders for the past eight years. I've just discovered that he set up tombstones and dug graves in
>
> *(p. 3)*

> I can see the sea from the window but it's mostly grey. I can see the Norfolk coast so I suppose Cromer is somewhere over there.
> I did hope I might get a spot of leave just before Christmas but now I don't think I shall. (not because I'm in here)
> I'm reading Sergeant Michael Cassidy (Sapper) which seems to be the only book round here.
> Every night they give me two white pills and two brown ones to make me sweat and every night half an hour after I am sick. The funny thing
>
> *(p. 4)*
> is that they do make me sweat just the same.
> I have been sick five times so far. Once I was sick intentionally a nurse came in and she said "No 6 bed" (that's me) so I said "yes" and she told me to swallow three small white pills. Just after I'd done it she said "dash I meant to give those to No 6 in the Waaf ward they weren't for you at all". She wasn't perturbed but I was so I had a large cup of cocoa which is a fine emetic round here and I hope I brought the three pills up again anyway they did me no harm but I certainly was sick
> no more for now
> tons of love
> your loving son
> David.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> The other day the Wing Commander MO was by my bed and I said "Stand aside Wing Commander I am going to vomit." I vomited, I was sick, I threw up. The Wing Commander said "I stand aside" ~~Matron~~ and let me vomit. He vomited he was sick. The Matron said "Stand aside Wing Commander" etc.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Actually what happens is He says "How do you feel today laddie? What?" and I say "Oh by cough is bedder"
> "Thag you sub"
> And he says "What's that laddie"
> and I say "Buch bedder Sub"
> "Thag you" vaguely and he smiles and asks the nurse whether I am one of the new Polish recruits.

### 08. c. 10 November 1943 (re-flighted)

Post). So of course I am sent with eight others to open a new billet called No 3 Wing Disposals. I am here now in this billet. We have been shifting beds and stuff ever since five last night. > Friday.

> *(p. 5)*

> - - - - - -
> So much for the gloomy side of affairs and now to consider other things. At half past seven on Thursday evening I passed through some iron gates (that was clever wasn't it?) into a building in Cecil Avenue Skegness.
> I was met by somebody who asked for my service number and name as hearing what I wanted.
> I said that that would not be necessary for certain reasons and was
>
> *(p. 3)*
> taken into a room in the middle of which was a table. At this were seated two gentlemen one tall with a moustache and one shorter. They were drinking tea. They were both wearing white coats. I was not familiar with the one with the moustache but the other one was my old and esteemed medical adviser - Dr Philip Silver.
> He was surprised to find that I had apparently grown. At first conversation was a little stiff. Then Dr Silver and AC2 Silver went off to get supper at the Lion Hotel. This was very good and was washed down with mild beer, (which I suspect neither of us really liked very much).
> Phil never mentioned D when I asked about the family - Lynette was the chief topic of conversation except for you at home.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> Phil tells me, Mummy, that he considers you are very well which I am glad to hear. He enjoyed Flemish Farm, the plot of which I didn't know, Daddy, until he told me. After supper there wasn't a lot of time as I had to be back in the billet by 9.30.
> He drove me there in his Riley which I admired - After Lynette the Riley evidently is his greatest joy. Poor chap he seemed a bit tired and depressed and since I was, just about that time, we didn't make an exactly hilarious party. (Sorry I seem to be going up hill!)
> I wish that he didn't live so far away from Exeter.
> I can't get any leave as far as I know - even 48 hrs but if I can I should like to see Uncle Herbert and Phil has also asked me to Grimsby (transport provided)
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I wonder, should I write to Uncle Herbert and explain that as far as I know I am not able to get leave?
> Christmas will be pretty grim for you I'm afraid with the old man of the sea or rather our little ray of sunshine and our encyclopedia humana as guests.
> Perhaps Wiggs will get home and help you out. I wrote to James some little time ago, does he mention ~~[struck]~~ getting a letter from me?
> Thank you Mummy dear for the parcels, and thank you both for your letters. How much poor Kit must depend on letters I now realize as it's bad enough in England.
> I am to meet Phil every week which will make a terrific
>
> *(p. 6)*
> difference to my time here. It will make something concrete to look forward to. I am glad Nanny has a decent house to work and proper children to look after. It's a pity about the distance but still the advantages are so great that it must be worth it.
> I liked 'phoning to you Daddy and I heard you pretty clearly Mummy. Of course I know what we always say about the telephone and how you can't think of anything to say, but still it's nice to know you are there and hear your voices.
> I shall get eighteen days probably for leave when I do come if it's in April.
> The roads have had a coating of ice over them all day a few days ago, but now it's gone and it is warmer.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> It's like the top of Blackingstone Rock outside the front door of my billet. I am in a room with eleven men.
> The flight sergeant is a fearsome person normally but the other evening when I was at a German class, he was sitting opposite me, and he gave me an enormous wink. This made me wonder whether perhaps he was human after all.
> Well I feel that's about enough for to night.
> . . . . . .
> Re the flight sergeant, he enquired whether I spoke 'Germany'? at dinner to-day. He asked me in German and that is at a mistake he asked me whether I spoke Germany. I replied that I did and asked him how he was, in German. This he didn't understand but he evidently
>
> *(p. 8)*
> felt that he was very informed to be able to speak German.
> It is amazing what a difference it makes to know that your N.C.Os can be quite decent if stupid off parade, as otherwise you get the impression that they are totally devoid of normal feelings at all.
> I don't mean this applies to all N.C.Os as 23 Flight had very decent N.C.Os but some of them are awful.
> A boy asked whether he might have permission off tea parade on Sunday a little while ago, as his mother had come down to Skegness. The N.C.O. obviously disbelieved him (and he had no reason to) and told him he must get his mother to come along and identify him before he could go. The boy did and I know it's true because I met the mother. Don't you think it was beastly of the N.C.O.
> Still, they aren't very many like that
>
> *(p. 9)*
> Well I see that I haven't much time as I have to get back to my billet, so I'll tear this sheet in halves and keep the other half for future use.
> I am sorry my writing is so bad and the letter so scrappy.
> I lay down on my bed this afternoon (Sunday) (contrary to regulations) and slept for about an hour which freshened me up.
> I go to the cinema about once a fortnight or three weeks. I saw that Will Hay film Daddy, the night I arrived at Bedford but I somehow don't remember it at all and I believe I went out in the middle to get supper. I seem to have used this sheet after all. I shall have to run like mad.
> With love
> your loving son
> David.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> Please may I have more Mittens back.

*Notes:* Dr Philip Silver held a clinic in Skegness every Thursday, so the brothers met weekly. Philip lived in Grimsby. "The old man of the sea" and "our encyclopedia humana" were the parents' expected Christmas guests. Blackingstone Rock is on Dartmoor.

### 11. c. Saturday 4 December 1943 (back to 23 Flight; battle training)

*Undated. Back in 23 Flight "about a fortnight" after being moved into 27 Flight, now in the second phase of training; Philip, Lynette and Dorothy came on the Thursday.*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] This address cancels all previous addresses and it is unlikely that it will be changed for at least the next four weeks.
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> No 4 Section
> 23 Flight
> "E" Squadron
> 3 Wing
> 11.R.C.
> Skegness
> Lincs
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you for your letters some of which I may not have yet received. I have not yet received a parcel containing two pairs of socks, 1 collar(?) handkerchiefs Did you ever get one, also a pair of pyjamas. It was posted to you as far as I remember about a week ago to-day (Saturday).
> I don't badly need the things or anything and it's quite likely here waiting for me at the Post Office only I just wondered whether you ever got it from me in the beginning.
> Now, about these addresses, before I go
>
> *(p. 2)*
> any further. You remember that first I was in 26 Flight then I was ill went to hospital and moved into 23 Flight and then about a fortnight ago I was moved again into 27 Flight as I thought incorrectly.
> Do you remember I said that I had an interview with the Sqdn Ldr about it and he said he would see what he could do.
> Well yesterday having passed out of the first part of my training with 27 Flight (for the first time), I was told that I was to report back to my previous flight 23 as my claim that I had been incorrectly re-flighted was true and ~~[struck]~~ the mistake was being rectified. So back I went again to the old flight and forward I went in training instead of being at the beginning of the first week of my second phase of training I am
>
> *(p. 3)*
> at the beginning of my second week, one week ahead, having missed a week. Fortunately it's mostly stuff I learnt in the O.T.C. [Drawing: himself carrying a kitbag on his shoulder, labelled "kitbag" and "moving!"]
> I am not in the same warm kitchen, I am in a room with an imbecile who is quite harmless but a bit trying. However the room isn't bad as they go.
> Every morning when we go on colour-hoisting parade, it being dark, we march along with a white hurricane lamp at the front of the column and a red one at the rear. [Drawing: a hurricane lamp.]
> Last Thursday instead of Philip only at the clinic, there were Philip Lynette and Dorothy. We proceeded on a somewhat unsteady course through the dark to the George Hotel. [Drawing: three adults and a small child walking hand in hand in the dark.]
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I must say I wasn't awfully taken by the way Dorothy spoke to Lynette. She kept saying ~~[struck]~~ do this or do that "like a lady" until I was nearly sick it sounded like a maid speaking or something. Do destroy this letter.
> Philip seemed less tired and so was I. Lynette seemed about the brightest of us at first. She eat with a table spoon at one time. She struck me as being quite a nice little child if only Dorothy wouldn't muck her about so and she could be brought up properly. I felt that Lynette must go through a somewhat similar life as Groggy. She's very spoilt about going to bed I should think. I should have liked her with our family without Dorothy.
> Dorothy kept making the most awful slushy remarks and praising me which made me feel ill and did I think Phil as well. I think she saw I didn't care for it much.
> Things like she thought I must be the only
>
> *(p. 5)*
> one who cared about Lynette and so on.
> She also informed me that she thought something or other I forget what when she decided that the family should come over to see me.
> Well as Phil first suggested it to me the previous Thursday about bringing Lynette + D over I knew that it wasn't her idea at all to come over. For a long time I hoped she wasn't as bad as you thought now I think she's worse. But what a misfortune it must be for her to be like she is!
> Battle Training! that's what I'm on So look out boys! here comes a hand grenade.
> [Drawings: "this is my present rigout" — himself in tin hat, battledress and glasses; then a strip of figures: one throwing a grenade, one advancing with rifle and bayonet, labelled "forward", "jumps", "up and" (continued overleaf).]
>
> *(p. 6)*
> [Drawing, continuing the strip: "over" — a soldier leaping out of a shell crater as a grenade explodes.]
> Sorry it isn't very clear only it's ten past ten pm. and I'm very tired. I'm due in bed and lights out within five minutes. Thank you for your letter and the washing just received Mummy dear. I will write again, sooner I hope this time.
> with love
> your loving son
> David

*Notes:* He asks his parents to destroy the letter because of what he says about his sister-in-law Dorothy. The drawings show him in battle dress throwing a grenade and going "up and over".

### 12. c. mid-December 1943 (remustering; his feet)

*Undated, from 4 Section, 23 Flight. Applying to see the Commanding Officer about remustering; "The Russians are making newspaper reading quite interesting again".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver. D
> No 4 Section
> 23 Flight
> E Squadron
> No 3 Wing
> 11. R.C.
> Skegness
> Lincs.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> I am applying for an interview with the Commanding Officer for Re Mustering to a new job. If I can possibly manage it and my age is against me I may have a chance of some promotion. I now know that Flight Mechanic, the job I am in for is a blind job with no promotion, low pay and long hours.
> If I can I shall remuster to a Grade I trade. Flight Mechanic is Grade II. [Pencilled above, in another hand?: "not correct"]
> I must get the job I am thinking of as it's the only loophole to work that is interesting.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> It may not come off, everything in it is dead against me but I am going to have a go at it. The job I was first accepted for was Navigator Radio, it was only open to men above 23 years old but I was accepted for that so I think I stand a slight chance.
> If I had been Navigator Radio I should have been in control of certain apparatus in the Mosquito night fighters.
> The job I have in mind is firmly on the ground Mummy so don't worry.
> I have been having trouble with my foot and have had it treated at the M.I. room.
> [Drawing: the side of a foot, with an arrow at the arch.] I have a rather high arch to my foot and the boot presses down and bruises the foot
>
> *(p. 3)*
> While I was having it dressed, the orderly said that I ought to have my toes straightened. I asked him what he meant and he said that the tendons on top were too short which is what Miss Cheeseman said. He said that when I had finished my training I should probably have to go before a foot specialist and they would decide whether an operation was necessary. If it was they would cut all the tendons on top and straighten the toes out and then put the foot in plaster for a certain length of time. Whether or not he knows what he's talking about I don't know but until the MO tells me I have to have something done to my feet I am keeping them as they are.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> They certainly do seem to be getting worse than they were, sort of hammer toes like that. [Tiny sketch of a curled toe.]
> - - - - - - -
> I am so glad Nanny has got a good post and fancy it being my superior officer and for one day employer the Air Commodore.
> I hope Phil's leg is better and soon gets down to its right size as it must be rather alarming besides painful. I've been looking out for poetry books, Mummy, but up-to-date have not been over successful. There are three sort of Chamberlains or small Wheatons here. I never get a chance to get at the shops when they are open though. That is my difficulty
>
> *(p. 5)*
> about washing. I can't get out to post it. Saturday afternoon is my only chance of shopping and often then I have to get my hair cut and so on. If I am on a fatigue or Fire Picket or anything of that sort - I've had it. no Saturday afternoon for me.
> The Russians are making Newspaper reading quite interesting again aren't they.
> I wish I could tell you about the places we have lectures in and so on as it would make my letters more interesting only there seems to be the danger of F W 190s here as well as at home so I don't mention places where there are concentrations of airmen together, by name.
> The man in the kitchen with me has the most awful cold and cough
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I have ever heard in my life. This is not encouraging, he is about forty three and though he tries to be agreeable between coughs it's like making conversation with a cockney version of Gardener. It's almost impossible to sustain conversation for more than about two minutes and most of it consists of platitudes on almost any topic by me and a rejoinder of "that's right" or "Ah" from him.
> I announce that the Russians are doing well and ask him whether he doesn't think so. He says "Yes, they are really - considering -" pause, during which I have a strong desire to ask considering what? and he has a good cough. - I then remark that we seem to have got hung up in ~~England~~ Italy, and he asks me what I said and I repeat it feeling an idiot and he says "that's right" and then
>
> *(p. 7)*
> we both feel that we have said enough to last a little while and appear sociable and I dive into my kitbag and hunt for something which I know isn't there as a kind of retreat. He asks me what I've lost and begins a wholesale search for something which I haven't really lost and so I have to find it quickly.
> Well now for supper —
> Meat Pie Mashed Potato Beans tea and bread and butter 8d
> Did I tell you I took myself to dinner at Hildred's Hotel a week ago and reserved a table for myself and had soup, roast chicken and iced strawberry (how do you spell it, it sounds like "foole"). It was jolly good and jolly expensive. I sat near the Station Medical Officer, a wing commdr which
>
> *(p. 8)*
> rather agitated me at first.
> The Chaplain shakes hands with everyone who comes out of church which must be a bit tiring I should think. (I feel as if, Daddy, you'll make some joke here as you read it).
> That shelter is a beastly nuisance I wish I could help you.
> Mummy, if you want matches or combs this is the place for them. The shops are well stocked up here, there's no waiting list for shoe repairs so if you want any matches or anything let me know.
> With love
> your loving son
> David

*Notes:* He was first accepted as a Navigator (Radio), the radar operator in Mosquito night fighters, before being made a Flight Mechanic (see the Bruton letter of 9 May 1942). Hildred's was a Skegness hotel. One page about washing seems to be missing before scan 0 109.

### 13. Sunday 19 December 1943 (a Christmas letter)

*Headed only "Dear Daddy + Mummy … This is to wish you a Happy Christmas", written "almost a week before Christmas" on a Sunday; "Fancy me being twenty the Sunday after next" (2 January 1944).*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Pen-and-ink drawing at top left: dark roof tops and a bare tree against a dawn sky, captioned "roof tops at Dawn Parade".]
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> No 4 Section 23 Flight
> "E" Squadron 3 Wing
> 11. R.C. Skegness
> Lincs.
> Dear Daddy + Mummy
> This is to wish you a Happy Christmas. I am having to send it a little early but still it can't be helped. I shall be thinking of you on Saturday and I do hope that your guests don't make it more depressing than it need be.
> Next Christmas ought to see us all at home, I should think. Poor Kit is going to have the rottenest Christmas of us all isn't he? It's bad enough on your own in England but alone in Italy must be awful.
> This isn't a very cheery letter is it?
> Well I hope you like your presents, I've been most unsuccessful with yours Mummy but I got you a book on British Historians only to find it had a horrid picture of Louis the ?th having his head chopped off
>
> *(p. 2)*
> so I changed it. There wasn't much of a choice left so I got this, it isn't very thrilling I am afraid. As regards your present Daddy I am afraid you'll wonder what on earth I've got you this for. Anyway it may come in useful in some way. Really Skegness is a rotten place to get anything. I've also got a flint lighter in case matches are difficult for you, Mummy. It's really a very dud show this year all round I am afraid.
> I got Wiggs a little book with every conceivable aeroplane in it and a poor sort of A.T.S. notebook. James had a nice book ~~with~~ woodcuts and saints and aquatints and pictures of churches which I wanted for you Mummy only it would have meant him not having anything. He also had a leather note book about the size of this sheet of paper, very good. Phil had a book too young for him too old for Lynette of W. de la Mare poems and a saw like yours Daddy
>
> *(p. 3)*
> and poor Kit couldn't have anything.
> I so wanted to get you a set of six tumblers and a jug which I saw, Mummy. They were war ones but quite thin and a good shape. Not particularly good you know but I thought it would be nice to have even an ordinary set the same. ~~Unfortunately~~ I asked for them and told them I wanted them packed and sent by rail and they said that it couldn't be done. They had no facilities for packing and knew nowhere where it could be done. Well I don't know what happens to me at Christmas but I think I get Saturday Sunday and Monday off.
> It looks as if it might be a white Christmas here judging by the weather. I'll be seeing Phil on your birthday Daddy.
> [Pen drawing across the foot: a flat field with rows of pollarded trees, captioned "Lincolnshire landscape".]
>
> *(p. 4)*
> We had an aircraft recognition exam the result of which will follow us about in our records. I am glad of this as somehow I managed to get 100%.
> We have the most hair raising P.T. possible
> [Drawing: a figure crawling along a rope stretched from an island across an "ornamental lake".]
> We have to crawl across a rope stretched over a lake. I haven't actually done it yet but a lot of the others have and I fear my turn will soon be.
> Also we do this
> [Drawing: a plan of a bridge over water, with a rope hanging from one side and a "rope looped" under the bridge on the other; a figure swings across on the rope.]
> You swing on the rope to the loop and then climb up onto the bridge
>
> *(p. 5)*
> This looks dangerous but actually it isn't you might fall into the water but it isn't deep, only about three feet.
> I am not much good at dummy hand grenade throwing I either throw it up straight in which case it doesn't go far enough or else I throw too low.
> On Monday I fire the Sten machine carbine on the range, as I've missed a week I don't know anything about it. I've bought a pamphlet on it however.
> This letter you see is written almost a week before Christmas to make certain you get it all right.
> I didn't get up till nine am to-day (Sunday) the first chance of doing so I've had for months.
> To-night until Thursday we are liable to be called out at any moment for defence exercises.
> Poor old Winston I hope he gets better all right.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Fancy me being twenty the Sunday after next.
> Mrs Holladay's mittens fit under my service gloves and keep my hands beautifully warm.
> I understand that the authorities try to make service life in all services at Christmas as nice as they can.
> Well I do hope you have a nice Christmas and your guests aren't too much of a wet blanket.
> With lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Pen drawing at the foot: a farmhouse among trees, with a cart in the yard.]

*Notes:* With pen drawings: roof tops at dawn parade, a Lincolnshire landscape and a farm. Churchill was ill with pneumonia in Tunisia ("Poor old Winston"). Mrs Holladay had knitted him mittens.

### 15. Christmas Day 1943 – Sunday 26 December

*Headed "Christmas Day" (1943 added in pencil), continued on the Sunday.*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] The cake is smashing.
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> No 4 Section 23 Flight
> E Squadron 3 Wing
> 11. R.C. Skegness Lincs.
> Christmas Day. [1943 added in pencil]
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you for the lovely enormous parcel of cake apples etc. and for the letters and money. It made a great difference in that I felt it really must be Christmas after all. You certainly made the letters extra good. In fact I laughed so much at various things in both of them that I think my companions thought me a bit queer. I am sorry that the writing in this letter is a bit odd but I've just been innoculated. That brings me to more or less the beginning of Christmas.
> On Thursday afternoon I had my "seventy-five" this is the stronger of the two innoculations TAB. (Tab means Tetanus - something or other for typhoid I forget)
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I had it in my right arm which went sore and stiff within half an hour afterward.
> I went to meet Phil and had supper with him (nicely fried plaice and chips) possibly it wasn't plaice I don't know.
> We still drink beer, but the flavour has got weaker or something as though I still don't like it, the taste doesn't make me feel sick. I think he likes to have it so that he can say "two milds please".
> He looked more tired than last week but was very cheery and was evidently very pleased at getting a heater for his car.
> I was the quieter of the two as my arm wasn't very comfortable.
> He had two cases to see at the infirmary so left at about 8.45. (I met him at 7.30). I hunted round the various Naafi's and Y.M.C.A's for some aspirin. I took it last time I was innoculated and it made me more comfortable. I was unfortunately only able to get an unknown brand. "Hasc" manufactured by A J Hacks London.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> I took two of these and went straight to bed shivering. Well to cut a long story short Friday Christmas Eve found most of us pretty shaky. I went to bed all the afternoon. (we were free from lunch time onwards). I had carefully worked out that if I went to the midnight service at St Matthews I would then sleep in peace till late to-day and not get up if I fell ill.
> Now, however, I felt a bit groggy for that, and as four people had to be carried home who were on pier guard all night the night before I gave the idea up and decided upon the 8.0 am service. However by evening I felt much better and went to the midnight service went to bed at 1.30 am and got up and gate crashed and wangled a breakfast out of the cook house at 9.15 am. (breakfast is not officially served after 8.0 am)
> I did nothing all this morning. At 12.5 we were marched off to dinner. We were told not to miss Christmas Dinner as it would be better than any anywhere else. I suppose it was. But it wasn't a patch on yours Mummy.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> We had to wait an hour for it. It was served by N.C.Os and Officers and the youngest airman toasted the Group Captain.
> We had horrible beer, it was apparently very good beer as it had such a strong taste but that was why I didn't like it.
> I had a shandy (beer and lemonade) The lemonade rose to the top and I began to think that I liked shandy until lower and down then "Ugh"! (I bet you can't read that aloud) it was vile, I swallowed it very quickly.
> We had turkey, I had breast, and pork, apple sauce, potato and dried peas. (Dried peas are the staple diet of the R.A.F)
> It was pretty cold, (the food not the weather) and I had to eat with my left hand as my right one isn't much good. In fact I think I'll have to stop writing for a bit and give it a rest.
> On reading this through I see that I have given you a very depressing picture of Christmas day here. This is not so. Everybody is quite cheerful and all the beastly feverish effects
>
> *(p. 5)*
> of the innoculation have gone and my arm is only a little stiff now. If I hadn't had breakfast so late I could have eaten more.
> To continue - second course mince pie of a Lincolnshire type unknown to me, Christmas pudding custard bread cheese apples biscuits. There's nothing wrong with that.
> By the way I tried to make some Nescafé in my mess tin with Household milk and saccharin. It wasn't very successful. I got the Household Milk in a very ~~[struck]~~ blackmarket way. I walked into the Cooperative and said "is that Household milk on the ration" They said it was, but that Mrs Doings or somebody didn't want hers so I could have that.
> I am going to make it stronger to-night.
> The cake is lovely. It's an absolute cinch. It's pre-war absolutely. It's lucky it's Christmas as I have no where to keep any of it but nobody looks to see at Christmas.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> By the way you may be interested to hear the various sinister Beer gardens I frequent in my spare time.
> Principally, in the evening for supper.
> The Tower N.A.A.F.I. once Tower Café
> Downstairs a large room with tables for four and chairs. A hatch at which you queue for buns and tea cleaning material chocolate cigarettes, aspirin etc.
> Upstairs the same scene with a piano loudly played and this time supper being served from the hatch.
> The Menu is this if you arrive early
> Steak Pie 5d or
> Cottage Pie 5 or
> Sausage + n Batter 4d
> Sausage Savoury 4d or
> pork + beans 4d and
> peas 1½d mush 1½d and
> or beans 1d
> Tea or coffee
> or still lemonade.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> Similar to the Tower Naafi only varying in size and comfort are the Crown Naafi, Lumley Road Y.M.C.A. ~~[struck]~~ Grosvenor Road Y.M.C.A. and two rest rooms run by the Station Chaplains and civilians - which are really only large rooms with large fires and a limited supply of cake and tea.
> Sunday.
> To-day I have done almost nothing, my innoculation is a thing of the past. My arm is still a bit blown up but is only a little stiff and only hurts when knocked. It will be completely down by tomorrow I should think.
> Christmas intervened to prevent my telling you, Daddy, that I fired the Sten Machine Carbine on the range the other day. It was very interesting and I got eight out of thirty shots on the target. That probably sounds a very small score and I expect it is, but most people either missed or got two so I felt quite pleased.
> It fires 9mm. ammunition automatic or a single shot at a time. This is no Official Secret. You can buy a pamphlet in a shop
>
> *(p. 8)*
> on it, for the Home Guard. I did this and learnt it all up from the pamphlet as I missed all the loading and firing lectures owing to being mucked about from flight to flight.
> I have seen quite a good film called "Dear Octopus". It's a British film and though a little disjointed makes a very nice escape from the war. It's about a family and their reactions on reunion, on their parents golden wedding. It's not very clever or subtle or anything but it gives you a look into peacetime for a minute.
> Have you heard from James yet? I sent him a letter some time ago but heard nothing. Auntie Muriel sent me 10/- for Christmas and Birthday. I received it all right though it was addressed wrong to the old flight. I will write to her and Mrs Brierley and everybody.
> This letter seems to be coming with my washing. I can't tell you where I am going when I am posted but I can tell you that, please may I have my washing back quickly as I might be moving soon. I have lots to tell you but keep feeling that I must wait till I see you, forgetting that because I may be leaving here very soon I won't necessarily be seeing you. With love your loving son David
> [Up the left margin:] (week more here)

*Notes:* The TAB inoculation is against typhoid and paratyphoid A and B. The Sten carbine pamphlet was sold for the Home Guard. *Dear Octopus* (1943) is the film of Dodie Smith's play.

## 1944

### 16. Sunday 2 January 1944: his twentieth birthday

*Dated "Sunday 2nd January": "Well to-day I am twenty."*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1
> 1587757 AC.2. Silver D.R.
> No 4 Section. 23 Flight. E Squadron
> 3. Wing. 11.R.C. Skegness Lincs.
> Sunday 2nd January
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> Thank you very much for your letters and good wishes.
> Well to-day I am twenty. It seems absolutely incredible to me, I don't feel twenty, I feel as if I stopped growing up when I left Bruton and am stuck at 18½.
> To celebrate my birthday Philip Dorothy Lynette and a friend of Dorothy's arrived on Thursday so instead of my quiet chat with Phil "over a half pint" I had to make conversation. Well I didn't, they must have thought I was dumb but honestly I didn't feel the strength to take part in the conversation.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> 2
> I was pretty tired. I had been shooting on the range all day and had also had the nervous strain of throwing a live hand grenade.
> Well anyway I felt as if I was staying up to supper with Lynette and was only about two years old. It was exactly as if I was with strangers, well, I suppose two of them were. Anyway I didn't get the chance to tell Phil any family news or talk about the family at all. In fact we had supper together, Phil at one end of the table, me at the other and after that they went home.
> Well now about the news as regards my future movements. There are about five possibilities which may be classed into two classes. Whether I am posted next Wednesday or whether I go on disposals here and am posted later.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 3
> If I am posted on Wednesday it means I leave early on Thursday for an unknown destination. This destination is a T.T.U. or technical training unit.
> Here I do a course say fifteen weeks long (I don't know how long it is).
> This school or unit may or may not close down every so often when the members may go on leave. If it doesn't I don't get any leave until after that course which makes it about April. Cheer up that's the worst it can be. Now these technical T.U's are situated so far as I can make out in three main places. In George A. Birmingham's particular country. At a place where I believe the car we had before the Austin was made. (Its name begins with an animal) and at a place which means sea in French and would be super for me. Of course the latter would be nice.
> Now the best that could happen. I could be put on disposals on Wednesday
>
> *(p. 4)*
> 4
> and apply for a little holiday in sunnier climes, in which case I should be seeing you late Friday night.
> BUT this is extremely doubtful Mummy so don't think I am coming because I've said that.
> You see equally well I could apply for the leave and be told "nothing doing you might be posted any minute". So there's a chance of about one in twenty that I might get leave.
> To-day as I have ~~[struck]~~ said before is my birthday so I celebrated the morning by queueing from 10.25 till 11.40 for a bath. (There are eight baths for the use of several thousand men). Had a five minute bath and got back for dinner at twelve.
> They gave me cabbage boiled potato meat (species unknown) and prunes and custard.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 5
> I thought of Ingo Jolliphant when I eat the prunes.
> By the way my washing hasn't come but I am expecting it to-morrow. I threw a mills bomb on the bombing range. Oh I told you ~~that~~ before. I've got the pin as a souvenir. It went off with a deuce of a bang but I was safely behind sandbags. I got 86 for shooting but I don't know what it's out of, 120 I think.
> I am fed up, I have to get my eye very near the back sight of the rifle and I wasn't holding it so tight as usual once and the cocking piece jumped back and cracked my glasses.
> [Drawing: a pair of round spectacles, one lens cracked.] It doesn't obscure my view and seems to be quite secure so I am going to leave it like that until I reach my next station. Then I shall get a pair of RAF glasses and have my own repaired at my own cost.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> 6. P.T.O.
> I celebrated the afternoon with an extra large tea, this was not a good idea as I did it for the sake of celebrating not for the tea. I also celebrated by going to "Dangerous Moonlight" last night. This was the film from which the Warsaw Concerto originated. I didn't like it half as much as most people seem to have. It was an agony of worry lest people should go to the war or get killed.
> I can't see films any more like I could Mummy and think "just a film" they are so real they depress me for hours if they are war ones. I don't much enjoy them any more as when you come out you come suddenly back to real life and fighting somewhere to get supper.
> I am getting tired so I'd better stop or I'll make this letter very gloomy. As regards photographs of me, I can't with cracked glasses. Only three more days! The boy in my room has T.B. So I've moved (unnecessarily according to Phil) Tons of love your loving son David
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 7.
> We went for a fifteen mile route march in battle order on Friday including two manoeuvres on route. I was tired when I came back.
> battle order consists of a pack containing, mess tin, water bottle, towel soap knife fork spoon, groundsheet 1 pr socks. One respirator, 1 bayonet tin hat + rifle.
> I saw a good show got up by people on this station with the help of Ensa. It really was quite professional.
> I'll send you postcard on Wednesday letting you know as much as I can about what's happening to me.
> Two mile run to-morrow in full kit in 17 minutes!

*Notes:* He guesses at his technical training posting in riddles: "George A. Birmingham's particular country" (Ireland), the town "where … the car we had before the Austin was made. (Its name begins with an animal)" (probably Wolverhampton, home of the Sunbeam), and "a place which means sea in French" (probably Weston-super-Mare, for RAF Locking). He was posted to Cosford, near Wolverhampton, a few days later. *Dangerous Moonlight* is the film of the Warsaw Concerto. Ingo Jolliphant is presumably a Bruton schoolfellow.
