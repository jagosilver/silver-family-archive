**Anthea Leigh** (30 April 1928 – ) grew up in Topsham, Devon. She married **[David Silver](David-Silver)**, the brother of [Christopher Patrick Silver](Christopher-Silver/). David and Anthea were Jago Silver's maternal grandparents.

The memoir below — *A Topsham Childhood* — was written in her own hand and transcribed from the original manuscript pages. It is published here with her words exactly as written.

---

# A Topsham Childhood

## Memories of Growing Up in Devon, 1928–1946

### By Anthea Leigh

*Transcribed from handwritten memoir pages. Words marked [unclear] indicate passages where the handwriting could not be read with full confidence.*

---

## Chapter 1: Biography

I was born in the Belmont Nursing Home on 30th April 1928. The Belmont was part of a beautiful Georgian terrace in Exeter, later destroyed in the blitz. My sister Irene Mary had been born there on February 20th 1925. Our parents were Edith Irene Leigh (née Pyne) and Thomas William Edward Leigh. I was taken home to Sundew at Countess Wear — a bungalow which my parents had built when they were married. There was a filling station and garage run by my father and when this was built it was on an isolated cross roads, there were no other buildings in sight.

I have no memories of Sundew. I have a photograph of myself in a large frilly sunbonnet, beside the granite bird bath (which is now in the garden at School Walk). I have been told that my mother had a fourteen year old girl from the local Barnardo's home to train as a housemaid — she was called Minnie Edwards. I know that we had several dogs — an Airdale and two Kerry Blues have been mentioned — these were to protect my mother if my father was away from home. My father was an excellent Rolls Royce trained motor engineer but a hopeless business man. At the beginning of the 1930 recession he very nearly became bankrupt. Sundew and the garage were sold, my father took a job with a petrol company and we moved to Kenilworth.

Once again I have no memories of the place. Mary can remember the house and going to school there. I don't know what was wrong but I was clearly being very difficult because I was taken to London by my father to the first Child Guidance Clinic in this country. I was talked to, played with and observed. My father was told nothing was wrong with me — I still have the letter from the clinic. Soon after this visit my grandmother Angelina Pyne (née Joslin) came from Denver House in Topsham and took me back with her. She was driven by their chauffeur called Prescott in a Riley car. The car had a hood which folded down rather like a giant pram hood, a let down rack at the back for trunks, running boards, snap in windows and sheepskin foot muffs.

When we got to Topsham I was greeted by Minnie Edwards who had transferred from Sundew to Denver. There was a new kitten to play with called Ginger and best of all Minnie gave me a teddy bear. I loved him instantly, called him John, and he has been my constant companion for 77 years. I learned later that he came from Marks & Spencer and cost 2/6 — a huge sum from Minnie's meagre pay. He was quite hard, stuffed with wood shavings, had short yellow fur, moving arms and legs and a wonderful growly noise when he sat up.

Soon after I was taken to Denver the whole family returned. We lived in a rented house at the top of Bridge Hill while two semi detached houses were built in my grandfather Thomas Bolt Pyne's meadow, opposite his fruit tree nurseries. I think it was at this time that my father took over the Station Garage, Topsham. I can remember being taken to see the partly built house. In what was to be the front garden there was a huge pit full of white stuff. My hand was held very firmly, I was told it was lime and would hurt me very badly if I went near it.

My next memories are of school. When I was four I was sent to The Garden School Kindergarten, Exeter, run by Miss Challice and her widowed sister Mrs Hall. It was unusual in that it was a purpose built building, two class rooms and a large covered verandah in a small orchard at the end of a long garden. Miss Challice was gentle and quiet, Mrs Hall less so. I only went in the mornings. I learnt to read very quickly but not to spell, but didn't like school at all — too many people, too many demands, home was much more comfortable.

Home was Meadow Hayes, the semi built for us. The other half was for Reg (Thomas Reginald Pyne) my mother's younger brother, his wife Margery (née McGowan) and their only child Ann. When I was born I was called Anthea but this was shortened to Ann. When my cousin Ann was born three years later I began to be called Thea to reduce confusion — I liked being Thea.

I find it difficult to date events beyond starting school. Meadow Hayes was a comfortable house to grow up in and apart from not liking school, life was fairly straight forward. I had trouble going to bed and getting to sleep, I was afraid of the dark and of a Red Indian in full feathered headdress who stood on the landing when my mother put me to bed — there was also a giraffe who sometimes looked over the door. No matter how many times my mother took me to see that there was no Indian on the landing I was convinced that he hid until she had gone down stairs.

Mary and I played together when we were very small but were quite different and as soon as my cousin Ann was big enough to join our games I found I had far more in common with Ann than with Mary. Mary was a pretty little girl with curly brown hair (mine was fair and straight) — she loved pretty frilly clothes and being clean and tidy. I liked the plainest clothes possible and hated being 'dressed-up' for tea parties etc.

While I was small we spent a lot of time at Exmouth on the beach. My grandmother rented a beach hut and at that time they stood at the back of the beach facing the sea. Inside there were chairs, a table with table cloth, china, cutlery, towels and some kind of cooker (parafin?) for boiling a kettle. I loved the beach and sea, my mother said as soon as I could crawl I crawled into the water. I can remember wearing rubbery garments like large elasticated knickers over our clothes — we could paddle and sit on wet sand without getting wet. To go in the sea we wore rather droopy swim suits and hats of thin rubber. Meals at the hut meant being clean and sand free sitting at the table with Grandma in charge.

Holidays when we were very small were spent at Lower Burston, a farm near Bow farmed by two of my grandmother's brothers, Will and Fred Joslin. The house was large and very old fashioned, no electricity, no running water. Lighting was oil lamps and candles, water came by hand pump from a well in the yard, cooking was by wood fire in an enormous black range. Mary and I shared a half tester bed so high it had steps up to it and a deep feather mattress. The hall to the front door (never used in my time) had a lovely tiled floor and a wide shallow staircase with a coloured glass window on the half landing. Through the yellow glass all was summery outside, the blue glass made everything wintery and the red glass made the garden catch fire!

## Chapter 2: Early Memories

I think the first thing event in my life that I can remember clearly is a visit paid to my grandparents when I was about three. My parents, elder sister Mary & I lived at Kennilworth, near Warwick at the time. I remember quite clearly the car — a Riley which was sent from Topsham to fetch me. It was a Riley, with a folding hood & celluloid windows. The window pieces took out when the hood was down — when it rained they were fixed with large press studs. There were sheepskin foot muffs for our feet. The car was driven by a man called Prescott who wore a navy suit & cap — neither of my grandparents — Thomas Bolt & Angelina Pyne — ever learned to drive.

When talking to my parents one day I thought I remembered being taken, in my pram, through a green painted door in a high ~~a~~ garden wall. The door led down two stone steps into a country ~~a~~ lane. My parents later showed me a house in Topsham which they had rented for a while when I was perhaps eighteen months old — the garden door & steps were exactly as I remembered them, though the 'country lane' was a rather overgrown footpath leading to a railway crossing.

One winter the Exe was almost frozen across. When the tide went out great sheets of ice, sometimes several yards wide, were left on the mudbanks. At this time Mary & I were not at school but being taught at home. Z* each afternoon were sent for a walk, unaccompanied. I think probably my younger sister Gillian was too ill to be taken out — anyway, the river bank was forbidden but although usually fairly obedient, the ice floes were too tempting. We climbed down the low stone wall from the footpath known to us as 'Smallick's Walk' (later found to be Sir Alec's Walk) onto the foreshore — & slid on the ice floes. The ends of the floes were over the river, which was covered with smaller moving pieces of ice, sometimes the floes were at alarming angles — but we had a lovely time & never gave a thought to the danger. We did this every day until the floes melted & no harm came to us.

At this time we also made friends with a group of fairly rough children who shared their home made sledges with us. They knew we came from 'Denver', some of their parents worked for our grandfather — and we found them exciting company. Their speech ~~language~~ we sometimes found difficult to understand — accents were broad & there were swear words we hadn't met before.

When I was about four I went to Miss Challice's Garden School — a kindergarten in Exeter. The school was unusual, being a long low purpose built wooden building at the end of an orchard. There were two class rooms & a wide covered balcony where we often did lessons when it was too wet to take our tables & chairs under the fruit trees. I can remember enjoying grammar lessons — a sentence was written on the board with no capitals, no punctuation marks — & we took it in turns to correct the sentence with beautiful coloured chalks.

I can remember playing with a 'garden' — a set of toys much like Britton's make now — with flowers that pegged into flower beds & crazy paving paths. I can remember being sent to stand in the corner (probably for being rude). On the way to the corner I passed a table on which were set out trays of pencils, crayons, rubbers etc for our use. I snatched a rubber & occupied my time in the corner by tearing the rubber into small shreds & spreading them around me on the floor.

There was no uniform at this school. I can remember wearing leather gaiters in the winter & someone using a button hook with a horn handle to fasten them when it was time to go home. Mary & I were ~~I was~~ driven to school every morning by my father and fetched by him at noon.

Afternoons at this time were occupied by going for walks with my mother or visiting her friends for tea. In the summer we often spent the afternoon on Exmouth beach — driven there, with Grandmother, by Prescott. We had ~~There was~~ a hut at the back of the beach (on a site rented from Exmouth council) in which to change if we swam — it also contained folding chairs, buckets & spades & the things necessary to make tea, china, a folding table & table cloth! We wore dresses of smocked cotton with matching knickers & white frilly sunbonnets. When we swam we had droopy knitted swim suits & caps of very thin rubber often with a decoration, rosette or flower, of coloured crinkled rubber.

## Chapter 3: John Bear

In the great depression, around 1930, my father could no longer make his garage pay. He had had it built (on the cross roads at Countess Wear) and the bungalow, called Sundew. He was an excellent Rolls Royce trained engineer but a very poor business man. He got a job with a petrol company and we had to go to live in Kennilworth.

I cannot remember anything about it — Mary can remember the house we rented and going to school there. When I was three I was ill for a long time and it was decided to send me back to Topsham to get better. My grandmother came to fetch me — she was driven by Prescott, the Denver car driver in their Riley, an open car with a hood & rather like a pram hood & snap in celluloid side screens.

When we got to Denver there were two treats to entertain me — a new ginger kitten belonging to the household, and specially for me, a teddy bear.

Minnie Edwards had been our housemaid at Sundew and had gone to work for my grandmother when we moved to Kennilworth. She bought the teddy bear to comfort me — from Marks & Spencer's in Exeter for 2/6 — a huge sum in those days. I don't know what she was paid but I do know that living in servants earned very little.

The bear was quite hard, stuffed with wood shavings, covered with short yellowish fur, and when he leant forward he made a deep growling noise. He had button eyes and an embroidered nose & mouth. I loved him instantly, called him John (don't know why) and was very rarely parted from him.

I made him clothes, mostly sewn rather badly, or knitted. Once my grandmother helped me to cut out a skirt for John. He usually wore a skirt because I found them easier to make than trousers. Instead of sewing the side seams, I glued the skirt together — my grandmother was shocked but I couldn't see what the fuss was about — it worked.

My mother's ~~Gran's~~ youngest brother Harry was unmarried & lived at Denver. He had several girlfriends and I remember one called Trixie Turner. I thought her very beautiful — she was friendly too and when I was about six or seven she won my heart completely. She gave me an outfit she had knitted for John Bear! It was a set of blue knee socks, blue shorts, and a Fair Isle jersey & hat. It was beautifully knitted on very small needles with fine wool so that it was the right scale for John & he looked wonderful when I'd dressed him. She later had a loud and very fierce row with Harry ~~soon~~ afterwards & didn't come to Denver again — I was so glad she gave me John's outfit before the row happened.

One of my God mothers was a friend of my mother called Peggy Dolling. She lived on the Strand in Topsham (in the house next door to the house Marion lives in). She was kind to me & sometimes gave me lovely presents. One present was a 'dressing case' (for make up etc) but I immediately used it as a suitcase for John — I packed his clothes in it when we went for holidays.

## Chapter 4: Lower Burston Farm

Many of my earliest memories are connected with Lower Burston Farm. My mother's mother, Angelina Joslin, came from Huish in North Devon but sometime moved to Lower Burston. When her father died, her brothers Fredrick & William took over the farm. After she married Thomas Bolt Pyne my grandmother continued frequent visits (Gran describes some of her memories of the place).

The house had at one time been fairly grand for a farmhouse. There was a front door (never opened except for weddings & funerals) which opened into a huge tiled hall. One side was lined with glass cases containing stuffed animals and birds. The staircase was wide with shallow steps going to a half landing with a tall ~~huge~~ window decorated with coloured glass. It was very exciting to kneel on the floor & squint through one colour at a time, apparently changing the seasons outside. The second half of the staircase led to a large ~~huge~~ square landing with a strange corner shaped stone trough near the window. There were five or six big bedrooms and a little used bathroom, no WC. Mary & I shared a ~~big~~ bedroom that had a high half tester bed. It had a small set of steps to help us climb in & there were no lights upstairs except candles.

My first task when I had unpacked my clothes was to unpack John Bear's suitcase and make him a bed. Meals were eaten at a beautifully polished ~~vast~~ oval table in the breakfast room, a cheerful room with a range & a door into a conservatory. If we wanted tomatoes with our meal we stepped into the conservatory and picked one. There was a dining room but I can only remember it being used once and that was for my great grandmother's ~~both~~ ninetieth birthday, when I was about three. She was very upright, wore black floor length clothes and a white lace cap on her hair. The drawing room was also never used — it was a beautiful long room with floor length double doors down one side which opened on to a tiled verandah with a glass roof supported by cast iron columns and covered with Wisteria. There was a ~~grand~~ piano tucked into one corner — to me, when I was three, this room seemed as big as a tennis court!

The kitchen was next to the breakfast room and had a huge fire with iron brackets over it to hold cooking pans and an enormous black ~~vast~~ kettle. There was also a dairy where milk was stored, cheese, cream & butter made. It was quite a big room, faced north, had a stone floor and slate shelves & metal ~~netting~~ netting over the windows.

Behind the kitchen there was a scullery with another open ~~huge~~ fire, always burning. The fire place was just a slightly raised stone shelf with a chimney above. ~~Huge~~ Pieces of tree burned slowly twenty four hours a day — when hot water was needed smaller pieces of wood were used to 'wake up' the fire. From the scullery led two 'box' (enclosed behind a door) staircases. One led to a group of small bedrooms for maids, which had a passage leading to the main landing of the house. The other lead to a single room for men servants, it had no connection upstairs with the rest of the house.

The water came from a well just outside the back door — there was a pump and stone trough over the well. Carrying water was an endless task — but worth it, the water was ice cold & tasted wonderful. From the pump yard there was a gate into an orchard (about half an acre) with hen houses near the gate. We went there to collect eggs for our breakfast. From the other side of the pump yard a long covered passage led to the farm yard, all the animal houses, barns etc. I didn't much like passing the ferrets which lived in cages in the passage.

We called Fred & Will 'uncle' though they were our great uncles. Fred was rather silent & withdrawn, he died when I was quite young. Uncle Will was my favourite person at Burston, followed closely in my affections by Art. Art (I suppose he was Arthur but I never heard him called anything else) was a farm worker who had infinite patience, helped us to ride farm horses, milk cows, feed calves, showed us nests of new kittens — and still did all his work. He was very slow moving & spoke in a soft slow Devonshire voice — I loved him.

Uncle Will was kind to Mary & I and sometimes took us for walks in the evening. Like all farmers at that time, he carried a gun under his arm. One evening as we stood at the top of a steep valley a rabbit rushed along the bottom followed by a weasel. Mary & I were horrified and kept saying 'Poor rabbit'. Uncle Will said the rabbit could run faster than the weasel but became so frightened that soon it would stop running & just scream until the weasel killed it. Seeing our awful distress he aimed his gun & shot — not the rabbit, as any farmer would, but the weasel!

On another occasion he took us to the gate of a small meadow surrounded by earth banks and trees. He told us to stand very still and to be quiet. He then made a soft whistling sort of noise — & rabbits started to come out of the holes in the bank, hesitantly at first, nervously — but soon they became confident & started to eat. My memory says there were hundreds, my adult reason suggests quite a few but not hundreds! We watched for ages, rabbits of all sizes, some in families, some alone. Then it was time to go home so Uncle Will clapped his hands sharply & every rabbit disappeared in a few seconds. It's a magical memory. ✳ A

Our holidays at Burston came to an end a few years after Uncle Will married, because he married a witch. She was called Eva, looked like a witch and must have bewitched him. Holidays there became uncomfortable, at any rate for the adults. During this time I got to know Edith (Eva's sister). She was an invalid, having a 'decline' (TB perhaps). She spent most of her time in the garden on a long rattan sun bed, she was very pale & quiet. Eva said 'There is no need to notice her'. I did, I sat on the grass beside her bed/chair and we talked about all sorts of things — my John Bear, the dolls she had when she was a child. At Christmas among all the large presents we were given was a carefully wrapped small bar of Cadbury's milk chocolate from Edith, one for Mary & one for me. My grandmother suggested it was a waste of effort to send anything so small — I felt it was a great treasure because it had come from Edith and I was very sad when she died. ~~I think my attitudes in such matters came from my mother and my grandfather, both kind, loving people.~~

✳ A Sometimes early in the morning we picked a basket full of mushrooms to be fried for breakfast — I wouldn't eat them, I thought they looked & smelled horrible.

There was a bakery in Bow village, about half a mile down the road from the farm. Mary and I were allowed to go there on our own and I can remember being asked to buy a dozen currant buns — they were huge, hot, altogether delicious — so we bought a dozen and a half and ate three each on the walk home.

I also bought some pale green wool at the village post office and while I was on holiday started to knit John Bear a jersey. I could knit quite well but very slowly — there were so many other things to do. My grandmother kept criticizing my progress & quite spoiled my enthusiasm for knitting — I finished it later at home.

## Chapter 5: The Pyne Family

My mother was Edith Irene Pyne before she married my father. The Pynes had lived in Topsham for a very long time and were well known there. My grandfather was Thomas Bolt Pyne who owned and ran Denver Nurseries. He was a nurseryman and florist — a hard-working man who had built up the business. My grandmother was Angelina, née Joslin.

They had a family of [unclear] children. My mother Edith Irene was one, and Uncle Reg was another. Uncle Reg later married Margery McGowan and they had a daughter Ann who was my cousin.

Grandfather Pyne was quite a character. He was well respected in Topsham and known for the quality of his plants and flowers. Denver Nurseries had several greenhouses and supplied flowers and plants to customers over a wide area. He worked very long hours and expected his family to do the same.

Grandmother Angelina was a gentle, quiet woman — quite different from Grandfather in temperament. She came from the Joslin family. I remember her as always being kind and welcoming to us children when we visited. There were always good things to eat at her house.

The nurseries were a wonderful place for children. I remember the smell of the greenhouses — the warm earth and the growing things. There were tomatoes growing in some of the houses, and the smell of the tomato plants was [unclear] and rich. In the spring there would be rows and rows of seedlings and young plants being brought on for sale.

My mother always spoke very fondly of her childhood in Topsham. She loved the town and the river. The Exe estuary was very beautiful and we children loved to go down to the water's edge when we visited. We would watch the boats and the wading birds.

Ann and I were close as children and played together whenever we could. During the school holidays we would spend time at Topsham together. We explored the nurseries and the garden, and played down by the estuary. My sister Mary, being the eldest, was sometimes put in charge of us — she took this very seriously though we were not always [unclear] cooperative!

Gillian was the youngest of us three sisters and tagged along as best she could. The cousins — Ann and ourselves — had some very happy times together at Topsham.

The garden at the house was full of flowers, as you might expect from a nurseryman's family. I remember the sweet peas particularly. They grew in great abundance and the scent was lovely. There were roses too, and all kinds of [unclear] plants both in the garden and in the greenhouses.

Christmas was always a very busy time at Denver Nurseries. There was holly and mistletoe to be cut and prepared, wreaths to be made up, and all manner of festive greenery. The whole family helped at the busy times. It was hard work but there was a good [unclear] atmosphere and we children enjoyed being part of it all.

My father, Thomas William Edward Leigh, who had trained as a motor engineer with Rolls Royce, was rather different from the Pyne family. He was not a countryman in the same way. But he and my mother were well suited and he was always welcomed by the Pyne family.

I have very happy memories of the Pynes and of Topsham. It was a special place in my childhood and the family connection to it, through the nurseries and through generations of Pynes living there, gave us a strong sense of belonging. The nursery business did not continue indefinitely but those memories of the place and the people remain very precious to me.

## Chapter 6: Denver House and Denver Nurseries

### Denver Nurseries

I have been told that my great grandfather George Pyne grew vegetables and sold them in Topsham from a donkey cart. I'm not sure that I believe this as his father Joseph Thomas was called a nurseryman before him.

I know that most of Denver house was built in 1901 but there was part of the house there earlier. The stables, working buildings etc were mostly built soon afterwards. The stables were wonderful, built of brick with cast iron fittings (divisions between stalls, mangers, hay racks etc) and a tiled floor. There were stalls for four heavy horses and a loose box for a cart pony. A 'sick box' although part of the same building had a separate entrance. Above was a loft for hay and straw — there was a cutting machine at loft level (in a separate room) which chopped hay and delivered it through a chute to the chaff room below.

The loft was reached by a straight up the wall wooden ladder and as soon as our legs were long enough to step sideways from the ladder to the loft floor we had wonderful chasing games — up the ladder and down a hay shoot into a surprised horse's hay rack — then out of the rack, along the edge of the manger and out of the stall — squeezing past an enormous Shire or Clydesdale!

In my grandfather's (Thomas Bolt Pyne) time Denver Nurseries employed about thirty men, with casual workers, usually women and children, taken on for fruit picking. The nursery was about growing and developing fruit trees and bushes for sale — the fruit was a by-product of the fruit tree and bush production. The fruit trees were sent all over Great Britain and sometimes abroad. When a fruit tree was to be sent to a customer it was dug up and taken to the packing shed by wagon from the field, pulled by the pony. In the packing shed a 'bacon wrapper' was spread on the floor — this was a huge sheet of sacking made of several smaller sheets which had once been used to wrap sides of bacon. The sheets were greasy and smelled strongly of bacon. We borrowed them to make tents and wigwams and became very smelly ourselves. A pile of straw was laid on the bacon wrapper, then a 'tree shape', a wooden frame roughly the shape of the tree. Next the tree was laid down, its roots tied in a sacking ball full of damp straw. More straw was piled on top of the tree, another bacon wrapper laid on top, and the whole thing was then sewn together in a tight tree shaped parcel, with destination labels sewn to the outside. Packing was a skilled job and someone called Bill Chambers was chief packer when I was a child. Men who worked at Topsham station between the wars can remember huge stacks of tree parcels waiting to be loaded into goods trains — all delivered there by horse and wagon.

My mother can remember when the nursery was run by her Pyne grandparents — she and her brothers (and parents of course) lived in various rented houses in Topsham. They moved into Denver when George Pyne died — probably some time in 1916 when my mother was fifteen. She lived there until she married in 1923.

Among the various fairly specialist buildings that made up Denver Nurseries was an apple store. It was long and narrow and partly underground with an earth floor and thick walls and a large stone slab which had been the floor of a bread oven — and was still blackened.

### Denver House

I don't know much about its history — a Topsham map suggests it was built in 1901, but knowing the house as I did, I'm sure there was an earlier house that was added to. The right hand end (looked at from Denver Road) had long Georgian type windows which came almost to the floor and that end of the house always smelled damp. Between the front (sitting room and kitchens, scullery etc, dining room) there was a very thick wall, perhaps 2–3' thick which must have once been an outside wall.

As far as I was concerned it was just Denver, big, comfortable and secure. My grandmother Angelina was always a domineering woman. She was brought up to believe women were born to wait on men and tried to instil this belief into my mother. Irene, my mother, was the only girl with three brothers (the fourth, Wilfred, died before she was born) and was expected to wait on them. My grandfather, Thomas Bolt, didn't think this way and encouraged her to think for herself and to do whatever her brothers did.

Although I didn't understand much about it when I was small, I know now that my grandparents were unhappy together and by the time I was old enough to notice, they led separate lives. My grandmother did everything domestic, my grandfather ran the business but they only met at mealtimes. My grandfather sat on my grandmother's right at table — he was deaf in his left ear. There was always a housemaid who lived in an attic bedroom, and later a companion/housekeeper who had a small bedroom on the first floor, also a woman who came to the house all day on Monday to do the washing, on Tuesday to do the ironing and on other days to scrub floors. She was called Mrs Sargent and was the mother of Roy Wheeler who later bought our house, Meadow Hayes. The laundry was done in a disused cottage slightly behind the main house — there was a wash boiler and a small fire under it to heat the water, and a huge mangle with wooden rollers. There were washing lines on a piece of grass called 'the drying green' beyond the stable yard.

There was also a boot boy — it was his job to clean and polish everyone's boots and shoes — he did this in the scullery early every morning. He also cleaned the grates — open fires were the only form of heating — laid paper and sticks and coal ready for the maid to light the fire, and filled the coal hods and log boxes. He also fed the hens and collected eggs and ran errands. He was usually about 14–15 years old and was replaced by another school leaver as soon as he was old enough to get a real job. The gardening was always done by one of the nursery men.

During part of the second world war my mother and Mary, Gillian and I spent some time at Denver while my father worked on aeroplanes and lived in digs in Mold, N Wales. The house always seemed too full of people for me — there were four evacuees from London and at various times other relations. I found a wonderful place to escape from so many people. On the second floor there was an enormous billiard room — no longer used for billiards, now a store. The billiard table was pushed to one end and all the discarded feather mattresses were piled on top. In a little book case by a dormer window were books left over from my uncles' childhoods — adventure stories mostly and inside I found 'Reggie's book!', 'Joscelin's book', 'Harry's book!' I crept up there when it became too busy downstairs and chose a book, then burrowed into the feather mattresses, usually with a cat for company. My grandmother never found me there and although I expect my mother knew where I was she didn't give me away.

Denver Nurseries formed the most wonderful play place a child could imagine and we spent most of our free time there. There were small sheds to turn into play houses, lots of animals, hard paths for cycling or pushing dolls' prams — and dozens of kind men working all round the place. They were all patient, mostly just getting on with their work, but never let us come to any harm. There were store rooms full of boxes (dolls' hospital beds), canes, sacks, baskets. We were allowed to take and use anything if we were careful and always returned it to its place. It was much bigger and more exciting than any modern adventure playground.

Between the stables and the cow sheds there was a manure heap. This had a back wall about ten feet high, its side walls were formed by the end of the stable and the side of the cow house. Its floor was some kind of grid with a tank underneath and a pump to pump the liquid into a special 'water-wagon' to take it to the fields. The back wall was a 9" brick wall and I can remember Ann and I daring one another to walk along it. If we fell off one side we went into the manure, if we fell off the other side we should have gone through the roof of Cousin Ted's glass house — we never fell off!

The cow house held four cows — they were 'house cows' producing milk, cream and butter for the family. There was a large colony of bats living in the cow house. I can remember climbing on top of the cast iron mangel chopper and lifting a bat off the rafter where it was hanging upside down. It continued to hang upside down fixed to my finger and didn't seem to notice. Later when there were no cows Ann and I kept our ponies in the cow house. I didn't much like going there at dusk, the bats came flying out but never flew into us.

My grandfather was interested in archaeology and had collected various pieces of broken pottery brought to him by the men who worked in his fields.* Because someone ploughing in a field to the right (as you face Exeter) of the Topsham Road uncovered the top of a wall. It was discovered to be the remains of a Roman bake house — a plain paved floor, walls and an oven. It didn't look like an oven, just a low slab of stone blackened by fire.

At Denver there was one large room in an outbuilding called the 'cage'. It was where the picked fruit was sorted, packed and stored during the summer. It had lots of windows which opened inwards, the outside was covered with fine meshed netting (to keep birds out) and the sliding door had a lock. While archaeologists were working in my grandfather's fields all their pottery finds were kept in the cage. I can remember being shown the baby's feeding bottle, a brooch, a spoon and various bits of pots.

My grandfather found a Roman well in land to the left of the Exeter road and a large hole was dug in a field on that side of the road — I think in expectation of finding a Roman road. One Sunday after church my grandfather took Mary and I to see the hole. I don't know how big it was but I was told not to go near the edge. I did and fell in. I can still remember screaming with fright because there was no way out — my grandfather immediately scrambled down to be with me, which stopped my screams, because I was quite unhurt. Then he found he couldn't get out, the sides sloped and the soft earth crumbled if he tried to walk up. In the end someone brought a rope and threw one end to us — we could walk up the slope holding the rope. I don't remember getting into trouble for being such a nuisance, but I don't ever remember my grandfather getting cross with me.

A growing problem was solved and a Roman bake house was found. In a field called Hurdle Field some young fruit trees failed to thrive. My grandfather couldn't understand why, the failing trees seemed to form a pattern. Somehow an aerial photograph was taken and the trees with yellow leaves formed a clear shape. Archaeologists became interested and were invited to dig — and quite a large part of a Roman bake house was found, not very far down — also a midden (rubbish tip). There was a plain paved floor, stone walls and a large stone slab which had been the floor of a bread oven — and was still blackened. *Back to D*

When I enquired at the museum in Exeter about the Roman artifacts found on my grandfather's land I was told they were not stored at the museum in Queen Street but in another store used by the museum and that everything was destroyed during the blitz. I expect at the time someone drew plans and made notes about the bakehouse, well, midden etc — but if so I don't know who, or if the records exist.

## Chapter 7: Christmas at Denver

Until I was ten or eleven every Christmas day was spent at Denver. The day started very early, with a huge knobbly knitted golf stocking (my father's) rustling on the end of my bed. I shared a room with Mary, so we had a lovely time showing one another the toys & sharing chocolate and sweets. After a hurried breakfast we went into the sitting room & received our 'main' present from our parents. I can remember several — a beautiful white dolls house, a red fairy cycle, a baby doll with a china head. Next, dressed in our best clothes, carrying our presents (if possible) we set off for Denver. This meant going down our garden path, then only a few yards along the road, then between ~~& into~~ the huge iron gates and up my grandparents drive. Denver was decorated with paper streamers, balloons, holly and mistletoe. My grandmother would be busy in the huge kitchen, helped by Minnie the housemaid

## Chapter 8: Topsham

The house in Topsham was in Monmouth Hill — a semi-detached house with a long front garden, and a yard at the back with a shed and an outside lavatory. It was called "Doone". Daddy had got a job with Mr Seldon who had a garage on the Strand. He was the only motor engineer — Rolls Royce trained — in the district, so he was kept very busy. The garage was just a big shed really, with a pit in the floor for getting under the cars. Mr Seldon also sold petrol from a hand pump at the front.

Mummy had Mary and me to look after — Mary was about seven and I was four when we moved there. She also had Granny Leigh staying with us quite a lot. Granny was not an easy person. She was very [unclear] and critical and Mummy found her very trying. Daddy was devoted to his mother and could not see any fault in her, which made it harder for Mummy.

The house had a sitting room at the front and a kitchen-living room at the back where we spent most of our time. There was a range in the kitchen for cooking and hot water. Upstairs there were three bedrooms — one for Mummy and Daddy, one for Mary and me, and the little back room for Granny when she came. The [unclear] was very steep and narrow.

I started at the village school when I was five. Mary was already there. It was a Church of England school — quite small — in [unclear] Street. I can remember my first day — Mummy took me and handed me over to Miss Gillard who was the infants' teacher. She was a dear little woman, very kind, and I soon settled in. We sat at long desks — forms, they were called — and we had slates and slate pencils at first. The [unclear] made a horrible squeaking noise on the slate. I learned to read very quickly and was soon moved up. We had a reader called "The Doone Valley Reader" [unclear] which I thought was very funny because our house was called Doone.

At playtime we went out into the playground which was just a piece of tarmac. The boys played on one side and the girls on the other. We played hopscotch and skipping and ball games. I remember the game of [unclear] — you bounced a ball against the wall and had to do various things between bounces — turn round, clap your hands, touch the ground and so on.

Uncle Reg and Auntie Margery used to come over from Exeter with Ann who was a bit younger than me. Ann and I used to play together in the garden or go down to the river to look at the boats and throw stones in the water. Sometimes we would go [unclear] along the river bank looking for wild flowers. Mummy and Auntie Margery would sit and talk and have tea while we played.

Gillian was born while we were at Topsham — in 1935 I think. I remember Mummy getting very large and then one day being told we had a new baby sister. Mary and I were thrilled. She was a lovely baby — very good — and we were allowed to help look after her. I used to rock the pram in the garden to get her to sleep.

Topsham was a lovely place for children. We could go down to the quay and watch the boats — the river Exe was tidal and there were always things going on. There were [unclear] boats and sometimes bigger vessels. At low tide there was thick mud and you could see the wading birds — curlews and herons and all sorts. Daddy told us the names of the birds — he was very knowledgeable about nature. In the summer we would go across on the ferry to the other side of the estuary and have picnics on [unclear]. The ferry was just a small rowing boat and we thought it was a great adventure.

I remember the floods one year — the river came right up and the water came into some of the houses on the Strand. Daddy had to help move things out of the garage to save them. It was very exciting for us children but the grown-ups were very worried. The [unclear] came up the drains and there was a horrible smell.

We were happy in Topsham. It was a close-knit community and everyone knew everyone. But Daddy and Mr Seldon had a falling out — I don't know what about — and Daddy [unclear] had to look for another job. So we had to move on.

## Chapter 9: Meadow Hayes

My parents bought Meadow Hayes when I was seven, so that must have been 1935. It was a thatched cottage in fairly poor condition, standing in about three quarters of an acre. It had no electricity, no mains water, no mains drainage and no gas. It had a well in the garden and its own cess pit. My father had the electricity connected quite soon but we had no mains water for years. The well was tested regularly and the water was excellent — much better than the tap water when it eventually came. I think it was about 1947 before mains water arrived.

The house was built of cob — that is, earth mixed with straw and small stones, built up in layers and then whitewashed. The walls were about two feet thick and kept the house cool in summer and warm in winter. The thatch was old and needed replacing but we couldn't afford to have it done and it was eventually replaced with slates — much to my mother's distress. She felt it had lost its character.

Downstairs there was a sitting room, a dining room, a kitchen and a scullery with a larder off it. The sitting room had a large open fireplace with a bread oven built into the wall beside it. The bread oven was no longer used but was useful for airing things. The dining room also had a fireplace but it was smaller. The kitchen had a range which my mother cooked on — she was never happy with it and longed for an Aga. The scullery had a stone sink and a copper for washing clothes.

There was a hand pump in the scullery which pumped water up from the well. The larder was on the north side of the house and was always cold — it had a stone slab and slate shelves and was where the milk and butter and meat were kept.

Upstairs there were four bedrooms and a bathroom. The bathroom was very basic — a bath with a geyser over it, a basin, and a lavatory. The geyser was temperamental and sometimes refused to light. It made alarming banging noises when it did work. Hot water was always in short supply.

My bedroom was at the front of the house and looked out over the garden towards [unclear] and the fields beyond. Mary and I shared it to begin with but later I had it to myself when Mary moved to the back bedroom. It had a sloping ceiling and a small fireplace which was never lit. The floor was uneven — the old floorboards had warped — and there were draughts round the windows. But I loved it. It was my own place.

The garden was long and narrow, running down to a hedge at the bottom beyond which was one of Grandpa's nursery fields. My mother worked in the garden whenever she could. She grew flowers at the front — roses, sweet peas, delphiniums, lupins — and vegetables at the back. My father grew potatoes and runner beans and made a good job of it. We were largely self-sufficient in vegetables during the war. There were fruit trees — apples, pears and plums — and a damson tree by the back hedge. My mother made jam and bottled fruit every autumn. She also made chutney and pickles. The larder shelves were always full of jars.

There was a chicken run at the bottom of the garden — my father built it — and we kept about a dozen hens. Rhode Island Reds, I think. It was my job to collect the eggs and I rather enjoyed it — the hens were friendly and I liked the warm feel of a newly laid egg. We also kept ducks for a time — Khaki Campbells — but they got into the garden and made a terrible mess so they had to go.

My father was very clever with his hands. He had been trained as a motor engineer at Rolls Royce and could turn his hand to almost anything. He built the garage — a wooden structure with a corrugated iron roof — repaired walls, put up fences, laid paths and was always mending something. He had a workshop in one of the outbuildings where he kept his tools and spent hours there, especially at weekends. He made me a wonderful doll's house one Christmas — it had real windows with glass in them and a front that opened and tiny furniture that he had made himself.

The stable was a stone building with two stalls which stood at the side of the house. It had a hay loft above, reached by a ladder, and a tack room at one end where the saddles and bridles were kept. The tack room always smelt of leather and saddle soap — a lovely smell. Next to the stable was a small paddock, about an acre, which belonged to my grandfather and which he let us use for the ponies. Beyond the paddock were Grandpa's nursery fields, Denver Nurseries, stretching down towards the river.

My first pony was Doris — a grey Welsh pony about 12 hands. She was very steady and reliable and I learned to ride on her. Mary had a pony too — Doris's companion — called [unclear]. We rode every day in the holidays, mostly on the lanes and fields around Topsham. Sometimes we rode down to the river and along the towpath. On special occasions we went further afield — to Woodbury Common or Aylesbeare Common. Ann sometimes came with us on her pony.

There were gymkhanas in the summer — at Topsham, at Woodbury, at [unclear] — and we went to as many as we could. I was never a particularly good rider but I enjoyed it enormously. Mary was much better — she won rosettes and cups.

Gillian was too small for a pony of her own at first but she rode Doris when I had outgrown her. She was a natural rider — much better than me — and was quite fearless.

During the war my father was away a great deal. He worked on aircraft engines — his Rolls Royce training made him valuable — and was posted to various places around the country. My mother ran the house single-handed and did a remarkable job. She coped with rationing, blackout, the anxieties of wartime, and three children, with extraordinary cheerfulness. She never complained — or not in front of us, anyway.

We had an Anderson shelter in the garden — a corrugated iron structure half buried in the ground and covered with earth. My father dug it in before he went away. We didn't use it very often — Topsham was not a major target — but when the siren went we were supposed to go down to it. I remember the damp smell of it and the spiders. My mother hated it and usually kept us indoors unless the raid was close. Exeter was bombed quite badly — we could see the glow of the fires from the garden — but Topsham escaped.

There were soldiers camped in the fields around Topsham in the run-up to D-Day — Americans, I think. They were very friendly and gave us sweets and chewing gum which we had never had before. There were tanks and lorries parked in the lanes and we could hear them practising on the [unclear] at night. Then one day they were all gone and we knew something was happening. That was June 1944.

Life at Meadow Hayes followed a regular pattern. In term time we walked to school — it was about a mile — did homework in the evening, helped in the garden at weekends and rode the ponies when we could. In the holidays we rode every day if the weather was fine, went to gymkhanas, helped with the hens and the garden, and spent as much time as possible out of doors. In the evenings we listened to the wireless — Children's Hour was a favourite — and read. My mother read to us every evening when we were small and we all grew up loving books.

We had very little money. My father's salary was modest and with three children there was not much to spare. We had no car for years — my father cycled to work when he was at home and my mother walked everywhere or took the bus. Clothes were handed down — I wore Mary's things and Gillian wore mine. We didn't mind — everyone was in the same boat during the war and it never occurred to us to feel deprived. We had everything that mattered — a home, a garden, ponies, each other.

Christmas at Meadow Hayes was wonderful. My mother made it magical despite the shortages. The house was decorated with holly and ivy from the garden, and mistletoe which Grandpa brought from the nursery. There was always a tree — a small one from Grandpa's fields — decorated with ornaments that came out year after year, some of them very old. We made paper chains and hung them from the beams.

We hung up stockings on Christmas Eve — usually one of my father's long socks — and found them filled in the morning. An orange in the toe, nuts, a few sweets, small presents. The main presents were under the tree. My mother always knitted something — a jumper or socks or a scarf — and my father made things in his workshop. There were books, always books.

Christmas dinner was the great event — a chicken from our own hens, roast potatoes and Brussels sprouts from the garden, plum pudding which my mother had made weeks before, with silver threepenny bits hidden in it. We all stirred the pudding and made a wish when it was being made.

After dinner we played games — cards, charades, consequences, and a guessing game that Grandpa always won because he cheated. The house was full of people — grandparents, Uncle Reg and Aunt Margery and Ann, and sometimes other [unclear] and friends. It was noisy, warm and happy. Those Christmases are among my happiest memories.

We left Meadow Hayes in 1946 — I was eighteen — when my father got a job [unclear] in the Midlands. My mother didn't want to go. She loved the house and the garden and Topsham and her friends and her family. But my father's work had to come first and there was no arguing with that. We packed up and left and I don't think my mother ever really got over it.

The house was sold to Roy Wheeler. His mother, Mrs Sargent, had done our washing — she was the washerwoman — and had connections with Denver. I heard later that the thatch was replaced and various improvements made. I never went back. I couldn't bear to see it changed.

I still dream about Meadow Hayes. I dream I am walking up the path to the front door and everything is as it was — the thatch, the whitewashed walls, the roses round the door, the smell of the garden. And then I wake up and I am glad and sad at the same time. Glad because for a few moments I was home, and sad because it has gone and I can never go back.

## Chapter 10: Gill and Nellie

I cannot remember exactly when Nelly came to us or where she came from — almost certainly it was an idea to help Gill in some way — ~~she~~ Gill was ill on & off throughout her childhood.

Nelly was a small donkey with a rough pale creamish grey coat & proper donkey marks — a dark line round her ears and a line from her mane to her tail, crossed by a line which ran down her withers. She was gentle and kind and we treated her like a toy. Just occasionally she would bellow during the night, a tremendous noise for such a small animal — I don't think anyone complained.

It was at this time that Gran had a conversation with a friend which made us all laugh when she repeated it later. She told her friend that I was eating breakfast half way up the stairs & Gill was eating hers sitting on the hall floor when Nelly came into the hall. I tried to persuade Nelly to come up stairs but Gran stopped me, in case Nelly couldn't get down again. Gran's friend (probably Bunty Fulford or Mary MacGahey) said that Gran was very unusual in the way she brought us up. Gran couldn't think ~~how~~ why, as she had stopped me taking Nelly upstairs! Every one else at that time was very rigid, meals were always formal, no one else had a donkey in the hall, or eat their breakfast half way up the stairs! Nelly wasn't really allowed indoors but once when someone was coming to tea we tried to persuade Nelly to hide behind the sofa, hoping she would bray & frighten the visitor. We were found out (Nelly had a strong donkey smell, even if Gran hadn't seen her she would have smelled her) — we got into a lot of trouble.

I walked miles leading Nelly when Gill rode her — a very comfortable occupation. Nelly sometimes stopped & wouldn't move — if we waited patiently she started again after about five minutes. Once this habit was very embarrassing — she chose to do one of her stops on the level crossing & a train was due. The signal man wanted to close the gates and shouted to us to move — but we couldn't make her budge. In the end two men from the station (porters, perhaps) came and pushed Nelly so hard she had to move. We didn't take her over the level crossing again.

## Chapter 12: Schools

### Mrs Hall's

When I was four (1932) I went to Miss Challice's Garden School in Exeter, run by Miss Challice and her widowed sister, Mrs Hall. It was unusual, being a long low purpose built school set at the end of a small orchard — the orchard was the end of the garden of a beautiful house in St Leonards. When ever possible our lessons were out of doors, under the trees or on a wide verandah.

I can remember enjoying grammar lessons — sentences were written on a blackboard with no punctuation or capitals — we took it in turns to correct the sentence with beautiful coloured chalks. We played with a 'garden', much like the Britain's garden that was made when you were children though when I was small it was all made of lead. I was driven to the school every morning and fetched before lunch — I did not go to school all day until I was nearly nine.

Mary had gone ahead to Endsleigh, a girls only day school on the corner of Grendon Road.

*Much later on Mary worked as a helper at the Garden School until it was bombed flat during the war. Miss Challice escaped from her house but didn't re-start the school.*

### Endsleigh

There was no uniform. I can remember wearing very warm leather gaiters in the winter, they had dozens of tiny buttons which had to be done up and undone with a button hook. Punishment (in my case probably for being rude) meant standing facing the wall in a corner. I snatched a rubber from a tray of equipment and occupied my time in the corner shredding it into tiny crumbs, and spreading them around my feet.

When I was nearly nine I was sent to Endsleigh. When I joined her there I found having a uniform very exciting. We wore brown box pleated tunics with cream shirts, gold girdles and gold and brown ties. In summer we had yellow checked cotton dresses, brown blazers and horrible panama hats with elastic under our chins. I didn't enjoy school at all — it was a small quiet place (probably not many over thirty children) with kind gentle staff. Miss Lilian Foale was the headmistress, her younger sister Miss Violet taught music and singing, and the seniors were taught by Miss Friend. There was a nursery class with another teacher where there were boys as well as girls.

I didn't mind the lessons but found it crowded and noisy, far too many people. I enjoyed hockey and tennis both taught very well by Miss Friend. It was an encouraging place which I should have enjoyed. I was told I could be in the third eleven hockey team which much later, given the size of the school I realised wasn't much of an achievement!

One event there I have a clear memory of. We had weekly dancing classes taught by Rosa Couldridge. She was a fierce woman who terrified me but one day she went too far, she shouted at Mary and made Mary cry. I couldn't bear to see Mary humiliated so I charged across the room and head butted Rosa. The headmistress was called but I don't remember being in trouble — we didn't go to Rosa's classes again.

When the Germans began sporadic daylight raids on Exeter we left Endsleigh — I was eleven. For a while someone called Connie Jackson came to teach us every afternoon — she ran a nursery school in the mornings.

### Ashford

When the school called 'Ashford' evacuated to Topsham and Countess Wear I found it very exciting. I had been taken away from my small private school in Exeter when that city was subjected to daylight bombing, and was being taught by an old lady who ran a kindergarten in Topsham. She was teaching a group of children between eleven and fifteen whose education had been disorganised by the war. I didn't much like her (though I learned to later) or her lessons and I asked my parents if I could go to Ashford. Most of my friends went to 'The Maynard', a direct grant grammar school in Exeter — very dull, it sounded. The Ashford girls wore a much more exciting uniform than any local school — brilliantly striped blazers in red, navy and grey, strange woolly hats with ear flaps and a button on the top — and best of all, knee socks. All other schools specified woollen stockings and I hated them and the liberty bodice they were kept up by — no stockings, no liberty bodice at Ashford — what bliss.

My mother enquired about places and fees, I had some sort of interview with the headmistress — and everything was settled. The senior school, to which I should belong, was spread over the village of Countess Wear. Two large houses 'Clock' and 'Mount Weir' were boarding houses, we had a barn for our hall and gym and Mill House for classrooms. On the first day of term, 29th April, I dressed carefully in my new uniform, collected my satchel (already packed with everything I might need) and my father and I set off on our bicycles. When we reached Clock House a maid fetched Miss Arnold, the matron, and my father left. Miss Arnold told me to bring my bicycle and satchel and set off to walk to Mill House. She showed me where to leave my bicycle, against the side of the barn, then took me into Mill House. It was an ordinary domestic house with five or six bedrooms and was used just for classrooms. We went upstairs and to a door at the rear of the landing. Miss Arnold explained that I was to join the class, Lower Vb, and left. We had gone into a smallish rather scruffy room containing ten wooden desks and chairs, nine of which were occupied — also a staff table and chair and a blackboard fixed to the wall over the fireplace.

Miss Cumberledge, the form teacher, told me to sit down, asked my name, then proceeded with the business in hand, which was the issuing of replacement exercise books, timetables etc — the usual jobs of a new term. I was given a set of exercise books and text books and packed them quickly into my desk. The child at the desk next to mine was introduced as Joan Turner, form captain, and told to look after me.

When Miss Cumberledge left the room there was a burst of general chatter and I dared to look around. There were two rows of five desks and I sat in the centre of the front row. My first impression gave me a severe shock — they weren't children at all, but grown-ups in school uniform! They all had hairstyles and busts (I had plaits and was quite undeveloped), they talked about boy friends and dances, of which I knew nothing. I felt very young and quite panic stricken. The lessons were difficult to understand and I longed to go back to lessons with Miss Jackson. At the end of the longest day I had ever lived through Miss Cumberledge came to see if I knew what to do about homework. Some of my horror must have shown on my face for she asked what was troubling me. I explained that everyone seemed so clever and so old!

As I had joined the school for the last term of the school year I had been put into a class which I should remain in for the whole of the next school year and I was to use this first term to get used to the school. No one would expect me to cope very well with the work. Also, the children were older than me (mostly fifteen to sixteen), they would go into the Upper V next September and I should remain in the Lower V in company with a younger set of children. Miss Cumberledge said although I was only fourteen, Miss Brake had decided I was bright enough to manage Lower V work — (I decided not to explain that I was only thirteen in case I was sent down a class and had to get used to another room full of girls).

Gradually I got to know the routine, the staff, and some of the girls. The headmistress was Lilian Brake, usually known as Cherub. She had a circular pink face, blue eyes and white hair plaited into a kind of coronet. She talked largely about doing things 'for the sake of the school' and always called us 'gels'! I disliked and mistrusted her — she was known to have favourites and was not good at listening to people. The second in command was 'Piggy Webb', a stumpy rather cross little maths mistress who was strict but liked by the senior girls for being a good listener and unfailingly fair. The French mistress, Miss Flatman, was always angry about something or other, often me! English language taught by Miss Cumberledge was dull, but Miss Churchill made literature exciting. Art and needlework were taught by a faded little French woman, Miss Haffenden — in the loft of a barn in the village. I loved art lessons but needlework was dreadful. Pupils were expected to make for themselves one garment every term. In all the years at Ashford I succeeded in half making one petticoat, which my mother finished off when I left.

I can remember the names of eight of the nine other children in that first term's class. Three others were day girls — Rosemary Gardner came from Topsham on her bicycle every day so we soon got to know one another. Faith came from Exton by bus, and Beryl Loverseed (what a name!) came from the big pub at the Countess Wear roundabout. One boarder, Lesley Atkins, seemed rather lonely but was always kind to me. She came to tea one weekend and seemed to enjoy herself but she seemed too old — sixteen — for me to be really familiar. Three years is an enormous gulf at that age.

When I went back in September most of Lower Vb had become Upper Vb. I remained, as did Rosemary and Faith. The new class coming up from Upper IV still seemed older than I was — but they were a bit nearer in age, mostly about fifteen. I made two particular friends in this group, Marcia Scowcroft and Felix Stephenson, both boarders. There were five day girls and gradually their number was increasing throughout the school.

There was no doubt at all in my mind — once I had settled into the routine at Ashford and the excitement of novelty had worn off — I didn't like the school. The lessons I found mostly enjoyable, especially English and Art and Botany. The other girls — boarders — were very narrow in their interests and inclined to get into tight little sets. I had friends at various other levels in the school — Ann Watkins, a shy little third form girl with whom I cycled to school, and Margaret Soper — older than me, a farmer's daughter from Kenton who I met out riding. Also several day girls in the Lower and Upper fourth forms who belonged to the pony club at Topsham that I helped to run. These friendships were frowned upon by girls and staff alike although to me the only unnatural behaviour seemed the rejection of friendship because these people were not exactly my age.

Games, team games, were one of the worst aspects of Ashford. I didn't have any 'team spirit', I mostly didn't know who was playing who in matches and certainly had no interest in who won.

I had been taught to play hockey properly at Endsleigh and as I could run fast, I had to play on the left wing at Ashford. Netball I never learnt, the games mistress would not believe that I had never been taught, so gave me no tuition but shouted at me whenever I made a mistake. It wasn't until about twenty years later when my daughters were learning to play netball at their prep school that I discovered that running while holding the ball was not allowed. Miss Roberts never once explained, just blew her piercing whistle and called me a fool.

Towards the end of my first winter at Ashford a most convenient accident happened. I was taking my grandfather's cart pony, Nigger, back to his field one evening, and riding bareback so that I should only have the bridle to carry home. Something startled him and he shied violently and threw me into the hedge. I wasn't much hurt — mostly scratched and bruised, but my left foot was uncomfortable. I caught the idiot pony, put him into his field and limped home. Next day my foot was much swollen and I was taken to Exeter for an x-ray. The arch was twisted in some way and the doctor said I must walk as little as possible and on no account do any running, but that riding would not hurt. This doctor's instruction was conveyed to Miss Brake, and I believe was meant to cover perhaps a month or six weeks, but no period was mentioned and I never played hockey or netball again. As the school was very 'games-minded' I managed to leave fairly early most afternoons, and rode instead — it seemed a wonderful escape.

When I was in the Upper V talk was all of 'school cert' of which I had never heard. Various people were very worried about it, others had been promised new bicycles or large sums of money for passing. I spoke to my mother about the pressure and general panic and she told me to please myself, take it or not. This casual attitude shocked my form mistress very much — she said of course I must take it, a pass!

My form mistress at this time was Miss Neal — very young and kind but so steeped in the public school way of thinking that I could hardly communicate with her. One morning as I walked down the drive towards 'Prayers' in the barn she got off her bicycle to walk beside me. 'I've some very exciting news' she said 'you are to be made a prefect this morning!' I was stunned and blurted out 'How horrible, how can I get out of it?' On another occasion our class was having a discussion (very rare, Ashford preferred us to just listen) when Miss Neal said I should do something, I've forgotten what, 'for the sake of the school'. I said that rather than me doing things for the sake of the school, the school should be doing things for me — that's what we paid fees for, and without us, the pupils, it couldn't exist. Very unpopular.

## Chapter 13: Horses and Riding

I was afraid of many things when I was small but never of horses. I was often put on the back of my grandfather's work horses when I was three, and loved it. Sometimes I rode a Shire or Percheron, held firmly by a stable man sitting behind me.

A special treat when we spent days at Exmouth was being allowed a ride on one of Mr and Mrs White's ponies. The ponies gave rides up and down the sea front, not on the sand — Mary came too. Mr and Mrs White seemed very old (probably not) and wore rather rusty looking black riding clothes. One day Mrs White said I sat well on the pony and should have lessons. What bliss — I was taken once a week to a rather shabby farm on the edge of Exmouth and rode for an hour with Mrs White. She taught me how to sit, hold the reins, rise to a trot etc — I should think I was about five or six and loved it.

At about this time my grandfather decided to help me to continue to ride. There was a very elderly work pony, long retired, last ridden by my mother, called Tommy. Tommy was brought out of retirement, provided with a bridle and saddle and I was taken out every weekend by my grandfather. He had a ball of thick cord and tied one end to Tommy's bridle. When I could make Tommy trot the ball of cord was unwound until my grandfather thought I was far enough ahead, then I waited for him to catch up. I loved these rides, the roads were quiet then and my grandfather pointed out flowers, insects etc — we usually went via some of his fields so that he could check on the previous week's work. I loved being alone with him and having his whole attention. The current wagon pony who had replaced Tommy was rather wild and needed a lot of work so some weekends he was harnessed in one of the four wheeled wagons and made to trot from Denver to Blueball — there we turned right into Clyst St Mary, right again through Winslade to Clyst St George, then home along the marsh road, up Bridge Hill and back to the stable. The pony trotted all the way, grandfather drove and I was always the only passenger. The driver's seat across the front of the wagon was obviously designed for men, my feet hardly reached the step so I had to hold on but don't remember feeling any fear.

At some time I think it was decided Tommy should really retire and Sorrel was bought for me, my first 'own' pony. She was a half bred Exmoor about 13 hh, a bay roan and came from Horace Williams at Cotts Farm. She was a real joy, very gentle, safe with traffic and easy to catch. She stood still while I mounted her and was patient while I groomed her. I cannot remember exactly when she was bought but she cost £18 and I should think I was about twelve. By this time Ann had Barney, a small bay Exmoor, and we rode together on every possible day.

I was taught to feel responsible for Sorrel's welfare. One night I left her in the stable because we planned an all day picnic the following day. When my mother came to say goodnight she asked if I had checked the stable water bucket. I hadn't and Sorrel couldn't be left without water. I asked my mother if she would walk across to the stable to fill the bucket but she said "no — my pony, my job." I got up and dressed and found a torch — and my kind mother came with me because she knew I was afraid of the dark. I don't think I forgot again.

It was on Sorrel I learnt to jump, which I loved, she was fast too and would canter and sometimes gallop, specially if we were on Woodbury Common and there was plenty of space. Sadly she developed laminitis, a lameness very difficult to cure — I didn't know what to do because although my grandfather offered me another pony, what should I do with Sorrel. He solved the problem by asking his friend Tom Tozer to find me another pony and provide a retirement home for Sorrel.

I didn't know Tom Tozer and was very much in awe of him, he was a judge (at horse shows, not the legal kind) and bred hunters. His wife suggested I should go to their house at Whimple to meet them and for Tom Tozer to see me ride. I went there by train — I think I was fourteen, very shy of adults I didn't know. Mr Tozer's groom brought out a ready saddled polo pony, bigger than I'd ever ridden, except Shires, and helped me on. I was asked to ride it several times around a huge field near the house, a field sometimes used for gymkhanas. I had to walk, trot, canter, turn etc — the polo pony was beautifully schooled and obedient. When he had seen enough Mr Tozer handed the horse back to his groom and I was taken inside and given tea — a nerve wracking experience.

### Going to West Living Farm

Going to West Living Farm with my cousin Ann, and taking our two ponies, was a great adventure. On the day we were to go, my father went by train from Topsham to Exeter, with our suitcases. We had caught and groomed our ponies the night before and cleaned their harness. Early on the Saturday morning we harnessed the ponies and put on to them any extra tack (headstalls, leading reins, blankets etc), then set off to ride to Exeter. Riding through the city was exciting and as usual, Timothy didn't behave very well, was impatient of traffic lights etc. Most drivers were kind and kept well away from us. We rode down Queen Street, then turned down a little side street which led to a railway yard where goods trains were shunting about. A huge horse box fitted with a groom's compartment and stalls for four horses was waiting in a quiet corner, and an elderly railway man let down the ramp and spread some straw. Predictably, Tim refused to step onto the ramp until shown the way by Barney, who was overcome by greed — there was a loaded hay net waiting for him. When the ponies were safely fastened in, Ann and I put our haversacks (containing picnic lunches) into the groom's compartment, also our suitcases, brought to the siding by my father. There was then a long wait until a train to Barnstaple shunted along and collected the horsebox. I felt excited and frightened — going off in charge of Ann and two ponies. I was sixteen, Ann thirteen.

The journey to Barnstaple, although not a great mileage, seemed quite long — it was such a slow train. In the middle of the afternoon we reached Instow Station and the box was shunted into a siding and unhitched. A rather grumpy porter let down the ramp. He said he didn't like "dealing with cattle" but cheered up when he found that Ann and I would unload our own ponies. Tim was silly and difficult to saddle. We left our suitcases in the porter's care and set off for West Living Farm. When we reached the farm Uncle Will Joslyn (not an uncle at all, my grandmother's cousin, I think) was waiting at the yard gate. He was a small man with thick white hair and a white mustache, wearing a darkish suit and high stiff white collar. He called a farm worker who showed us two empty stalls in a small stable and we took the ponies inside. I checked on the way Ann had tied Barney's head collar rope because he was a notorious escaper. We hung up the harness and went up to the house. Aunty Polly (large, very upright, white hair in a fierce hard bun) welcomed us and said that as our suitcases were at the station she would excuse our breeches at tea time — but not again! Tea was laid in the farm kitchen and was a very satisfying meal — the bread was homemade dough cake — a plainish dough bun with a few currants, made every day by Aunty Polly and served sliced and buttered like bread, at every meal. We were given milk to drink and I found it horrible — it had been dipped from the top of a pan in the dairy and was thick enough to spread.

### Gymkhanas

The first gymkhana I ever went to was held in Winslade Park. I saw no posters or advertisements but was told about it by my cousin Ann. Although I rode Tommy I had no pony of my own. I packed my own picnic lunch (bread, cheese, apple, bottle of water) in a haversack and set off on my bicycle about an hour too early. Never having been to a gymkhana before I was worried about not having enough money to get in, so took all my pocket money — about five shillings. When I reached the gateway in the stone wall I was delighted to find that the charge was sixpence. It was, judged by later standards, a very small and amateurish gymkhana, but I found it very exciting. Ann was there with her Exmoor pony Barney and I knew several other competitors. Echlyn's fat pony was there, looking as slow and obstinate as ever — and many more, exciting grown up people on huge horses, tearing about. At lunch time I was invited to sit with Ann and her family but wouldn't because I didn't trust her mother not to laugh at my picnic. If she did I should have felt this a criticism of my mother — who didn't have time to pack me a picnic because she was always caring for Gillian, who seemed always to be ill. I loved that gymkhana and it made me quite determined to soon be a competitor myself.

A few weeks later I was taken to a gymkhana at Four Firs on Woodbury Common — run by Diana Brook in aid of one of the war time animal charities. It was a more ambitious affair, better run and with more competitors. I began to recognise people and their ponies and to know what the various classes in the schedule meant.

The first gymkhana in which I competed was held in a field near Exminster station and was run by Hazel Longmore. I sent off entries for showing classes, and, greatly daring, a novice jumping competition. Sorrel could jump perfectly well — my problem was staying on top. The day before the gymkhana I spent ages grooming Sorrel and cleaning tack. On the day I took a haversack with grooming things, picnic and a mac, and set off, with Ann on Barney. It was very exciting and I was scared stiff. My Uncle Harry, and Lilian, came on bicycles.

I cannot remember much about the showing classes except that at last I had a competitor's number tied on my arm and felt very important. I do, however, remember the novice jumping class. I waited in the waiting ring with what seemed like several hundred other children on ponies (probably about twenty) most of them as inexperienced as I but probably not all as frightened. When my number was called, towards the end of the class, no child had done a clear round and most had collected many 'faults'. I set off into the ring and Sorrel cantered easily over the jumps, until she came to the last one — a gate. She meant to refuse, stopped suddenly and I shot out of the saddle and up her neck. At the last minute she changed her mind and jumped. I clung on and was still with her, though hanging half under her neck, when she reached the other side. I managed to scramble back into the saddle and found I had won. My Uncle was as excited as I was and immediately rushed off to enter me in the open jumping competition. I was calmer this time and gave Sorrel some help and we came third. Riding home to Topsham at the head of a cavalcade of tired ponies, I felt I'd started on my way to the top. When we reached Denver Road the stableman came out of his cottage, and cheered when he saw my rosettes. My parents and Ann's parents came to congratulate me and to hear all about the gymkhana from Harry and Lilian. I went on to win many more rosettes at bigger shows where the competition was much keener, but I don't think the feeling of elation was ever greater. As far as I was concerned I had joined the gymkhana fraternity and nothing could be better.

## Chapter 14: The Second World War

When adults were talking about and worrying about 'another war' I was eleven and had very little interest. I knew the previous war had been awful, there were so many names on the town war memorial, my mother spoke of it with horror and everyone stood still at 11.0 am on the special day.

The first real worry I had was seeing my mother crying, being comforted by my father — she couldn't believe it was going to happen for a second time, she felt it was madness. Everyone spoke about being 'gassed' because it was used in the first war — my concern was not having a gas mask for my pony!

The impact (on me, on Topsham) was gradual. The first real change was when we left our Exeter day school, Endsleigh, because there had been a few daylight raids on Exeter. The first awfulness came when my father went off to work in North Wales. He was too old for war service but the war had a terrible ~~awful~~ affect on his garage & thus his income — there was almost no private motoring. My father bought a large powerful motorbike and went off to live in digs in a place called Mould or Mold in N Wales. He worked in a place where planes with Rolls Royce engines were repaired — he did the final ground check before pilots flew the planes — very stressful. He came home for occasional weekends, very tired.

In the meantime in order to generate more income we moved to Denver (my grandparents house) and our house was let. I didn't much mind but it must have been very difficult for my mother, she and Gran had never got on very well & Gran was very bossy. Mary and I were fifteen ~~fourteen~~ & eleven and for one term had lessons every afternoon with an ancient witch called Connie Jackson. When I was ~~much~~ older I got to know her and like her very much! After a term she gave up — she found Mary unteachable — she didn't know about dyslexia and Mary could barely read or write — I just behaved badly.

I enjoyed the freedom this change brought, no school uniform or awful games — hockey & tennis — but was probably a nuisance. Exeter was being sporadically bombed so we never went there but were free to do what we wanted at home. When we became too much of a nuisance Miss Jackson agreed to have us at her nursery school in Elm Grove Road — she had a group of older children waiting for places in secondary schools, or just disrupted, as we were. Richard Gardner was there, two girls called Susan Adams & Kippers (never knew her real name) and a few others I've forgotten. We did maths (taught by a strange but interesting woman called Sue Pazoltz). She also taught botany/biology and art — she took a group of us, by bus, to Woodbury Common & showed us sundew plants eating insects — she also took us to a roller skating rink in Exmouth — don't know what subject that was, but I loved her. At other times Miss Jackson tried to teach us poetry — a lost cause. I often helped her with her small pupils (age about three to six or seven) I was used to looking after Gillian and was good at telling simple stories. I told stories if we had to sit for long in the dank dark underground air raid shelter. Much later when I was sixteen my mother was Miss Jackson's assistant. When my mother had to go away to I think Yorkshire, because Mary was ill there, I daringly offered to voluntarily help Miss Jackson. She accepted and I worked with her older children for several weeks and really enjoyed myself, and got to know and like her very much.

After a while — I've no idea how long, my father came back from Wales and we moved back to our own house. Then ~~Before this~~ Exeter was blitzed and that was very frightening. Our house shook & shuddered, we all crouched in our home made air raid shelter and in what was probably a very shakey voice, I told Gill stories and held her on my lap — she dozed and cried, she didn't like the bangs — we didn't either!

The next morning Mary set off on her bicycle to go to the Garden School where she was an assistant. The Garden School was a purpose built kindergarten at the end of Miss Challice's (headmistress) garden and orchard, in St Leonards. Mary had to push her bike round bomb craters, there was very little left standing and when she got to where the school had been there was nothing. Fortunately Miss Challice and her sister had been in their shelter and were unhurt. Mary came home much shaken and very upset. She said the road from Exeter to Topsham was full of people pushing carts or prams with the few belongings they had saved, hoping to find shelter.

The WVS provided food and then asked everyone with a spare bed to offer it to a bombed out person. Mary & Gill & I moved into one bedroom and into Mary's room we took an old lady who was bed ridden. The bedroom was at the back of the house and she loved watching us play and ride our ponies outside her window. After a while her house was repaired but she was so comfortable she wanted to stay. My mother had had quite enough of going upstairs dozens of times a day and didn't offer to keep her.

The blitz was very sobering, I was older and began to take in the horribleness of what was going on. Pupils at Ashford, several of them families of rich European Jews, had missing relatives — we didn't know about the death camps then.

My grandfather was struggling to grow as much food as possible. Before the war he employed thirty men, with dozens of women and girls extra during the fruit season. Now his men were all in the forces and he said he was expected to double his output with 'only pensioners and idiots' left to employ. He had Italian prisoners for a while — when he found they were members of a famous Italian orchestra he provided them with gloves to work in so that their hands shouldn't be damaged. He said they were charming and the laziest men he had ever dealt with. Next he had some German prisoners — they were rather sullen but very hard working — much more use to him. Several were farmers or farmers sons and were worried about their land and who was working it, also frightened of what was happening to their families, particularly those on the Russian side of Germany.

Life was different — if you needed something you couldn't go and buy it, mostly you had to make it. We made & remade clothes, and toys for Gill were all home made. Shoes were difficult to get & warm coats. My mother made me a lovely dressing gown out of an old blanket, I was still wearing it (in bed on cold nights) when I was a student at Bristol. This 'making' applied to tools, furniture, just about everything. Skirts were let down or had bits added on, jerseys were carefully unpicked and reknitted — perhaps two small ones reknitted to make a larger one, with stripes! Everything was valuable, saved, reused, given to someone who could use it. By now my father worked at Rowe Bros, builders merchants in Exeter and he was always very pleased if he persuaded a 'traveller' or 'agent' to give him something difficult to get. I can remember everyone laughing when one of his surprise presents was a large pack of loo rolls. We had become used to using cut up squares of whatever softish paper was available — usually the Radio Times.

Food must have been very difficult though my mother was inventive and we certainly didn't go short. Various relatives with farms gave us butter & rarely, cream, when we visited. We kept hens so there were eggs (they were fed on boiled veg peelings & a small ration of hen food) this another job for my mother. We also eat the hens when they were too old to lay. We were never short of fruit and veg — we grew quite a lot where our back lawn had been. I think I missed cheese most — it was already my favourite food & the weekly ration was very small.

All this time I was riding, first on Tommy, a wagon pony brought out of retirement by my grandfather for me to learn on, then Sorrel, a very gentle Exmoor — later, when I was about thirteen I had Timothy, a difficult show pony. They were all fed on grass or hay; oats and other feed was rationed and kept for the working horses. There were gymkhanas & shows, all very local, few owners could get petrol for transport — this meant I could win quite a few prizes, the competition wasn't very hot, though I didn't realize it at the time, I thought I was good at it!

I remember a few specific events. We were bombarded with propaganda about spies, about not talking to strangers. On one occasion Ann and I were asked by a man we didn't know for directions to Exeter. We gave him careful directions to Exmouth in the opposite direction and felt we had done our bit for the war effort.

Once while picnicking with our families near some woods we heard some mysterious tapping noises — spies sending messages? We set off to investigate and finally arrived at the source of the messages — a very busy woodpecker.

Much more serious was when I was cycling to school (Ashford, at Countess Wear) when a plane above began to machine gun. There were two of us, we rode our bicycles right into a wide dry ditch and kept still, too frightened to move. I think the plane was probably a stray, returning from a raid, just firing at something further up the road. When it had gone we dusted ourselves, got our bikes out & rode on to school, both a bit shakey about our knees and a bit scratched, but otherwise all right. I don't remember telling anyone about it at the time, a bit ashamed of diving into the ditch I think, or of being accused of making it up.

School (Ashford to which I went when I was thirteen, nearly fourteen) was also make do in many respects. We wrote on paper in pencil then rubbed it out & wrote on the rather furry surface in ink. In needlework classes we made underwear for ourselves and learnt to mend. Outings, very important to boarders who could rarely go home, were somewhere reached on foot. We once walked on the canal bank to Turf and had a picnic. We also went fruit picking at St Bridget's nurseries — like my grandfather's nursery they were turned over to food production. Games matches were played against local schools. We were doing 'The Tempest' for school cert and were taken to see an excellent production by the Maynard School, they really brought the play to life for me.

Towards the end of the war when most air raids had stopped some things began to get nearer normal — but not Exeter — I could hardly believe what I was seeing, acres and acres with almost nothing standing, just rubble. Also at this time food rationing became the fiercest yet and just as the war ended it got even worse. There was a bit of half hearted ~~hale~~ rejoicing when war ended but too many people were dead, too many homeless — and almost everyone was short of food. Apart from being no longer under attack there wasn't much to 'party' about.

I was very fortunate in losing no close relatives during the war ~~altho~~ although just at the end of the war my very dear cousin Edward died of an illness contracted during service in the merchant navy in the Far East. When he was growing up we sometimes spent time together but not much — he didn't ride and we hadn't much in common. He was the only child of Joslin ~~Joslyn~~ (Gran's eldest brother) and Eunice, and when he joined the merchant navy Eunice told me one day that he would like letters. I wrote to him via an address in London and rarely knew where he was — he hardly ever came on leave and when he did he was very silent. He also wrote very rarely, saying little, but I liked writing so just kept on.

When he died his parents were not surprisingly shattered and within a year Joslin died. This completely unbalanced Eunice, who became an alcoholic. She told my mother she had left everything she had to me, her favourite niece. As she became more dependant on alcohol she sold her house & moved into lodgings. Her landlady looked after her very kindly, right to the end of Eunice's life — we then found she had changed her will & left what little remained to her landlady, which seemed quite right.

Denver, my grandparents, took in four East End evacuees, Kate (14) & her sister Olive (10) Coates, Rita Wiefel (14) and Doris Clark ~~can't remember her second name~~. Kate was loud & stroppy and very cockney, Olive, mostly called Lolly was miserable & naughty, Rita was blonde with a huge bust, quiet & laid back, Doris was small, dark & quiet. My grandmother gave up on Olive & after a few weeks she was moved to a small terrace house with working class people where she settled much more happily. The other three stayed quite a while and gradually got used to Denver. They were given their own sitting room and generally got on by looking after themselves. Half Topsham children seemed to develop cockney accents — I expect the cockney children learnt quite a bit of Devonshire. After the war most of them came back to visit — Kate until she last came with two small children. I found the evacuees interesting, I can remember sharing a bedroom with Kate & Doris when my mother was ill at one time — I was younger than they were & they were kind, always used to mothering children younger than themselves. Mary didn't like them & they knew this and didn't react well — they thought her 'stuck up and snobby'!

We were interested in the way they talked and they thought we talked like people on the wireless — most peculiar. They also found it odd that we were privately educated, never swore, mostly did as we were told, and knew about animals. They were terrified by the work horses, never went near the stables and thought cows & pigs were very peculiar indeed. They had only ever seen them dead in butchers shops. Mostly they were far more sophisticated than we were, talked about the relationships between their parents, about boy friends etc — we were miles behind them in these respects. I think they were more flexible and independent. If I'd been thrust into a strange family, different environment, different class, I'm sure I shouldn't have survived and coped like they did.

Since I've grown old and sometimes doubt my memory I've wondered if I imagined being 'machine gunned'. I wish I could ask the other girl who was with me what happened. Almost certainly we were not the object of the attack — the road in those days was lined with big trees which overhung the road, we couldn't have been seen from above. The incident ~~certainly~~ happened but I doubt we were anywhere near the actual gunfire. I'm trying to be accurate!

Much later I learned that during 1940 when my father was working in Wales and invasion seemed imminent and inevitable, my mother slept with a loaded revolver under her pillow. When I said she couldn't have kept the German army away with a revolver she said 'It wasn't for the Germans, it was for you'. Information from occupied countries about what German soldiers did to women & children had made her decide she would kill us & then herself rather that let us be subjected to the horrors that were reported. It made me feel quite sick when she told me and also made me realise how really dreadful ~~awful~~ the war was for parents — what an awful thing to have even thought about. It still makes me shudder all these years later at the thought of what my parents went through and yet they managed to give us a relatively carefree and happy childhood. I am full of admiration for them.

Our GP went into the army and was replaced by a man who to me seemed very old — probably too old for active service, but that didn't mean old! Gillian was often ill so he came often to the house (no NHS then) and he and Gillian became friends. He told her stories or rhymes to make her laugh and I can still remember one — he told her about a fat woman who when she hurried up a hill ~~took~~ "takes off her coat & pants". Gill was quite a little girl & when she saw the double meaning of the sentence thought it very funny. His name was Carmody, he was a kind man.

## Chapter 15: The War Years and Family Life

On Sept 3rd 1939 we all gathered round the wireless to hear the Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain tell us we were at war with Germany. This was no surprise as the war clouds had been gathering for some time. We had already been issued with gas masks and Anderson shelters were being erected in many gardens. At school we had been having air raid practices — filing into the shelters in an orderly manner. I was eleven and had just started at Maynard School in Exeter.

Daddy was too old to be called up — he was 44 — but he joined the Home Guard and spent many nights on duty. He was also an ARP Warden. Mummy was a member of the WVS. Life at first went on much as before — we had the blackout of course and rationing was introduced but somehow Mummy always managed to produce good meals.

We had an Anderson shelter in the back garden, half sunk into the ground with earth piled on top. It was damp and cold and we didn't use it very much. When the air raids started we used to go under the stairs. We had a Morrison shelter as well — a large steel table which we had in the dining room. We used to sleep under it when the raids were bad.

The raids on Exeter were terrible. We could see the glow of the fires from Topsham. The worst was the blitz of May 1942 when the centre of the city was devastated. The beautiful Cathedral was damaged and many fine old buildings were destroyed. Bedford Circus and much of the High Street were flattened. The Maynard School was also badly damaged and we had to have lessons in various temporary buildings around the city.

Many people were made homeless and Mummy helped with the WVS feeding centres. I remember going with her once and being very upset at the sight of people who had lost everything.

Daddy was out all night during the raids helping with rescue work. He came home exhausted and filthy and Mummy would have hot water ready for him. He never talked about what he had seen.

The Americans arrived in Devon in the build up to D-Day. There were camps all around — soldiers everywhere. We were fascinated by them — they were so different from our own soldiers, with their smart uniforms and easy manner. They had things we hadn't seen for ages — chewing gum, candy, nylon stockings. "Got any gum, chum?" was the cry of every child. They were very generous and would throw sweets to us from their trucks.

Mary was seventeen by then and very pretty. Mummy kept a close eye on her! But the Americans were mostly very well mannered and polite. Some of them came to tea and I remember them being amazed at how little we had. One of them said "Ma'am, how do you manage?" and Mummy said "We manage very well, thank you."

Daddy had the garage going all through the war. Petrol was rationed of course but there was always work to be done — keeping essential vehicles on the road. He was very clever at improvising when spare parts were unobtainable. He used to say that the war made him a better engineer because he had to use his brain instead of just ordering new parts.

The Home Guard took up a lot of his time. He was very proud of his unit and took it all very seriously. They trained hard and Daddy said they would have given a good account of themselves if the invasion had come. He had his rifle and I remember him cleaning it at the kitchen table.

We had an allotment as well as the garden and grew as much as we could. We kept chickens — it was my job to feed them and collect the eggs. I hated it when we had to kill one for the table but the meat was [unclear] welcome. We also kept rabbits for a while but Gill and I got too fond of them and cried when they were killed so Daddy gave them up.

Mummy was a wonderful manager. She could make a meal out of almost nothing. She made jam from whatever fruit was available — rosehip syrup, elderberry wine. She bottled fruit, made pickles and chutney. Nothing was wasted. I remember her making a cake with grated carrot and potato when there were no eggs to be had. She used to swap eggs from our chickens for other things — there was quite a barter system going on.

We were lucky in Topsham because we could get fish. The fishermen still went out and we could get herrings, mackerel and sometimes plaice. Mummy would cycle down to the quay to buy fish straight off the boats.

School life went on despite everything. After the school was damaged we had lessons in all sorts of places — church halls, private houses. I remember having French lessons in someone's front room. It was all very makeshift but the teachers carried on magnificently. I was doing well at school and had a particular love of English and History. Miss [unclear] was my English teacher and she was a great influence on me. She encouraged me to read widely and I devoured everything I could get my hands on.

The library was a great solace during the war. I read my way through all the classics. Jane Austen, the Brontës, Dickens, Thomas Hardy — I loved them all. I also read a great deal of poetry and could recite long passages from memory. I still can.

We didn't have a television of course — nobody did during the war — but we had the wireless and I loved it. ITMA with Tommy Handley was our favourite — the whole family would gather round to listen. And the news — we always listened to the nine o'clock news. I can still hear the voice of Alvar Lidell reading the news. When things were going badly the tension in the room was terrible. Mummy would be knitting furiously and Daddy would sit very still with his pipe.

Music was important too. We had a gramophone and some records — Daddy loved classical music, especially Beethoven. Mummy preferred lighter music — musical comedies and so on. Mary was mad about the big bands — Glenn Miller, Joe Loss. I liked everything.

We used to go to the pictures in Exeter when we could. I loved the cinema and saw all the big films of the war years — Mrs Miniver, In Which We Serve, Casablanca. The newsreels were often frightening but we felt we had to watch them to know what was happening.

Ann used to come and stay with us quite often. She and I were the same age and great friends. Uncle Reg was in the Navy and Auntie [unclear] worried about him all the time. Ann was a cheerful girl though and we had great fun together. We used to cycle all over the place — down to the estuary, along the canal, out into the countryside. The lanes were empty of traffic because of petrol rationing and we felt very free.

I remember VE Day — May 8th 1945. The relief was tremendous. We went into Exeter and there were crowds everywhere, singing and dancing in the streets. Somebody had a piano out on the pavement and people were dancing to it. There were flags and bunting everywhere — goodness knows where people had been hiding them! We all went to the Cathedral for a service of thanksgiving. I remember standing there with tears streaming down my face — tears of relief and thankfulness but also sadness for all those who would not be coming home.

Daddy opened a bottle of something — I think it was whisky — that he had been saving. Even we girls were given a small glass. Mummy cried. She had been so brave all through the war and now it was over she just let go.

But of course the war with Japan went on and it wasn't until August that it was truly over. VJ Day was celebrated too but by then we were all rather exhausted with celebrating.

The aftermath of the war was in some ways harder than the war itself. Rationing went on for years — some things didn't come off the ration until 1954. The country was exhausted and broke. But there was a tremendous feeling of hope and determination to build a better world. The 1945 election swept Labour into power with a huge majority and I remember Daddy being rather shocked — he was a [unclear] Conservative. But the welfare state and the National Health Service were wonderful things and even Daddy had to admit that.

I was seventeen when the war ended and about to start my last year at school. The war had shaped my childhood and youth in ways I didn't fully understand at the time. We had learned to manage with very little, to be resourceful, to help each other. We had learned that ordinary people could be extraordinarily brave. And we had learned not to take anything for granted.

### Family Life

I want to go back now and write about our family life in more detail. I have written about the war but there is so much more to tell.

Daddy was the most important person in my young life. He was a big man — well over six feet — with large hands that were always stained with oil from the garage. He was gentle and kind and had a wonderful sense of humour. He could fix anything. People used to bring all sorts of things to him — not just cars but lawnmowers, clocks, wirelesses, anything mechanical. He never turned anyone away and often didn't charge for his time.

He had been trained at Rolls Royce — he was very proud of that — and he had the highest standards of workmanship. He used to say "If a job's worth doing, it's worth doing well" and he meant it. I never saw him do a sloppy piece of work.

He loved music — as I have said — and he loved books. He was a self-educated man in many ways. He had left school at fourteen but he had read widely and could talk about almost anything. He had a particular interest in history and science. He was fascinated by how things worked — not just machines but the natural world too. He used to take us for walks and point out the birds and the wild flowers and the geological features. He knew the names of everything.

Mummy was different. She was small and neat and very practical. She was a wonderful cook and housekeeper — the house was always spotless and there was always something good to eat. She was not a great reader but she was very intelligent in a practical way. She could sew beautifully — she made most of our clothes — and she was a skilled gardener.

She had been Edith Irene Pyne before her marriage and came from a [unclear] Devon family. Her brother Reg — Uncle Reg to us — was the one in the Navy. She also had a sister — Auntie [unclear] — who lived in [unclear]. Mummy was very close to her family and we saw a lot of the Pyne relations.

Mummy and Daddy were devoted to each other. They rarely argued and when they did it was over quickly. Daddy would give in with good grace — he used to say "Your mother is always right" with a twinkle in his eye. They were a good team.

Mary was four years older than me and I looked up to her enormously. She was pretty and clever and good at everything. She was head girl at school and won prizes for everything. I was always "Mary Leigh's little sister" which was sometimes tiresome but I was very proud of her. She was kind to me — not all big sisters are — and she used to help me with my homework.

Gill — Gillian — was four years younger than me so I was the middle one. Gill was the baby of the family and perhaps a little spoiled. She was a lively child, always getting into scrapes. She was the naughty one — Mary was the clever one and I was the good one, or so the family mythology had it. In fact we were all a mixture of everything.

Our house in Topsham was a comfortable old place. It had a big kitchen where we lived most of the time, a sitting room which was kept for best, and a dining room where we had the Morrison shelter during the war. Upstairs there were three bedrooms and a bathroom. Mary and I shared a room and Gill had the small room. The garden was long and narrow with fruit trees at the end and Daddy's vegetable patch.

Topsham itself was a lovely place to grow up. It was a small town on the estuary of the Exe, with old merchants' houses along the Strand and narrow lanes running up the hill. We could walk to the river in five minutes and we spent hours there, watching the boats and the birds. The estuary was full of wildlife — herons, curlews, oystercatchers — and at low tide we would go out on the mud flats. We knew all the fishermen and they would sometimes take us out in their boats.

The town had a strong sense of community. Everyone knew everyone else and people looked out for each other. This was especially true during the war. If someone was in trouble the neighbours rallied round. Mummy was always taking food to people who were ill or had had a bereavement. It was what you did.

We went to church every Sunday — St Margaret's — and the church was the centre of the community in many ways. There were fêtes and jumble sales and whist drives. I was in the choir and sang there for years. Daddy didn't come to church — he said he could talk to God in the garden — but Mummy was a regular attender and we children had no choice in the matter.

I look back on my childhood with great happiness despite the war. We had so little by today's standards but we didn't feel deprived. We had love and security and a sense of belonging. We had the river and the countryside and the freedom to roam. We had books and music and each other. It was, I think, a very good childhood.

# Eulogy

*Written and delivered by Jago Silver.*

I wanted to say a few things about Anthea.

I recently found a letter from the medical director of a London clinic, written to Anthea's parents in 1931, it reads:

> "You will be interested to know that actual figures worked out from Anthea's test last Saturday show that she is not only high, but unusually high in intelligence, her mental development being slightly in excess of that normal for a child of six years."

Anthea was only three at the time… it continues…

> "This is undoubtedly a very big factor in her present, rather trying behaviour, but her future brilliance will amply repay you for any trouble you may experience while she is very young."

78 years later I think we would all agree she certainly was brilliant.

The Anthea I remember was always full of stories… one of my favourites regards her dislike of dogs (something we share) …I recall her telling me how once at a play park she placed her foot under an annoying sausage dog and flicked it through the air away from a small grandchild that it was scaring.

She definitely had a rather mischievous side and was also fiercely independent…

She told me how in her male-dominated architecture school one particularly prejudiced lecturer liked to criticise weak drawings by calling them feminine and was convinced that female students could not compete. During a group criticism he then praised her own work as a fine example of a strong masculine drawing — without realising that she had produced it. She said she had great pleasure in claiming the work as her own and watching him eat his own words.

Anthea always seemed knowledgeable about absolutely everything, and there seemed to be no topic that did not hold some interest for her. I loved having long rambling conversations with her, covering everything from the Internet and computers to politics, books or art. She was always genuinely interested in what you had to say and was always ready to listen to you.

She was ridiculously modest about her artistic abilities, indeed often to the point of cutting up what we might all have considered masterpieces, often turning them into birthday cards, bookmarks or envelopes for vouchers. I have been looking through the enormous collection of artwork she left behind and I am utterly staggered at her exceptional talent for drawing and painting. She was never satisfied with her work and was constantly striving to be better at it, something I find truly inspiring.

When I think of Anthea I cannot help but think of her meticulous organisation and the almost obsessive way that she carefully reused everything that could possibly be reused. In fact she was so efficient at recycling anything she no longer needed that we often feared we would find some family heirloom neatly sawn up into a box of kindling.

Anthea always seemed a very calm and peaceful person, unfailingly kind and welcoming too, always thinking of other people and ready to help in any way she could. She had a healthy disrespect for authority and admired individualism. Anthea was the centre of our family, our one common relation. I feel privileged to have known her for 29 years. I will miss her terribly but I will smile whenever I think of her.
