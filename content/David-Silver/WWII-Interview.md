*← [David Silver](David-Silver/) · [Silver Family Archive](./)*

In about **June 1994**, at the age of seventy, [David Silver](David-Silver/) sat down with a tape recorder and a list of questions about the Second World War from his grandson Jago, then fourteen, who needed them for a school project. He reads each question aloud and answers it, then goes on to the questions he thinks "I ought to have been asked". The tape begins at question 4, so his first three answers are lost, and it stops mid-thought where the side ran out.

He talks about the convoys and his brother Christopher's landings in North Africa and Italy, and the night in the **Exeter Blitz** when he was most frightened, when the bombing "made my knees tremble". He takes his whole RAF career in order, from the night-fighter medical that kept him on the ground to his work as an **engine fitter** on Fortresses, Mosquitoes, Liberators, Spitfires and Wellingtons. Along the way come train journeys through air raids, his kit bag ("I still have got mine. It's in the garage"), the Baedeker raids, and a flight **round Cologne Cathedral a few days after VE Day, about forty feet below the top of the spires**. He ends with why the war shaped the rest of his life: "This hasn't been history for me. For me this has been what happened; for you it's history."

His letters home from each station are on the [Letters from the RAF](./#letters-from-the-raf) pages.

## Question 4 — How did the war affect transport? `[00:00]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-01.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

**Air.** Well, as far as I know there wasn't any civilian air transport at all. There might have been, but I doubt it very much. I think it was confined to military use, and I think that the only civilians who might have travelled about would have been people like the Prime Minister. I don't think there were any civilian airlines running in England. There had been a few, probably to Manchester and Edinburgh before the war, but nothing much other than that — and of course overseas, to Paris and further. But during the war, as the war started, all civilian airlines virtually stopped.

**Sea transport.** `[01:06]` Well, there wasn't any sea transport when it comes to private individuals being transported anywhere in England, unless they were doing something because of the war — in which case, if they were civilians, they possibly travelled across to France before it was overrun by the Germans in 1940, but not any further; or to Ireland.

But for soldiers, sailors, airmen and merchant seamen, they travelled of course by sea. The various stages of the war required them — for instance, as you know, from D-Day — to return to the continent, and in that case you know that they travelled in landing craft and in transport ships and so on. My brother, for instance, went to North Africa and landed in Philippeville [now Skikda, Algeria] in a transport. And then he was transferred again to Italy, to southern Italy, and there he landed in landing craft. And so there was sea transport, but it was for the soldiers, the Air Force and the Navy, largely.

And of course, when you talk about sea transport, there was the practically permanent danger of being attacked. I mean, I went to the Isle of Man just when the war ended [1946], went out of Liverpool on a ship, and we passed all the anti-aircraft gun batteries mounted on stilts in the entrance to the river [the Mersey], to defend the ships in the port against air raids. And once you were out after that — during the war, but not for me on that occasion — they were subject to attack by submarines. And also, before the Germans lost supremacy of the air, which happened in the last year of the war really, about 1944 and '45, they were liable to dive-bomb you or attack you from the air. But they certainly were very likely to try to torpedo you. And of course there was always the danger that the German Navy, with pocket battleships as they were called, would be patrolling somewhere and you would be sunk by them too.

The ships travelled in convoy, as it was called, and in fact they travelled to America in convoy. A number of ships would sail together, merchant ships, and they would be accompanied by warships to defend them. And then if there was an attack while they were crossing the Atlantic or anywhere else — and this happened to troopships too — they were defended by warships: battleships, cruisers and destroyers. We didn't hear a lot about the details of this, because obviously they didn't want the Germans to know any more than [necessary]; there was no need for us to know. So we didn't really know where the ships had been sunk. Though of course the people who were on them — their families were told if they'd been lost at sea. So it did get around that people had lost their brothers, sisters, husbands, whoever, when they'd been crossing.

And for me, at school, right at the beginning of the war — as I probably told you before — there was a boy who was evacuated to Canada, because there were a number of people who sent their children to Canada at the beginning of the war. He was evacuated to Canada at the beginning of the war, but he very, very quickly reappeared at school again, because his ship, which was called the *Athenia*, was torpedoed in the Channel. [The *Athenia* was sunk on 3 September 1939, the first day of the war, north-west of Ireland, on her way to Montreal.] They had to take to the boats and they were rescued and they were brought back to England. He came back to school the next term. So it didn't always happen like that, of course, but he was one of the lucky ones.

## Question 6 — Do you remember when you were most scared? `[05:58]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-02.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

Probably, although I was in the RAF and I was scared in the RAF, I was probably the most scared before I went into the RAF, when the bombing was taking place at Exeter. I think I'm right in saying that the worst bombing was on the Sunday, the 3rd of May 1942. I think that's correct. It could be '41 or it could be '42 — you'll have to look in that book *Exeter Phoenix* to find out, but the dates are right anyway. [It was 1942: the main Baedeker raid on Exeter was the night of Sunday 3 – Monday 4 May 1942, after smaller raids on the nights of 23–24 and 24–25 April.]

Sunday May the 3rd was the worst raid, and I had just gone back to school in Somerset [King's School, Bruton]. But I didn't go till the Saturday, which was the 2nd. And so I was at home on Friday the 1st of May, and during that time the Germans bombed Paris Street and completely removed it from existence, and various other bits.

And I know I was what was called an air-raid warden — my father was an air-raid warden — and we had to patrol the street. And I know I was very proud of the fact that I could recognise aircraft, I thought — which I later could do very much better than I could at that time. And I recognised an aeroplane as being an English aeroplane, a British aeroplane, an Avro Anson. It wasn't at all; it was a Heinkel 111, and it dropped a whole lot of bombs just after it passed over us.

And I can remember incendiary bombs being dropped on our road where we lived [West Avenue], and they made a pattern in the pavement. They were about the size of large bottles, sort of fizzy lemonade bottles, and they went straight through the pavement — burnt through it and drilled a hole, and smoke came up out of the pavement. But what they were meant to do was to come down on buildings and set them on fire. Very fortunately for us, they dropped them in a straight line up the road instead of dropping them in a straight line up the houses in the road.

But the bombing that went on while we were inside, when they dropped them on Paris Street, made my knees tremble while I sat there, I can remember. So that's why I think I was the most scared then. And in that case they did succeed in destroying part of the barracks, full of the army, which was about 300 yards away from our house. So I had good reason to be frightened. But I was also very scared in the RAF during those attacks. People very often were.

## Question 7 — Did you ever consider what might happen if Germany won the war? `[08:52]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-03.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

No, I never considered what might happen if Germany won the war. I knew what had happened in France and Belgium and Denmark and Holland and Norway and practically everywhere except Sweden and Switzerland, and I knew that it would be a terrible thing if they won the war. But I never considered it as being possible that they would win the war, because, as far as I was concerned, if they ever set foot in this country, every single person in this country would fight until they were killed — and that included me. And we wouldn't leave them anything to be conquerors of except the land, and we would do everything that we possibly could to destroy them. And if they just killed us all, well, they'd kill us all. But we hadn't got the slightest intention of surviving it if we weren't going to win.

## Question 8 — Where were you based? `[09:57]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-04.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

Well, that's quite a lot of places. I joined the RAF on, let's see, March 21st 1942, to go into the aircrew, and I was deferred until October 1943 because they couldn't train the aircrew fast enough. And when they finally — I got to the point where I was going to be in a night fighter — they changed their medical standard, and so I couldn't go into the night fighter. So I had to be what you call remustered: that is to say, to decide what to do with me on the ground. And they sent me to a place called Penarth in South Wales in order that they could do that. And at that point they told me that I could go into the aircraft engineering side of the RAF, on the ground, and so that's exactly what I did.

And the first place I had to go to was a place to be generally trained. So I went to Cardington, which is the place where the two airships that were made in this country, the R100 and the R101, were made long before the war, and it was still an RAF aerodrome. And I went there and I was equipped with uniform and everything like that.

And then I was sent on to Skegness, which was on the extremely cold east coast of England, and there I was supposed to be trained for eight weeks in general drill and various things like using a machine gun and that kind of thing, and also made to be a lot more physically fit. Well, the first thing that happened was that I had an injection against various illnesses, and was myself ill as a result of the injection, so I had to stay an extra four weeks. So I was there twelve weeks in all.

And then I was posted to a place called Little Horwood, which was in Hertfordshire I think, and near Hitchin [Little Horwood is in Buckinghamshire, near Winslow; it is Henlow that is near Hitchin], and I stayed there for a very few weeks learning a little bit — I beg your pardon, that isn't correct. I was posted not to that place, [but] to RAF Henlow near Wolverhampton [he means RAF Cosford, and corrects himself later], where I was trained for six months — that's right — to become a flight mechanic. And when I'd become a flight mechanic at the end of the six months, I was sent to this place, Little Horwood, in order to work on aeroplanes. But I didn't stay there for more than three weeks, because I was moved again.

And fortunately for me — I was very glad — I was moved to RAF Oulton, 14 miles from Norwich in Norfolk, where there were three separate units. There was a training unit for training aircrew; there was a unit which was No. 204 [214] Squadron, Bomber Command; and there was an American unit, which was also a bomber unit. [At Oulton: No. 214 Squadron, 100 Group, flying Fortresses; No. 1699 (Fortress Training) Flight; and the USAAF 803rd Bomb Squadron.] And I was attached to what they called the conversion unit. The conversion unit converted pilots from British aeroplanes to being able to fly American aeroplanes. And we had B-17 bombers — they were Boeing Flying Fortresses — and we had to keep them running in order that the pilots could be trained on the American aircraft. And we had been trained, of course, on both British and American aircraft for this purpose. And so we tested them, maintained them, took them to pieces, kept them there, and did everything to them that was necessary to ensure that they were able to go up in the air whenever it was necessary. And I was there for a year, I think, certainly until 1945.

And in 1945 the end of the war came in Europe, and VE Day, and I was posted. I was sent to Scotland, to Leuchars near Dundee, where I had to work on Mosquitoes. And from there I was posted again to Oakington in Cambridgeshire, where I had to work on Liberators, which are Consolidated B-24 bombers — American again. The Mosquitoes were de Havilland — English, British.

So from the bombers I went on to the Isle of Man [RAF Andreas, then Jurby], where I worked on Spitfires, Seafires, Wellingtons and so forth. And from there I went to a place near Leamington Spa [RAF Wellesbourne Mountford], which is now an experimental station for agriculture, where I worked on all sorts of other aircraft. And then I came out, looking for some new work.

## Question 9 — What was your job? `[15:24]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-05.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

Well, I told you that earlier. I should have told you that I actually did further training, and went on from becoming a flight mechanic to becoming a fitter — an engine fitter — which meant you could do anything you liked to an engine: take it to pieces to the tiniest bit, any huge aircraft engine. And I worked on 2,000-horsepower [Bristol] Centaurus engines and that kind of thing, on Rolls-Royce Merlin engines, and I took them on test — the engines, I mean — and went up with them, and I had to know everything there was about them.

## Question 10 — Did you enjoy it? `[16:00]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-06.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

Well, I enjoyed being capable of doing it. No, I can't say that I really enjoyed it. There are many small activities that I enjoyed, but not as a whole, compared to living as a civilian in real peacetime. But what I enjoyed about it, I made the best of. And I enjoyed going home on leave. I enjoyed being right in the countryside — always there, as the airfields were in the countryside [?]. And I loved being in various camps. On the Isle of Man I was within a few yards of the sea, just at the back of the seaweed line on the back of the beach. And I enjoyed being technically able.

And the rest of my life I've enjoyed the things they taught me, which I've never used for a job, but I have used for my own benefit, in using tools and machinery and that kind of thing. [I] know perhaps just a little bit more about engines and about mechanical things than lots of people do know.

Right, what else did you say? That looks like it — I think I've answered them.

## His own questions: travelling in the RAF `[17:18]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-07.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

Now I'll ask you what questions you think I ought to have asked you. Pause for a moment.

Yes, OK: how did I get about when I was in the RAF? I mean, what was it like, for instance, when I went from one camp to another — what happened? The answer is, I went by train, and it depended on whether the whole camp was going there or it was one posting, just me being sent there. Both kinds happened.

Now, for instance, when I'd finished all the drilling and so on at Skegness, I went on to RAF Henlow [Cosford], where I was trained, where there was even more drilling, but I also went to school at RAF Henlow [Cosford]. It's still there, just outside Wolverhampton. And it had a great many buildings which were really classrooms for you to learn; and it had aircraft out on the runways on the airfield; and it had aircraft in the workshops in various stages of being taken apart; and it had instructors who had virtually blackboards, and drawings of parts of aeroplanes, and then it had bits of the aeroplanes in front of you. And you'd got instructors who told you: this is a Claudel-Hobson carburettor, it's used in a Hawker Hurricane fighter, and it is of this particular type; this is a Stromberg downdraught carburettor, which is used in the Spitfire in replacement for the Claudel-Hobson, because it means that the Spitfire can roll and the carburettor will not cut out, whereas with the Claudel-Hobson it will cut out if it turned over. So those sorts of things they taught you, you see.

But as to getting to the places — well, when I went from Skegness to Henlow — I'm sorry, it was Cosford, near Wolverhampton, and Henlow was later. When I went to Cosford near Wolverhampton, I was in my train, and the journeys were very slow and very sort of erratic, because of two things. First of all, because they had to go from one place to another and then wait, and then go on to another place, and then sometimes you had to get off the train and change and go on another train, and so it might easily take 12 hours or 18 hours to go across England. But not only did it do that, but it was also in danger of being bombed, the train was. So all the lights would go out and the train would stand still somewhere, and you'd see that there were a lot of flashing lights and things going on, and you'd know that some city further out, distant from the line, was being bombed. And your train mustn't go into the city until the raid was over. So it was a bit hairy like that. I mean, you got a bit sort of anxious about whether you might get bombed by the way, on the train, or even machine-gunned, because they would have machine-gunned the train if they could see it. So the train shut up and stopped and turned everything off if there was something like that happening and they couldn't get away. Sometimes they cleared out of the town.

I mean, it's perfectly true to say that — I used to be very glad — I used to cross London, go to Liverpool Street Station during the time when the V2 bombs were coming down, and they came down all round the station, and you'd hear a terrific crash and the whole place would rock, and you'd be sitting in the train thinking, "My gosh, I wish this chap would get this train out of here." And presently — oh boy — he moved, and he went, and then you'd go out into the dark outside the station and you'd see burning buildings all over the place in various directions, out each side, and you'd hope heartily that the train would get a move on and there wouldn't be a hole in the railway line ahead of you. And the train would pull out, and then presently you'd be ticking along and you'd come to Chelmsford or somewhere, and you'd think, "Thank heavens we got clear of it." That was on the way back to Norwich, when I was going there [to Oulton]. So travelling about was pretty awful.

But when you travelled with a whole party, as I did when I went up to Scotland, to Leuchars, from Oulton in Norfolk — that was where there would be three or four hundred people at least, maybe more than that, eight hundred people perhaps, on the train, all going to the same place. Well, then we would be taking enormous stores and things in goods trains as well, with bits of aeroplane engine and things like that, for the purpose of setting up a new airfield with these aeroplanes on. And the aeroplanes of course themselves would fly — the ones that were working — and it would be the bits which would go with us. And we'd go in the ordinary train, and we would be completely equipped with everything that was our kit.

## His kit `[22:50]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-08.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

When we joined the Air Force we were given our kit, and our kit would be two uniforms, two sets of boots, two lots of everything — shirts, vests, pants, socks, tie — no, not two ties, one tie — and so on, and a pullover. And we'd have a greatcoat, which was heavier than an [overcoat?], very warm. And we'd then have anti-gas equipment, which would be a gas cape to put on, with a hood on it, so that if we were attacked from the air by gas being sprayed — which was sprayed in a kind of light rain, generally — we would be protected from this. We had respirators on our backs — gas masks, for civilians — but they were different from civilian gas masks; they were much more complicated and bigger. And we had a rifle and a bayonet — unless we were doing special, you know, guard or something like that, when we would be carrying perhaps Sten guns, which were machine guns, light machine guns. But we would be mostly carrying rifles, having bayonets. And we had — oh yes — we had a steel helmet, a tin hat, so-called, and a camouflage netting to go over it, so you couldn't catch a sight of it glinting as metal in the moonlight. And we would be carrying in our pockets our pay book, [which] showed who we were.

And we'd have a kit bag, and the kit bag would have most of our clothes in. I still have got mine — it's in the garage. And it would have anything you'd got, like a writing pad and a pen. You didn't have much of your own, but you might have that. I did sketches on pads too, and I had one or two pens or pencils or something, probably, and a few stamps so I could write home. After the war, but before I was released from the Air Force, I used to have my camera with me, but not during the war. We weren't allowed to.

## Demobilisation `[25:25]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-09.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

And of course people didn't get released from the services immediately, or on the same day. Because I didn't go into the war, actually, until 1943, I couldn't come out at once. So I didn't come out until the summer of 1947. There were two reasons, really. First, they didn't know what was likely to happen in the world, so they needed to keep a big army in. But second, probably more realistic than that: to have such an enormous army as that, or air force or navy — I mean, it was bigger than anything there is now, by far. If you put all the American army and the British army and the French army together now, it wouldn't be as big as the services were then, because it was the greater part of the young population of the country. I mean, anybody between the ages of about 17 and 40 was in [?], pretty well, so long as they were fit enough [?]. So you really got a very vast volume, and of course they couldn't all be sent home. I mean, if you sent them home, first of all there wouldn't be any food for them distributed in the shops, and certainly there wouldn't have been any jobs for them to go to, because [things] were [still] going to get organised. So it was all done very slowly and cautiously, and they were let out bit by bit by bit by bit. And so I came out in late July [or] August 1947 [his letters date demob to 9 July 1947, at Kirkham], and I went straight to the architecture school in Bristol [the machine heard "New York"].

## Entertainment, and the Baedeker raids `[27:08]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-10.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

So there you are — what else can I tell you? Did we have anything to read, and how were we entertained, you might like to know. Well, if you were in civilian life, the cinema still existed and still had films on all the time, and so you could go to them. There was a bit of a danger of being bombed, but the danger of being bombed was like this, you see.

In the time when that raid took place in Exeter in 1942 — could have been 1941, you'd better check that — in that raid, that was a major raid, it was what was called one of the Baedeker raids, B-A-E-D-E-K-E-R. Baedeker was the name of a guidebook which was produced by the Germans, used for holidaymaking before the 1939–45 war by Germans who came to England, and other countries too. And they used this as a guidebook to tell them which were the nicest places to go on holiday to. And amongst the nicest places they regarded Bath and Exeter and Coventry and quite likely Canterbury, I think — yes, Canterbury. I forget which; I feel there were five cities, as being some of the most desirable and beautiful to go to see. [The 1942 Baedeker raids hit Exeter, Bath, Norwich, York and Canterbury. Coventry was bombed in 1940 and 1941, not as part of them.]

Well, if you listen to what the Germans say now, they say that the RAF bombed Dresden and various other towns, and were appalling in doing so because they caused so much damage. Well, it is absolutely right to say that they weren't appalling, because it was Hitler who decided, in that earlier stage of the war, that he was going to overwhelm the British. He was going to come and land here and conquer us completely, and he was going to make us do as he did in the other countries, and he was going to run us, if you see what I mean. And in doing this he would have used all his concentration camps and everything else. And he wanted to force us into subjection.

And one of the most important things about the war you can know is that he determined that, when he'd beaten us out of France — which he did during the first year of the war, 1939 to 1940; we came away from Dunkirk a beaten army — only the Channel separated us from the Germans, and so their only way of attacking us, unless they invaded us, was to come by air, which is exactly what they did. And Hermann Göring — G-O-E-R-I-N-G — Hermann Göring, who was the head of the air force and was one of the main Nazis, sort of on the right hand of Hitler, said that he would easily beat the British, and that he would do so in a matter of weeks. And so he sent their very superior force of aeroplanes to bomb the south-east of England and London. And when I say their very superior — they'd been preparing this for several years, whereas we hadn't, and so we only had a few aeroplanes, relatively. We had Spitfires, which were magnificent aeroplanes, and they had Messerschmitt 109s, which were their fighters. And so they sent their fighters, and [bombers], which were Heinkel 111s and Junkers 88s and various other makes, which have faded from my memory now. They sent them over England while I was still at school. And they bombed largely the south-east and London, and they bombed further and further, but they weren't able to get us to give in.

And they got very nasty about this. We still showed a lot of fight, you see, and they didn't like this, and so they decided to bomb — what they called the Baedeker raids. They were going to bomb the towns which were in the guidebook: our most beautiful — bomb — our most beautiful towns. And these were what Hitler called the jewels of the British towns. And so Bath was one, Exeter was one, and so on. And so they decided to bomb them. Well, that's the raid I told you about. And they pretty well flattened it, and they took huge chunks out of it, and you can see in that book what large areas were missing. And when they did that — I mean, there's a whole tale to tell you about what it looked like when I came to see my parents after they'd been bombed, but anyway, never mind that — when they did that, they killed hundreds upon hundreds upon hundreds, if not thousands upon thousands, of civilians. And they caused the young children to be sent out of London, to be evacuated to the country, and sometimes — very often — they never saw their parents again, because they were killed in the bombing. So really they were totally merciless, and they were totally, entirely going to bring us to our knees [he says "feet"]. And they used such appalling methods when they did take over a country, that people were sent to concentration camps and everything else. And we knew this. Right in the war we knew it, because of various things like the Secret Service and what was called the Maquis in France and so on. We got messages back, and so on; we knew about it. And so we weren't going to give way to this, not under any circumstances whatsoever.

## Bombing Germany `[33:51]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-11.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

And when we began to make our attack, to get back and to conquer Germany and Europe, we had to do something to reduce them to their knees. The Russians were fighting on the Russian front the whole time, and it was taking up half Germany's strength to fight that. But on our side, the Russians were saying, well, you have to bring in a second front — the front being the face of Germany which was facing the enemy. So there was an eastern front facing Russia, and they wanted us to have a western front for us to attack, which is what you've heard about on D-Day. But in order to prepare for this, we had to soften Germany up — that was the term people used. And in order to soften Germany up, we had to make sure they didn't have armaments.

Now, I've flown over the Ruhr, which is where their armaments were. In fact, I've seen the smoking ruins, and the towns were Essen, Düsseldorf, Cologne. All these towns were on the River Rhine, and they were all — Mönchengladbach was another — there were so many. And night after night our bombers went to bomb these places, and night after night they hoped and tried to set fire to armaments factories and to anything which helped the German war effort. But if you can imagine the RAF flying at night, 3,000 feet up or something, in the dark, being fired at [?] by anti-aircraft guns — if you've ever seen a war film, there were some good ones — you will realise that they were flying without any of the aids that they've got now to help them see in the dark and that kind of thing. And they were given targets to find from maps, etc., and they bombed what they could. They were told not to bomb where they knew there were civilians, but they couldn't help — because of the extraordinary closeness of the cities, etc. — bombing vast parts of the towns which had civilians in. But they did attempt not to.

And I can tell you that a few days after the end of the war, I flew around Cologne Cathedral at about 40 foot below the top of the tower, the spire, and the cathedral was still standing. The main hospital, with huge crosses on the top, was still standing, undamaged. And there were vast acres of buildings in between, flattened. So they had managed to save these major places, but they didn't, of course, save the civilians, any more than ours were saved in Coventry. But we were doing this after they'd done all the bombing to our towns, and we were doing it with this sole aim of bringing the Nazis down to their knees and beating Hitler.

So when the second front started, at D-Day, and the armies actually moved in, we had reduced a very large amount of their armament, and they had far more problems making tanks and things like that than they ever had before. They were having to manage; they were living in a worse state than the English were by then, because they were so badly damaged. They kept their forces, their soldiers, the best fed and the best looked after, and the civilians went a very bad way. But when we advanced on their towns, their soldiers were very good at fighting still, and so it was a terrific battle right to the last point — I mean in Berlin — till they finally succeeded in capturing it. And the Russians came from the east, and we came from the west with the Americans.

I should have said something about the Americans, because the Americans were bombing by day and we were bombing by night. The Americans did what they called pattern bombing: they dropped an enormous amount of explosive in a huge chunk, and it wiped out a huge sort of square patch. We were bombing by design, and bombing specific targets. So this went on over the last 18 months of the war like that, and it was the most colossal sort of amount of explosive dropped all over the place. And of course it really would have gone on still further. But the Russians had maintained their battle so wonderfully well, with practically nothing, in the most appalling circumstances, and the Germans were very far stretched, with lots of soldiers kept on a very long front on that side. But they kept very, very powerful forces on the western side, because they were always afraid they'd be attacked by the British and Americans — which they were.

## North Africa and Italy `[39:46]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-12.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

The other thing you need to understand is the route of the attack, because it wasn't just D-Day, as you saw the other day on television. It was an attack through North Africa, which is where Christopher, my brother, went. They were brought out by ship to North Africa, and then they crossed to Anzio — and they crossed to — that's in Sicily [he means Sicily; Anzio is in Italy] — and they crossed to Italy as well, very much under attack as they were going in landing craft. And when they landed in Italy, they fought their way all the way up Italy, and the most famous was Monte Cassino, the battle. And Christopher went there. And then after that they went on up until they pushed them back into Germany, and then they pushed them back, and they pushed them right to Berlin.

## The Germans today, and democracy `[40:43]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-13.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

And if you want to know what my attitude to them is: I have no attitude whatsoever about the Germans today. I've been delighted to meet any and be friendly with them. I have nothing whatsoever to say. I hope that one day they're part of Europe in a real sense, and I hope that there's no longer any cause for any war whatsoever, because we're all so closely knit that we don't even consider such matters.

But the Nazis are something else. The Nazis were the extreme right wing. They were what are commonly called fascists. And in Italy they were fascists: Mussolini was the leader of the fascists in Italy, and Mussolini had a fascist party. I'm glad to say that they finally got rid of him — we got rid of him — when we were attacking Italy. But the Italian people didn't have the same feeling about fighting us as the Germans did; they weren't so good at it. But the fascists still exist. They keep trying to revive them. The Nazis and the fascists are people who don't believe in democracy. The only kind of politics I've believed in, so strongly, ever since 1939, is democracy.

So there you are, that's all I can tell you. But it's occupied a great deal of my thinking all my life — the result of being in that war, the result of having that war — and all the history we've had since has been built, like blocks, upon it: of why things happened. They all happened as a result of the influence of the war.

And prior to the war there's a great deal of history to learn about the rise of Hitler from just after the First World War. The First World War had nothing to do with Hitler — although he did actually personally fight in it — but it had nothing to do with him. He didn't come along until the 1920s, the late 1920s, and he formed his Nazi party, I think, around 1930. And his main object was to teach the Germans in Germany that they were being misled by financial people, people with financial advantage, who were Jewish — German Jews. And they treated the German Jews extremely brutally; mainly, as you know, they carted them off to concentration camps like Buchenwald and other concentration camps, and murdered them by the millions — Polish Jews in particular.

So as far as I'm concerned, there were a very large number of people who did things for the Nazis because they were frightened not to, because they wouldn't stand up against them in Germany. And this is where the shame of Germany lies, and the Germans still feel ashamed of this. But Hitler got such a grip with his totalitarian government — a government with no opposition. And that is why I believe that we must never, ever have any single-party government in this country.

There you are, that's the best I can do. But you can see that it rouses me up like this — and I'm 70, and I was 17 and a quarter when I joined the RAF to fly, as I expected then (and very fortunately for me I did not) in night fighters. You can see that for 53 years or so it's had a very major effect on my life. But most people don't talk about it very much, because they want to get on with — not the four years they had at the war, the five years they had at the war — but the rest of it, which they enjoyed afterwards. This hasn't been history for me. For me this has been what happened; for you it's history.

## The war against Japan `[45:15]`

<audio controls preload="none" src="audio/WWII-14.m4a" style="width:100%"></audio>

And something I've left out, which is very important, is that of course the Americans were attacked by the Japanese at Pearl Harbor during the course of this war. The Japanese were allies of the Germans: there was what was called the Axis, which is the Germans, the Japanese and the Italians. They were our opponents. And we had been at war with Japan, because we were at war with Germany, but it wasn't until Pearl Harbor happened — which I think might have been 1941, I'm not sure, or possibly '42, maybe it was '42 [it was 7 December 1941] — that the Americans came into the war. They'd helped us by sending us food and ships, but they hadn't actually fought. And they came into the war, and a whole new war zone developed, which you mustn't lose sight of, which was in the Pacific, and involved Australia, New Zealand, and Burma, and India, and Indochina, and the eastern side of the USSR. So really they were as much our enemies. And we were fighting them, and our Navy was fighting them, and our soldiers were out there in all those parts of what used to be the British Empire, like India and Burma, and they were fighting the Japanese out there. And quite a lot of the people I knew went in the Army to the Far East instead of going to Europe.

And so we were also divided — like the Germans were divided, on the Western Front and on the Eastern Front against the Russians. We were divided, because some of our people were fighting in Asia against the Japanese. And so really you have to consider the whole war together, which ended, for the Japanese, with the atomic bomb.

## Notes against his letters

- **Posting order he gives:** Penarth (remustering) → Cardington (kitting out) → Skegness (12 weeks, Oct 1943 – Jan 1944) → Cosford (six-month Flight Mechanic course) → Little Horwood (about 3 weeks) → Oulton (about a year, B-17 conversion unit) → Leuchars (Mosquitoes) → Oakington (Liberators) → Isle of Man (Spitfires, Seafires, Wellingtons) → near Leamington [Wellesbourne] → demob 1947. This matches the order in his letters (Oulton → Leuchars → Oakington). He doesn't name Henlow as a posting, but the "further training" that made him an engine fitter was the 1946 fitters' course at Henlow, between Oakington and the Isle of Man. He says "Henlow" twice when he means Cosford.
- **Cologne flight:** the Oulton letters page already guesses that his one passenger flight in a B-17 was from Oulton just after VE Day. This tape seems to confirm it: "a few days after the end of the war" he flew over the Ruhr and round Cologne Cathedral, which fits the RAF's post-war "Cook's tours" of bombed German cities.
- **The Exeter raid he saw:** he dates it to Friday 1 May 1942, the day before he went back to Bruton, but there was no raid that night. His Bruton letter of 9 May shows he was at school for the main raid of 3–4 May, so what he saw from West Avenue was probably one of the April raids (23–24 or 24–25 April 1942), during the Easter holidays.
- **New details not in the letters pages:** Penarth and Cardington as his first stops; the extra four weeks at Skegness after the injection made him ill; being an engine fitter on Centaurus and Merlin engines and going up on engine tests; a flight around **Cologne Cathedral, about 40 ft below the top of the spires, a few days after VE Day**, and over the Ruhr (Essen, Düsseldorf, Mönchengladbach); crossing London through Liverpool Street during the V2s on the way back to Oulton; the Heinkel 111 he took for an Anson, and incendiaries on West Avenue during the Exeter Blitz; the *Athenia* schoolboy; his kit bag "still in the garage" in 1994.
- **"17 and a quarter":** he was born on 2 Jan 1924, so this fits spring 1941 (A.T.C. / volunteering) rather than the 21 March 1942 enlistment date he gives earlier, when he was 18.
