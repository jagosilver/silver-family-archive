*← [David Silver](David-Silver/) · [Silver Family Archive](./)*

While he was training as an architect in [Bristol](Bristol-Letters), [David Silver](David-Silver/) set off alone on his new motorbike, later called "Susie", for a tour of France, probably in the summer of 1951. These three letters to his parents in Exeter follow him from the West Country to Dover, across to Calais and on to Amiens, Paris, Bourges and the Loire, and then home again.

On the way to Dover he waits for two circus elephants at Fenny Bridges, is buttonholed in Lewes by an Irishman wanting his "passage home", and crosses Romney Marsh at 33 mph, sounding "like an awfully angry bluebottle imprisoned in a jam jar". In France he loses the pack holding the bike's customs papers between Calais and Boulogne (the police find it), meets "the worst roads in the world" and bad petrol, and arrives at the Loire with his panniers in ribbons and only top gear. He enjoys it all the same: riding round the Arc de Triomphe "in all that mad hooting traffic", the châteaux, the poplar-lined roads, and the French saying "Oui Oui Oui Oui Oui like machine guns". It was, he wrote, "rather like being thrown into a swimming bath if you can't swim. First you think you are drowning and then you find out how to swim and begin to enjoy yourself."

## 1951

### 01. Monday – Tuesday morning, c. summer 1951 (Exeter to Dover: Romsey and Sandgate)

*Begun "Monday Morning" at the White Horse Hotel, Romsey, continued "Monday Night" at the Royal Kent Hotel, Sandgate, and finished "Tuesday Morning 7–30 am" before crossing to France. Undated. No envelopes survive. He is riding a motorbike that is "not yet run in" (475 miles on the speedometer at Romsey), which fits the new machine of his Bristol letters: in February 1951 he had not yet ridden it home, by May 1952 it is "Susie", and in April 1953 he compares Bristol traffic with "the Champs Elysées & the Arc de Triomphe". He travels alone and does not mention Anthea or the school, so probably a summer vacation, most likely summer 1951.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1
> White Horse Hotel
> Romsey.
> Monday Morning.
>
> My dear Mummy and Daddy,
> I arrived here last night at about a quarter to nine, six hours after I left Exeter — 109 miles from Exeter.
> First of all as regards the weather — I only met with two storms one very slight and one rather heavy — my waterproof clothing and helmet were absolute proof against the second storm which was like a cloud burst — I didn't have to stop.
> Secondly about the motor bike — with the exception of a slight mishap which was of no consequence it has run well
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I have found that in view of the fact that it is not yet "run in" and the heavy load which really makes a difference on hills, and because I must take no chances of anything going wrong, I must go slower than I had hoped until I have done the 500 miles which accounts for my late arrival here.
> Now the mishap — I was just crossing Fenny Bridges before Honiton when I had to come to a stop because of two circus elephants which were going my way. But when I started again I stalled the engine every time I put it in gear although the clutch was out. I looked at the clutch
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2
> cable and followed it down to the casing. the casing had worked loose due to one set screw being loose. So I got off tightened up the screw, tried the clutch found it all right and off I went again.
> I had my tea-supper on the top of some hills just before Dorchester. Very good too!
> I found the part of the road behind Bournemouth — Ringwood etc very built up so that had I wanted there were plenty of beds and breakfasts.
> However I was determined to reach Romsey. I liked the New Forest quite particularly
>
> *(p. 4)*
> the open bit which isn't like the New Forest but like Haldon.
> Finally very tired I arrived here, it was a good choice for a town to stop at. Rather like Calne a very small, mostly Georgian town with a square. I went twice round looking for Beds and Breakfasts but there weren't any and I didn't feel equal to any strenuous thought so I drove into the yard of the White Horse unloaded my panniers, went inside, got a room, got a porter to take my luggage, put the bike into a lock-up and retired to my bedroom.
> This morning I have had porridge or cornflakes — cornflakes
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3.
> sausage and tomatoes or kipper — sausage and tomatoes and toast and feel fortified to face a rather dull looking overcast day and the tipping of the hall porter chamber maid and waiters.
>
> The Royal Kent Hotel.
> Sandgate
> almost Folkestone.
> Monday Night.
> Last night I was tired — tonight I am almost extinct but in better spirits. I have ridden and ridden and then ridden again. To continue my tale of this morning if it really was this morning.
> I didn't have to tip the porter because he was not on duty
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I didn't tip the waiters because the hotel was a Trust House and I had not yet received my bill to see whether they put gratuities on it. When I did get it I found they didn't so asked that 6d be given to her. It was the only waiter: It was a nice trust house like the Swan at Tewkesbury and it cost 12/-. Tonight — Je ne sais pas — the account should be less if you go by quality, more if you go by quantity — its bigger but not so good. (also I have had dinner here.
> So many things have happened to-day I can't record them all — I am tired.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4
> I left Romsey with 475 miles on the speedometer and was soon past the 500 mark, the end of the running in period. It is extraordinary that now not only am I past the 600 mark but quite well on towards the 700. The bike has gone splendidly though coming across what I take to be Romney Marsh, I was belting along at 33 m.p.h. (which, (you needn't smile) is the fastest its been so far on this engine. its sounded like an awfully angry bluebottle imprisoned in a jam jar.
> I had tea at a place called the "Pear tree" just after Lewes — did you ever go there
>
> *(p. 8)*
> with Philip — its a barn decorated attached to a cottage.
> Je suis fatigué mais determined to record what has happened.
> At Lewes something happened which I think would only happen to me — you'll laugh when I tell you. I was very tired and hadn't stopped for a long time and so was cramped and my hands were cold — I got mixed up in the names of the streets and was going back for a street which my route form told me was the way out for Hastings. I stopped just on a corner to ask a man who looked as if he was
>
> *(p. 9)*
> 5.
> watching me to see if I was going to ask the way.
> I said "Could you tell me where such and such a road is?" he was wearing a rather heavy overcoat not buttoned up. He laid one hand on my shoulder so that his coat almost enveloped me he seized one of my hands and in an unmistakably broad Irish voice said "Your hands are cold, you've been driving a long while. Why do you not stop those gears. I've something to ask you!" I thought "Oh gosh I would pick a lunatic"
>
> *(p. 10)*
> just then the engine which had been stuttering fitfully stopped. The Irishman took up a position against my handlebar so that I could not go forward.
> I knew what he was going to do — he wanted money — I could hear it coming, I've heard it so often. "I am an honest Southern Irishman, I've worked in England twenty two years but I haven't the money for me passage home. I thought if he leans over me much more he'll pick my pocket! his overcoat enveloped my handlebar; I let him go on talking while I worked out a way to dispose of him.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> 6.
> Finally after having told this thundering lie about trying to get home to Ireland and shown us that the only other way would be to sell his rather decent looking sportscoat — I said "and I suppose you are going to ask me for some money to help you on your way". He said "yes" as if he thought I've got him now so I regret to say that I countered with what was really just as much a lie. I said "I've changed my money into Francs as I am just going to France." He said "well would you not give me the pennies in your pockets" I said "I need them for petrol".
>
> *(p. 12)*
> he turned ~~away~~ away muttering something about "a wide boy" which either meant that he was one which means easily taken in or that I was one and it means not easily taken in I don't know but I've heard it before. I saw him take the arm of a passer by and walk up the street peering into his face and talking volubly while the poor man shook his head in refusals.
> I turned and asked the way from a small boy who looked safer.
> I've had nothing of lunch to-day. I am sorry I have reversed the chronological order
>
> *(p. 13)*
> I bought a rather "Canesian" [?] lunch in Chichester and eat it in a pine wood.
> Two curious meat pies, some buns, cherries, and lemonade.
> Well thats all for now except to say that its only a short distance to Dover.
> Tuesday Morning. 7–30 Am.
> I can't have breakfast here till nine so I'll write some more.
> After Romsey my road took me along the side of a hill behind Portsmouth overlooking the straggling muddy inlets with a great many ships and cranes. The road surface was not over good and I was glad when I reached
>
> *(p. 14)*
> Chichester.
> Here I put the bike in an RAC car park and walked into the town and bought a sketch pad and my lunch. I thought that it would have been a good place to stop had I been going to Newhaven. I should like to have a better look at it. Then I went on again through wooded country to Arundel. There I should certainly have liked to have had a look at the castle but must needs press on again. I passed rather behind Worthing right by Lancing church which I glanced at as I went by.
> New Shoreham is a gaswork-y place and with apologies
>
> *(p. 15)*
> 8
> to the Ack Ack I was glad when I had gone through Hove and Brighton.
> Then I don't know how I did it but I made my first mistake of the way. I thought that I had seen that I must follow the coast a few miles further when in fact I should have turned inland so I went to Rottingdean and for all I know it may have been Newhaven before I realised my mistake and turned up toward Lewes. There I met the Irish gentleman and at the Pear tree Cottage had tea. Hastings and S. Leonards Winchelsea and Rye — by now there was what was really a sea mist which blotted out
>
> *(p. 16)*
> the landscape and made it rather gloomy. There followed a spirited dash across the marshes before Hythe and then Sandgate. It must have been about 8.30 pm. I enquired at a bed and breakfast sign but was glad to find that they couldn't have me — I became glad after I had inspected it outwardly for a few minutes. The man directed me to another one which looked worse. I saw an R.A.C. sign on this hotel — it didn't look very large, so I stopped. On entering the hall I found no one present and in response to my ringing of a bell I was surprised to find the chef with white clothes and a high hat coming in reply.
>
> *(p. 17)*
> He fetched the manager from his dinner who gave me the last room in the hotel a double room for a single charge. Then he lead me to the dining room where I had dinner, he produced someone to carry my luggage and while I was being shown my room he put my bike in the garage himself — I hope he didn't get oil on his turn-ups too.
> So there we are — and now I am going to marshal my belongings so that they are easily dealt with and hope to have everything ready to leave the moment after breakfast.
> The sun is shining between the salmon coloured curtains of my room.
> Demain [above: (Aujourd'hui aussi)] On parle Français but not Moi.
> your loving son
> David

*Notes:* He left Exeter on the Sunday afternoon: Fenny Bridges (two circus elephants), Dorchester, Ringwood and the New Forest to Romsey (109 miles in six hours), then Portsmouth, Chichester, Arundel, Lancing, Brighton, Rottingdean, Lewes (the Irishman), the Pear Tree at Lewes, Hastings, Rye, Romney Marsh and Hythe. The Swan at Tewkesbury and Calne (near Bowood) are places he knew. He signs off: "Demain on parle Français but not Moi".

### 02. Friday, c. summer 1951 (Bourges: the lost pack)

*Headed "Friday", Hôtel de France, Bourges. It summarises the week since he crossed on the Tuesday. Undated. No envelopes survive. He is riding a motorbike that is "not yet run in" (475 miles on the speedometer at Romsey), which fits the new machine of his Bristol letters: in February 1951 he had not yet ridden it home, by May 1952 it is "Susie", and in April 1953 he compares Bristol traffic with "the Champs Elysées & the Arc de Triomphe". He travels alone and does not mention Anthea or the school, so probably a summer vacation, most likely summer 1951.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Hôtel de France
> Bourges.
> Friday.
>
> My dear Mummy and Daddy
> I am so sorry I have been so long in writing — as a matter of fact I wrote to you the day before yesterday but it was then such a melancholy letter that I did not send it.
> I am getting on quite well despite an awful start. I can't hope to explain all that has happened but I will give you a brief summary and you must ask me when I get home
>
> *(p. 2)*
> Tuesday.
> Crossed with full petrol tank (allowed) on the Invicta — calm as a mill pond — sun in France. — terrible bumpy streets in Calais worse than imaginable.
> Motor bike wouldn't start when I had finished with the customs had to clean the plug and drain and clean carburettor — got away about 4.0 pm (crossing took 1 hr 20 minutes).
> Set off for Boulogne — terrible roads (not the war).
> Lost pack from carrier without noticing between Calais & Boulogne. Went through Boulogne to Samer — found pack missing — returned Boulogne — reported
>
> *(p. 3)*
> missing pack to police and gendarmerie and Mairie. With great difficulty found tiny hotel. Stayed night. Pack contained all customs documents concerning Motorcycle.
> In morning collected pack from Commissariat de Police all complete very glad.
> Set off one day late for Amiens — arrived palatial hotel — lovely bath — felt much better — cost less than the miserable one I stayed at, at Folkestone (Sandgate). Had own suite bathroom hall bedroom etc.
> Breakfast and ~~[struck]~~ spoke English. — Staff English at dinner — jolly good.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> The roads were terrible — my panniers breaking — the motor bike springs under terrible strain — the tyres!! c'est affreux. Next day left Amiens the worst roads in the world, for Paris, can't get to Bourges except through Paris: all roads go through Paris. Arrived in Paris stayed in small hotel near the Arc de Triomphe.
> Proprietors spoke English well.
> This morning went down L'avenue de Champs Elysées Place de la Concorde — traffic quite mad. Enjoyed it.
> Out through the Porte d'Italie
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I shall break the bike [?] right up soon, it is so difficult the petrol is bad and awful things have happened about which I will tell you later.
> However I am all right. I am being boiled by the sun my nose is très rouge. To-morrow to Blois — I shall go on again the next day back again as it takes so long. There are stacks of English people over here on holiday every other car is G.B. or U.S. Les Anglais they look most impressive on their large Triumphs with
> [The letter breaks off here; the following sheet is not among the scans.]
>
> *[One or more pages missing here.]*

*Notes:* He crossed Dover–Calais on the *Invicta* (1 hr 20 min), lost the pack with the bike's customs papers from his carrier between Calais and Boulogne, found it the next day at the Boulogne police station, and so went on a day late: Amiens (a "palatial" hotel), Paris (a small hotel near the Arc de Triomphe), the Champs Elysées and the Porte d'Italie, then Bourges. The page after 0 26 is missing, and a page may be missing between 0 28 and 0 26.

### 03. Monday 8 am, c. summer 1951 (Blois: the return trip)

*Headed "Monday 8.0 am", Hôtel de France et Guise, Blois. He is setting off home via Chartres, Dreux and Evreux, then Amiens and Boulogne. Undated. No envelopes survive. He is riding a motorbike that is "not yet run in" (475 miles on the speedometer at Romsey), which fits the new machine of his Bristol letters: in February 1951 he had not yet ridden it home, by May 1952 it is "Susie", and in April 1953 he compares Bristol traffic with "the Champs Elysées & the Arc de Triomphe". He travels alone and does not mention Anthea or the school, so probably a summer vacation, most likely summer 1951.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Hôtel de France
> Et Guise.
> Blois.
> Monday 8.0 am.
>
> My dear Daddy & Mummy
> This morning I set off on the return trip — I don't quite know where I shall stop to-night but to-morrow night I must be in Amiens and the next in Boulogne.
> I am afraid news from me has been rather sparse but due to having to land so far north and losing those papers its been rather a marathon and poor Susie has not been too well poor thing — I am hoping she's going to reserve her breakdown until Dover, or else I shall have one too.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> The faithful creature stopped galloping 2 km out of Blois and I had to push her in. She was too hot and by the time I had finished I was too.
> The sun is brilliant all day every where — the heat intense and my nose from being a burning beacon is now gently dis-integrating or rather the skin is.
> My experiences should be of immense value to Wiggs [?] and Christopher as there are so many things you only discover at the second time the ~~[struck]~~ first time being the time you are wrong.
> Yesterday I saw the inside of three chateaux and the outside of four. I took some photographs but it was rather difficult — so not many.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> My pannier bags are beginning to be cut to ribbons and all the nuts are being shaken off that framework. However it was well worth it and I am now enjoying myself very much — its rather like being thrown into a swimming bath if you can't swim. First you think you are drowning and then you find out how to swim and begin to enjoy yourself.
> This hotel here is rather expensive but not as nice as the one at Amiens. The one at Paris was a funny little one but I did enjoy riding round the Arc de Triomphe and down the Champs Elysées in all that mad hooting traffic — that really was an experience — particularly as now Susie has only top gear, bottom gear having
>
> *(p. 4)*
> become possible only for a few seconds before it slips out into neutral. Its only a small adjustment within I feel sure but I daren't start taking the engine to bits in a big way out here. As it is at every restaurant I stop I have to ask whether I can wash my hands because of the oil.
> The country round here is so much nicer than further North. I don't much like it above Paris its so open
> [Pencil sketch: a perfectly straight road across open country lined with a single row of tall poplars.]
> no hedges and only trees lining the perfectly straight roads.
> Down here there are woods and rivers and hedges — they feel very laid out not wild but they are much nicer, and one doesn't meet the ghastly smell of garlic and Turkish cigarettes
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I am getting browned-off — I have been waiting for my petit dejeuner now for half an hour — I wonder if the bell does not work — it did yesterday. I want to make a good start to-day. I shall go first to Chartres then Dreux and then if I can make it to Evreux, My original tour would have been easy — Le Mans isn't far off. That ride from Paris to Bourges was as Nanny would say in reference to walks quite a good step.
> × × ×
> I had to go down to get my breakfast this morning the place is dead. I am going to get away in about ¾ hour I hope. I see that to Evreux is 192 kilometres thats about 120 miles I think.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> They are all awfully kind and friendly and not at all fierce. There was a man at the garage at Amiens who said "Oui" about forty times in succession like "Whey". I said did he think my petrol tank too full as they had filled it to overflowing. I suggested a fire — he nearly had a fit — not in anger but in his earnest explanation that it was all right.
> I had to have the tanks drained later and refilled with Shell and my own oil which I bought in Dover. All the oil is bad and the petrol except Shell.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> I am keeping my fingers crossed about my castrol XL. I have not enough to get me back so shall have to use some French oil. I'll get to Amiens on what I've got left I think — thats another 100 miles and then I've got to get to Boulogne. thats 70. 290 miles altogether.
> I've no idea what the temperature is but it must be higher than it is England I think. The French wear floppy straw hats in the country like in Cézanne's paintings and are so swarthy and dark eyed. They are all very friendly and all the time they are saying "Oui Oui Oui Oui Oui" like machine guns ~~well~~ thats about all for now — lots of love
> your loving son
> David

*Notes:* On the Sunday he saw "the inside of three chateaux and the outside of four" on the Loire. Susie had only top gear left by now. Wiggs [?] and Christopher were evidently planning a similar trip. Castrol XL was his engine oil.
