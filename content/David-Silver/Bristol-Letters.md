*← [David Silver](David-Silver/) · [Silver Family Archive](./)*

Three months after leaving the RAF, [David Silver](David-Silver/) began training as an architect at the **Bristol School of Architecture** in Clifton. These thirty-two letters and notes to his parents in Exeter run from his first week in lodgings in October 1947 to August 1953, when he left Bristol, newly married, for his first job.

In the first he finds that "tea" at his digs is really supper, and that the drawing board and adjustable set squares have left him broke. Within a month he decides that architecture is "the right job for me — its all art from the word go". He makes a balsa-wood model of an information bureau, designs terrace houses, and has a church design torn apart by the principal. He shares a flat with his friend Michael Eames and Eames's car "Jemima", and rides his motorbike "Susie" home through the Whitsun traffic.

From 1951 the letters are full of **Anthea Leigh**: reading Trollope together, dyeing a bridesmaid's dress in a tin bath, lying ill in a garden hut at Topsham. In November 1952 he sets out, carefully, how they could afford to marry on his grant; soon afterwards he wrote, "Anthea and I are going to be married at Christmas", and they married quietly at St David's, Exeter, at the end of that December. The later letters come from their attic flat in Vyvyan Terrace and include a drawing of the two of them on the motorbike under the Meteor jets at Filton, signed at last "D. & A."

In a summer vacation, probably 1951, he also took the motorbike on a solo tour of France: [Letters from France (c. 1951)](France-Letters).

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. Most letters are dated from postmarks and from what he says; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. House numbers have been left out. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it.

## 1947

### 01. Monday 29 – Tuesday 30 September 1947 (first days in Bristol)

*Headed "Monday", continued as "Section Two: — Tuesday". In Jago's folder postmarked 1 October 1947, 12.30 pm, to Tresillian. It is his first week: he has just arrived at his digs and is buying his drawing equipment.*

> *(p. 1)*
> c/o Mrs Gay
> Meridian Vale
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> Monday.
>
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I had no difficulty with the luggage or taxies. I was a bit staggered to find on directing the taxi into Meridian Vale that the road was not tarred but was rather worse than the garage lane used to be before it was tarred. It was partially over grown with grass.
> The house looked even less promising, however, as before on entering ones fears are dispelled — every thing is very well polished.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I found rather to my surprise that tea was laid and that it consisted of two pilchards (cold) ten pieces of bread and butter, two pieces of cake and three and a half cups of tea in a pot.
> I didn't realise until later that this was to be my supper under the name of tea. On talking to Mrs Gay I found that she thought supper rather late at 6.45 pm and would like to give it me at six. So I am going to do that and have what will correspond to tea outside at supper time, or buy some apples or something.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> I asked her about rations and she said milk was very difficult. There seem to be a lot of people downstairs somewhere, one must be her husband and two daughters I think. The situation of the house is not as I had imagined it.
> It is thus: —
> [Sketch: section through the house on its slope. Street level with a lamp post on the left; "my sitting room", "my bedroom" and "bath room" (with "window") in a row on the entrance floor; "unknown rooms above"; "unknown rooms below lived in." dug into the hillside; "back yard" and "lane" at the bottom right.]
> Looked at from above the back of the house — [small plan showing the stepped back wall: "bath room", "my bedroom"]
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I am to have breakfast by my request at 8.30 am (I think she thought it rather early)
> I shall be called at eight. They don't use the geyser except for baths so they have to bring me up hot water for shaving. I haven't discovered how often I may use the bath.
> Now a description of my rooms. My sitting room is smaller than I imagined but has a higher ceiling. I shall measure it with a foot rule.
> It is 12 feet long, 11 feet wide and as near as I can measure nine foot six high. It has one light centrally placed a 60 or 75 watt bulb.
> Beneath this is a table rather like the study table 3 foot square
>
> *(p. 5)*
> [Plan of his two rooms: "front door", "dark passage", "flight of stairs upwards", "bath room"; the sitting room with "table", "fire place", "side board" and armchairs; the bedroom with "bed" and "chest of drs."]
> There is a carpet on the floor. In this light I can't tell what colour the wall paper is but it looks grey. There is a rather neat side board with a mirror and a jugs and mugs on it and one good tea cup on display & on closer inspection I don't think — gosh I just sat down in time as there was a knock at the door and in came Mrs Gay. She said "I am afraid I can't offer you any thing to drink as I have no milk but I have brought you some biscuits and cheese."
> I'd just been looking under
>
> *(p. 6)*
> that cup for a makers name and there was none. Osborne biscuits, quite nice — she's becoming quite chatty. I'd just finished eating a bar of chocolate and must have had chocolatty lips.
> Oh — to continue about this room its very dark in the day but quite well lit by artificial light. The window is rather nice — sash like this
> [Sketch of a three-light sash window.] only the middle part moves.
> What about the wireless did you say. Well you would laugh, I've just switched the mains on — the Midland. I could blow myself out of the room with the volume. You twist the scale and with a roar you have the
>
> *(p. 7)*
> Welsh, another twist and its the ~~light~~ West (not so loud.) another and its the third — loudest of all. The third at both ends of the scale is so loud that I have to turn it right down.
> And what aerial do I use and what earth. Ha! I put up about six foot of my aerial and switched on with no earth, imagine my surprise on getting the Welsh home service quite loud. I made an earth to the fire place, it made no difference. In came Mrs Gay. She said the "young gentleman" before me had had a wireless and plugged it in at a wall plug near the window. I looked, it was a power plug. I carefully explained that mine
>
> *(p. 8)*
> was a battery set.
> She went out and looking later I found two wires one went up, one down, I connected up and at once it roared forth. The trouble is it isn't so selective as at home and I always get one of the other strong stations in the back ground faintly, however its all right. I get about six stations very loud and nothing else. So far no light programme and so far no licence so I mustn't play it much yet.
> Mrs Gay was rather upset that I had brought no towels and I think it would be best if you sent me some however many you think necessary, as she hasn't any I think.
>
> *(p. 9)*
> My bedroom has a bed with silky eiderdown a wardrobe, dressing table, and chest of drawers and a nice view, a central light, and carpet on the floor.
> It has also a door like that of another room which on opening discloses a shelved cupboard filled with Jam Jars (full).
> She has given me a latch key with a special knack in using it which she was rather hampered in teaching me as she can't do it herself, any way I've mastered the art now. I've fetched my bike from the station and its somewhere in the lower regions. She's quite kind as she asked me if I'd like the electric fire on, it isn't a bit cold in here so I refused it.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> She from time to time remarks that she hopes that I'll be comfortable but she is sorry that I can't have a hot drink as there isn't enough milk. I think some past inmate must have had a hot drink at night.
> There are no coat hangers. I have one, so could you please send three with the towels. On the walls of this room are two photographs presumably of the daughters, and two red sail fishing boat scenes and a horrid mirror hanging by chains above a coffee and cream tiled fireplace with brass candle sticks (highly polished).
> Well that's all for the first instalment: [squeezed in at the foot:] Please send the books and board on my bed. I've discovered she has a son here too.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> Section Two: — Tuesday
> Went to the architecture school soon got to know Michael Eames, we spent most of the day together chasing round Bristol trying to get adjustable set squares. Terrible expenditure on equipment — I am broke now and shall have to write myself a cheque.
> One enormous board and T square I had to get and adjustable square cost over £5 and I had to have them, everybody has them and there aren't any cheaper ones. About the form for the grant — you have to fix up with an architect yourself the school doesn't help for that eight weeks in holiday time. There isn't a lot of chance in Bristol so I thought I'd better try Exeter
>
> *(p. 12)*
> or locally to avoid complications. You don't expect to be paid or to pay as far as I can see.
> Michael Eames applied for 30/- a week for staying at home in the holidays so I am, as he's got his alright.
> At lunch time in the canteen I ran into Michael Edmonds who was at Bruton. He's doing second year and to-night he came over here and we went out for supper together, he's a very nice chap and a terribly good artist, he's had an exhibition in Cardiff. He showed me four things he'd just finished.
> I can't apply for eight weeks allowance as if I were in rooms when I don't know if I shall be and also by
>
> *(p. 13)*
> saying that I'll be in an architects office it makes it look as if I'll be paid which might make them think twice about the "no income" at all part.
> So I thought I might make up on the 30/- per week a little.
> — — —
> I had two kippers for breakfast and bread and butter and marmalade, and a chop and a half and vegetables and apple pie for supper or tea.
> — — —
> We did some work to-day, ~~isographic~~ isometric projection, I've done it before but am not good at it. Mr Hobbis, my chief instructor advised me to have my instruments trued up at a shop here which I am doing, everybody else has had to buy sets at between £2 2
>
> *(p. 14)*
> and £4 4s. (mine cost 17/6)
> My bed is very comfortable.
> Please excuse this letter being such a jumble but I am cramming everything I can think of in, that you might want to know. (My T square is too small — after all that carrying). The snag is after the first year we have to buy even larger and more expensive boards.
> Well that's about all for now its eleven o'clock.
> I hope you are feeling all right Mummy dear.
> Michael Eames nearly walked me off my feet, Daddy.
> With lots of love
> your loving son David.

*Notes:* His digs were with Mrs Gay at Meridian Vale, Clifton. Michael Eames, an Old Shirburnian with a war-damaged arm, became his closest friend and later his flatmate. Michael Edmonds was an Old Brutonian in the second year. Mr Hobbis was the first-year instructor. The 30/- a week was the vacation allowance on his further-education grant.

### 02. Sunday 5 – Tuesday 7 October 1947 (the first week at the School of Architecture)

*Headed "Sunday 4.30 pm", continued "Tuesday … two days later". In the folder postmarked 8 October 1947. It describes "the first week".*

> *(p. 1)*
> Meridian Vale
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> Sunday 4.30 pm.
>
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I expect you are having tea as I write this. So far so good for the first week.
> First the Architecture School. —
> I haven't paid the fees as they are content that the matter should wait until the grant is settled one way or the other.
> The fees if the grant is successful are paid direct to the school over my head.
> There is no allowance for books etc. The allowance for living is paid to me in ~~four~~ 3 cheques per year.
> This term ends about a week before Christmas day.
> One arranges with an architect oneself where you like to do the eight weeks in holiday time.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> Thank you very much for the parcel which arrived intact but bursting open. All first year students (14 of us) work in a largish room on the ground floor of the school we have trestle tables in 3 rows one stool per table and a wood block on which to rest your drawing board. The drawing board is immense with a steel edge. This is only the smaller size, in a year or so we have to have a large one. Eames and I immediately tried to arrange the larger one now and to cut out the small one (so called its about a yard by about 2'6"). However this was not allowed.
> On Monday when we weren't shopping and Tuesday we did orthographic and isometric projection.
> On Wednesday all our papers (same size as the boards) were pinned
>
> *(p. 3)*
> round a room and criticised. We were told that under 10 was poor 10–12 was pretty good and 12–14 the highest mark usually given was exceptional. I found it rather difficult, not so much the work but the drawing to keep it beautifully clean and accurate.
> There were a lot of tens about three elevens and two thirteens. We were told that we were a very good year (not very much to go on if you ask me just two days work), Eames and I, both got eleven. There is another Shirburnian if that's the correct word, besides Eames a chap called ~~[name struck out]~~ [above:] I've forgotten his name — very nice. They both knew the Whiteways at school.
> Lunch up till now has been a problem however I think I've got it straight. The school offers a lunch which is adequate but invariable at 1/6 — very moderate. That's all right but you have to queue
>
> *(p. 4)*
> for ages. Next door is the Y.W.C.A. this is open to us for meals. I have become an associate of the Y.W.C.A. (1/-) so that I can have meals there.
> I had lunch there one day, it was better than the Architecture School lunch though it cost slightly more (2/1). I can also have supper there which brings me to another thing.
> Owing to the fact that Mrs Gay doesn't want to give me supper after six pm, a point to which I have no alternative other than to agree, this means that I have breakfast at 8.30, lunch (rather light) at 12.45 and supper or tea or whatever you may call it — a cooked meal at six. This leaves me hungry at about nine thirty but not hungry enough to
>
> *(p. 5)*
> want a proper meal, in fact really I've had supper but no tea that's what it comes to and rather a light lunch. So I've got Mrs Gay to move my evening meal forward (which suits her better) to when I come in from the school and then I can go round to the Y.W. for a snack at eight before it closes.
> This should be a good deal more economical than what I've been doing this week. For lunch this week ~~twice~~ once I've gone out with ~~[name struck out]~~ thingummy and Eames. After trying various places we arrived at one where we had lunch (all this because of the enormous queue at Architecture School). It was very expensive. Not so to Eames who doesn't seem to mind but anyway enough to make ~~[name struck out]~~ thingummy go home to lunch, and in future me decide to try the Y.W., you were quite right Daddy I should think Eames is well off. He gets a pension as well as the grant, for that arm of his.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> The place where we had lunch was not expensive as a place to go out to but it was as a place to have lunch at every day of the week.
> The writing of this letter has been interrupted by tea which on Saturday and Sunday is at five and consists of lots of bread and butter and jam or syrup and two pieces of cake and a tea pot that holds about 3½ cups of tea. ~~After~~ During this I listened to the first instalment of John Halifax, Gentleman on the childrens hour It was quite good. It goes on four six weeks on Sundays at five. I seem to be getting on quite well here and to be looked after all right as far as food and every thing goes. We have the Mrs Heard [?] stunt in the morning of a knock on the door and a jug of hot water in the bathroom. I find to my dismay that I've run out of writing paper
>
> *(p. 7)*
> Tuesday
> Having got some more paper I've decided to go on, two days later.
> On Friday I went round to see Michael Edmonds, the old Brutonian, and he showed me some of his pictures which are extremely good. He sent one to the Royal Academy which was rejected but he's going to try again. I think he might get one in in a few years but I doubt if he will yet as his life drawing and so consequently all his drawing is not absolutely sound.
> On Saturday we went to a place near the docks and both sketched in pen and ink mine was brought abruptly to a close by the parking of a private 'bus in front of me.
> On Saturday morning having got Mrs Gay to sign a card I went to the public library and got out two books one fiction, one non-fiction. It's quite a good library and should
>
> *(p. 8)*
> be very useful as it has good history books which I need. I got out "Silver Ley" Adrian Bell's book and am enjoying it. I also got out Macaulay's Translation of Herodotus history which is very useful and from which I am learning about the ancient Greeks to give me a back ground for the Greek architecture. I find by my work on my own in the R.A.F. that I've learnt a lot of useful stuff which we shall do. I find also that I have many useful books though still have many more to get, alas! Christopher has two books which we are supposed to have.
> Being equipped with books and boards is very expensive although I already had many essential books. Almost every book I have we have been advised to buy and many more beside.
> We did a measured drawing last Wednesday for which once again Eames and I got eleven each though this time we had more rivals.
>
> *(p. 9)*
> We are now working on an Imperial sheet of cartridge doing Roman and other lettering, the standard we have to attain is very high. We have till Friday to do it. I want to get 12 for this if I can. The trouble is you can't bring your drawing work home at night as the board is nearly as big as the study table top.
> We have had introductory lectures on Building Construction: — Mr MURCH — not very good as a lecturer.
> History (Architectural) — Mr Freeth [?] — quite good and a nice man
> Theory — Mr Hake the principal.
> Mr Hake is a tall man with ~~grey~~ white hair a little Lloyd Georgian about the hair though quite so much as Lloyd George. Wears a grey pin stripe shirt red bow tie and handkerchief in breast pocket to match blue socks and dark suede shoes and has a big forehead. Looks pretty clever. He must be about 60 I should think.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> The school has 105 students. It's a nice school and the majority of students might have been at similar schools to Bruton (anyway in the first year) There are 14 now in the first year.
> Almost every one has been in the forces — you can tell by the expressions used in conversation. I enjoy it and think that as long as the Mechanics which I have to-morrow for the first time don't overcome me, I can cope with the rest in time. It's of advantage to have done drawing before for it seems to be a very very close thing to doing Art, much more so than one would think from outside. Every plan, every detailed measured drawing is judged and criticised publicly before you not only for its accuracy but for the excellence of the drawing itself, the balance and arrangement etc.
> I like Bristol. There are to be lectures by good people, free, in the public library on Thursdays. I shall go. Have you got any thing I could wear
>
> *(p. 11)*
> for a smock, anything does, like one of our photography coats if it were moderately clean and in one piece. It's to wear to keep ones clothes clean as the lead from soft pencils on such big boards makes the rooms unbelievably dirty. You don't see the dirt but its like London, by the end of the day your hands are black. My cuffs too get filthy in the first day I wear a shirt. If you have nothing it can't be helped but it would help if there were anything. I am sorry to keep on troubling you about towels and books and smocks but there seem to be so many things to get.
> My wireless goes well, I had the accumulator charged at a little shop about three minutes walk from the house. I can't get the light programme loud enough or separate enough the third encroaches on all the others. I am just next to something called a
>
> *(p. 12)*
> booster station for the third about ten minutes walk from here.
> I might as well draw this little map so that you can see where I am in Bristol.
> I went to St Mary, Redcliffe, church on Sunday its a beautiful church with a marvellous choir.
> [Sketch map: "me" at Meridian Vale; Queens Rd; "National Provincial 5 mins walk away"; Park St; St George St with "Arch. Sch."; City Centre; the Floating Harbour with a ship; "Temple Meads Station"; "3 minutes from docks". Beneath: "School of Architecture 12 minutes walk at most."]
> On Sunday afternoon I went to the Art Gallery — very good — Turners — Cotmans, Paul Nash — enjoyed it.
> That's about all for now except the financial times (see next sheet) with love your loving son David.
>
> *(p. 13)*
> The Financial Times Supplement.
> Expenses this week have been heavy owing to Equipment bought and various trial shots at meals at different places.
> I started off with £5 from you drew £7. 10 — from the bank. Thus had originally £12 10. Of this I have £2 11 9½ left. The rest has been spent as recorded on the other sheet.
> Next week I should be able to cut expenses to something like this — including meals laundry board and lodging and hair cut drawing materials
> £3 15 —
> that's going on expenses last week. This is bad enough worked out over a long period — without considering any more books or boards. However I'll do what I can to keep the expenses down.
>
> *(p. 14)*
> Michael Eames is trying to get some digs, he's tried a boarding house but doesn't like the look of it (45/- a week but nowhere to work)
> Some time during the course of time I shall be paying for two books from Wheatons and shall have to get Simpsons History of Architecture 3 vols second hand if I can.
> I am sorry that the first week should be so much, but the board on its own cost something like £2 10s and one of the instruments £1 10.

*Notes:* Fourteen first-years, 105 students in all. The staff he names are Mr Hobbis, Mr Murch (building construction), Mr Freeth [?] (history) and the principal, Mr Hake. *John Halifax, Gentleman* was being serialised in Children's Hour. *Silver Ley* is Adrian Bell's farming novel. The last two scans are the "Financial Times Supplement", his weekly accounts.

### 03. Sunday 12 October 1947 (top marks, mildew, and a hypnotist)

*Headed "Sunday Afternoon", from Meridian Vale. Not in a postmark folder. The lettering sheet he got M 14 for was due "Friday" in the 5–7 October letter, and Michael Eames is only now moving into digs, so this is the following Sunday, 12 October 1947. (Jago's folder labelled 11 October 1947 holds a later letter, so its envelope may belong to this one.)*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1.
> c/o Mrs Gay
> Meridian Vale
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> [Top left:] Please forgive this letter being so scribbled and involved. and so long.
> Sunday Afternoon.
> [Top right:] Thank you for the sports coat which I received all in order.
>
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> Thank you very much for the apples which are very good, and also for the letters. My accumulator is being charged for the second time since I have been here so the wireless is working rather quietly from a 1½ volt battery this is because the H.T. battery is possibly beginning to tire.
> I was very well pleased with the result of the criticism of the lettering. We were all called into the criticism room and found our lettering sheets pinned round the room. They all have the marks on them, the best of them being retained by the school
>
> *(p. 2)*
> for exhibition purposes.
> We had laid our lettering out in the manner of the example given which happened to be the best sheet of last years students, it had M13 written on it in ~~[struck]~~ red chalk. M for 'mention'. Though very neatly executed the letters were not very nicely shaped I thought and I determined to do better. Michael Eames and I, had each got 11 for our previous work. This time we determined to get more. We were very careful and took our time about the work.
> So when I went into the "CRIT" room as it is called I looked around for my work among the fourteen other almost identical sheets. At first I didn't see it but finally I spotted it in a corner. On it there was
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2.
> a red seal and a great 'M 14' (I was top.)
> I was so pleased. Mr Lance Wright the registrar gave the criticism, taking each one in turn. He was very nice about mine though a bit biting to some poor chaps who had tried very hard but had got rather ugly shaped letters. Michael did very well he got M13 so we were very pleased.
> After this comes the inevitable decline and fall. We had tracing for the next two days — it was very dull just copying a sheet under your tracing paper to make a negative for blue prints. The marking is for good clear lines and accuracy. Mine was awful — really I am not pretending I shall be lucky if I get nine. Some people have been down to seven I think.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> But now comes the real tragedy. We had to do rendering. that is putting a flat or graduated wash on a sheet of paper. Its very exacting and requires great care and a certain amount of skill. If it goes badly wrong nothing can be done, if it is not too bad you can sometimes patch it up to look all right but ideally you should get it right with the first application of paint.
> In case you can't make out what I am talking about heres a little sketch of what we are doing on a large scale in paint.
> [Pencil sketch of the exercise sheet, 32" × 23": a graded panel dark at the top on the left, a graded panel dark at the bottom on the right, and a fan-shaped arch of washes in the middle, above a small lettered title.]
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3.
> In the left panel which incidentally must be carefully drawn straight at the edges — the colour starts dark at the top and with succeeding additions of weaker colour becomes almost white at the bottom — the object being to grade the colour so that you can't see the stages through which it passes as stages ie like this [tiny pencil sample of a stepped wash] but so that it varies all the way down evenly. The panel on the right is the same only starting weak and becoming strong (you may not turn the board upside down so that its like the first) and the middle one, the least skilled but the most difficult because it takes so much longer so that you are liable to get fed up consists of fifteen fan shaped washes of equal strength laid upon each other the diameter of each being slightly less so that it gets darker toward the middle.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Well heres the tragedy. I bought water colour paper from the school store and mine suffers from mildew. No one else knows what it is but I am sure its mildew I've had it with Pad's paper. I didn't find it out until after I'd strained my 32" × 23" one and a pennyworth of paper, had drawn the shapes of the washes out and started the centre wash. It came out in black spots like measles all over the wash. The spots do dry lighter but they spoil the effect.
> At first Mr Hobbis thought it was my mistake in technique somewhere so I had to sponge out the first nine washes — a mornings work. This didn't do any good I knew it wouldn't and has not improved
>

### 04. Monday 27 – Thursday 30 October 1947 (the grant arrives; the school party)

*Headed "Thursday". He received his grant "On Monday 27th" (27 October 1947 was a Monday), and quotes Tommy Handley's joke on that evening's ITMA about Hugh Gaitskell's "fewer baths" speech of late October 1947. ### 05. A Sunday, c. 28 November 1948 (the information bureau)

This is not very encouraging. It is however because the business of how many marks you get is relative to how good or bad the designs of the others are. ## 1950

### 06. Wednesday 6 December 1950 (Moss Bros, and the 'Mile Three' party)

*Dated "6th December 1950" in full (6 December 1950 was a Wednesday). Jago's folder is labelled "undated letter possibly 1951". The suits from Moss Bros are for his brother Christopher's wedding, and term ends on "Friday the 15th".*

> *(p. 1)*
> Page 1.
> Richmond Park Rd
> Clifton,
> 6th December 1950.
>
> My dear Daddy & Mummy
> Thank you very much for your letter, Daddy; I am glad to hear that things might possibly be moving as regards Bedford Circus.
> On Sunday I woke up to find the snow on the ground and there was another fall on Monday morning — but now it has more or less all gone except for one or two rather treacherous pieces of ice on some of the pavements.
> I have had the most dreadful cold ever since I left home. It absolutely laid me low last week so that I couldn't do anything and felt so feverish — but this week its better and now that it is, it sounds much worse like "a code" in the "dose" and everybody who didn't realise I had one last week when I felt so awful is now very sympathetic and I feel perfectly all right.
> Our 'gas' expenditure has been much heavier this last week or so.
> Now about the brothers Moss — I have visited
>
> *(p. 2)*
> them and now have some information to impart which I hope does not come too late.
> I asked them first about their connection with Yendells. They said that the Bristol Brother Moss did not deal with anybody in Exeter except Pinder and Tuckwells so that in all probability the London Brother Moss co-operates with Yendells. I explained the situation to them and they told me that in their opinion if it were possible for you, Daddy, to come to see them the day before they could supply you with everything that was necessary, at once and that you could try them on there and then.
> I then intimated to them that you could not usually wear clothes straight off the hook, and they were rather pained and explained that they had a simply enormous stock of every possible size in clothes and that it would put them on their metal to find a man they couldn't send out of their shop straight off quite satisfied.
> I asked them about dealing with their firm from the Exeter end, and they said that they would not advise it, if it were at all possible to personally come to their shop in Bristol. They explained that the Exeter firm merely sent off a list of your measurements and
>
> *(p. 3)*
> Page 2.
> received back a suit, which usually you were bound to have, as by then it would be too late to change it. Whereas if you could call in at their shop in Bristol you could try them on. I asked them about returning the clothes and they said that if the clothes were put in the post before we returned to Exeter that would be perfectly all right.
> As regards myself they said that if I called in about a week before they could put aside a suit for me. I wondered why I had to call in a week before — and so the man said "well" as far as our stocks go we could fit you out at this moment completely without any warning".
> This slight element of doubt made me wonder whether supposing you thought of collecting a suit up here it would be best for you to send me a list of your measurements ie chest, waist, inside leg and arm, and for me also to allow them to take my own measurements before I come down to Exeter so that they could if need be set aside some of the approximately correct sized clothes to make sure they really had got them. This is merely my own precaution as the man seemed quite confident about it and certainly the department in which we were standing could have coated a regiment of ~~[struck]~~ black hatters.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> ~~[struck]~~ The end of term is Friday the 15th — that is the Friday after next. Whether I shall be coming down that night or the following morning I don't know — but I think probably the Saturday morning as it is rather miserable travelling at night at this time of year.
> Anthea and I are having fifty fits about our clothes for the "Mile Three" party, which is on next Tuesday. Thank you very much for the mended pullover, trousers, and material. I looked at the material and then I looked at my dressing gown and the dressing gown had given way round the ~~[struck]~~ neck and it was practically falling apart — so I took the material to a tailor and asked him how much it would cost to have the material made into a dressing gown. He said 35/- and I agreed so you have not sent me material for a fancy dress but rather for a dressing gown. My old dressing gown — well what more logical — its going to be the fancy dress. I have pinned it up and it makes a good tail coat. It is to be dyed black and the trousers are to be pale blue.
> Anthea's white taffeta bridesmaids' dress is to be dyed too as yet an unknown colour. She has had made by her mother a stiff underskirt of "apple bag muslin" which makes the skirt like a crinoline.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Page 3.
> We have had such a game as the dyers here advertise a two-day service and we have been to see umpteen to-day and they all say "sorry nothing less than a week" although they have cards and notices everywhere saying "try our famous two day dyeing service"
> So for less than the price of the dyeing we have bought a large galvanised iron bath which Anthea says will be useful for washing, and a quantity of Daddy's favourite "Dylon" which I believe he saw demonstrated at Waltons and we are going to produce the most ghastly results. You may wonder why I bought 'Dylon' when I've used Drummer Dyes before. Well, Anthea's dress ~~being~~ is taffeta & all the dyeing firms say they will accept no responsibility if it drops to pieces in the dye as taffeta is treated with some surface chemical, but Dylon says on it that unlike other dyes you can ~~[struck]~~ dye chemically treated fabrics with it with no ill effect. Anyway Anthea says the dress would be no good for anything else if its got to be white — so this weekend we are going to retire into the kitchen and come out dyed all colours of the rainbow.
> It's a nice day to-day with sunshine and my cold
>
> *(p. 6)*
> feels really better and its so nice.
> I do hope you are better, Mummy dear. I have a pound? of icing sugar for you and shall also have the tea ration for the month and if you need it, some margarine. Michael and I are going to a party ~~[struck]~~ specially for us ~~[struck]~~ run by Anthea at her digs on Saturday. We are going to play monopoly. We had a very good game of heads and bodies the other day in which the staff featured a good deal. It was while I had gone to bed early for my cold and Michael game and sat on the end of my bed most of the evening.
> Now about coming up to Bristol — my invitation is always open about you, Daddy, coming up and staying with me from the day before — should you think well of the idea it would be most convenient if I could let Mrs Thomas know before I come home for Christmas. I should also like to know if Christopher is coming and must write to him.
> I am giving them a mincer and ~~a~~ a dozen tea towels.
> With lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* By now he lives at Richmond Park Road, Clifton, sharing a flat with Michael Eames, with Mr and Mrs Thomas as landlords. "Bedford Circus" is his father's bombed dental practice in Exeter (see Bedford Circus, Exeter). "Them" in the last line are Christopher and Nancy, whose wedding was that winter. Anthea's white taffeta bridesmaid's dress is presumably from that wedding.

## 1951

### 07. Monday c. 22 – Thursday 25 January 1951 (after Christopher's wedding; his 27th birthday)

*Undated, continued "Thursday". In the folder postmarked 25 January 1951, 10.30 pm. His birthday cake had 27 candles (he was born on 2 January 1924), and Christopher and Nancy have just married and gone to Italy.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Richmond Park Road
> Clifton
> Bristol.
>
> My dear Mummy and Daddy
> At last I am "up sides" with this terms work and with going to tea with Mrs Pym and buying food and cleaning the flat and scrubbing the kitchen which occupied all Saturday morning. I can settle down to write this letter as part of a scene which would present to the eyes of an observer, were there one present, a very domestic appearance.
> I am sitting on Anthea's sofa before the gas-fire writing to the tune of a very steady hiss from the fire, while Anthea sits next to me doing some mending.
> This term seems to have been going like a merry-go-round that one has climbed onto but which will not stop until it has run its course round and round and round until the Easter holidays.
> To begin with there was "The Wedding" and there was going to ~~tea~~ supper once with Mrs Pym Christopher and Mary afterwards, then supper with Mrs Pym alone, and then tea with Mrs Pym and Anthea yesterday (Sunday).
> There was my room which when you have no time mounts up and up until there are piles of books and clothes and dust and desolation and there was the kitchen with a dustbin on whose lid reposed the bodies of countless spent matches while round its base were little pieces of potato peeling and bacon rind which had just missed going in. Then there was Michael — Michael
>
> *(p. 2)*
> who seems to have spent until 10.0 in bed every morning ever since the beginning of term and who twice has been there until 11.25. However I separated all my food and cooking from him so that does leave me rather less tied than I was before.
> I've been designing a Church and Village Hall which has occupied me considerably but its done now though I rather wonder how you would react on seeing the church. All the time like a sort of backcloth there have been reports from Christopher and Nancy in Italy and all the time I've been buying tins of meat in case they disappeared and queueing at Bright's Sale to buy sheets at 6.15 am at 42/6 per pair because they were going up to sixty something. (I got them and tea towels)
> I wrote to James and Auntie Muriel proper thank you letters for my birthday presents, I paid three bills by post and I wrote you one whole page this size — I had a ghastly cold — then I had rheumatism in my chest so that I had to go to a doctor as it hurt to breathe and then I had to get Ephedrine from the doctor for my asthma and now I am coming out of the muddle and can really think straight and write to you.
> So here after this rather lengthy introduction is my real letter — my asthma's better — the rheumatism gone and I've done my work!
>
> *(p. 3)*
> Thursday
> Time has marched on and I have been engulfed by work again, this time a housing estate with various teams working on it. For my team I have to produce by to-morrow morning a design for three bed-room terrace houses.
> Last night I went to supper with Anthea who had received a small chicken from home ~~[struck]~~ by post. She boiled it and browned it and it was very good and we had cauliflower and potatoes with it and afterwards a fruit salad.
> She is getting very keen on cooking and makes scones for tea. Anthea came to me for lunch and we had chops potatoes and carrots and rice pudding — we find it more economical going out to meals with each other than going to restaurants and providing you plan ahead it doesn't occupy too much time.
> One Saturday — I think the Saturday before last Michael took us out to Lawrence Weston — a suburb to see a church designed by Mr Burrough. Then he took us on up the Gloucester road to a loop in the Severn and a lovely village called Frampton and then on to Gloucester
>
> *(p. 4)*
> to look in the cathedral. Michael was running his rebored engine in. It was a star turn.
> None of us knew when we were starting off that we were going further than Lawrence Weston. We stopped at the "Ship Inn" at Almondsbury for lunch. It is a trust house and very nice.
> You will no doubt hear all about Philip's photographs from dear Mrs P. whom I like more on every meeting. She liked them, Nancy liked them less than the professional ones, I thought Christopher looked more like Kit in Philip's than in the professional ones — but Nancy looked better in the Cyril Howe professional ones.
> I am most worried at the back of my mind that I seem to have lost a grip on Lydia's [?] present, on James' socks, and that I have never thanked you for any of the dozens of things you have done for me.
> For a start thank you for the fancy dress we had a lovely evening at the theatre and afterwards at the school where I introduced Anthea to Andrew Hake and we spent the evening chatting — he gave C and N. a wedding present.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Thank you many times too for the most excellent cake on which there burned 27 candles so that at times I wonder the heat did not scorch the ceiling. (exaggeration there). Anthea and Michael came to my birthday tea and we played that "Wot" card game, and had some crackers and I enjoyed it — we had it rather late as we stayed at school as I had declined an invitation to Mrs Pym to tea on the grounds that I should be at school (please note this.)
> The presents were all delightful — thank you for the towel which I shall look forward to seeing with my other things (the other towel, material, etc).
> Thank you also Mummy and Daddy and Wiggs for the ties and books. Anthea and I are reading Framley Parsonage together which is far more enjoyable than bought entertainment like cinemas. Anthea has read Mansfield Park but so far I haven't had time.
> Please don't think because I don't write much I've forgotten all about you as its only that I seem to rush from one thing to another in a desperate panic and never get anything quite finished these days hence the fact that I have a
>
> *(p. 6)*
> a once started letter to you all ready and that I haven't still posted those bills yet but have carried them ready a week in my pocket.
> Please do tell me, Mummy. What about John O'. Have you had the bill yet, if so please do pass it on to me as I pay that.
> Michael has been so good as he is going to run me to the G.P.O. to post this letter to catch the 10.30 pm post as I have done something for him for his car to-day, but its now 10.20 so I must finish off as its quite 10 minutes run by car
> With lots and lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> P.S. I want to come home for a week and soon, please? perhaps the one after this next one — if that would be all right or perhaps if not the one after that.

*Notes:* The first letter in which Anthea appears: he writes it sitting on her sofa while she mends. Mrs Pym is Nancy's mother, and Christopher and Mary Pym are Nancy's brother and sister. Michael (Eames) runs a car he later calls 'Jemima'. Cyril Howe was a Bristol photographer. *Framley Parsonage* is Trollope. Andrew Hake was a member of the school's staff (the principal's son?).

### 08. Sunday 4 February 1951 (terrace houses and a dining recess)

*Undated; it ends "It is now 8.20 on Sunday evening". In the folder postmarked 4 February 1951. The Ashes test-match commentaries Eames wakes up for were from the 1950–51 series in Australia.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Richmond Park Road
> Clifton
> Bristol.
>
> My dear Daddy & Mummy
> I had it in mind to buy some postcards and stamps so that I could send one off on Thursday after you suggested it, Daddy. However it was Friday before I thought goodness! it won't get there in time and the telegraph being less expensive than the telephone I sent you a wire — which I hope didn't make you wonder what on earth was in it. All of which seems to be a rather complicated sentence. I was very sorry to hear about Uncle Herbert's death — I wish I had been a bit nearer them when I was in the Forces.
> I would have come home this weekend but I have had rather a set-back in my work and also if as I had wondered I had decided to bring my motor bike back — it didn't seem very suitable weather.
> The set-back in the work was my church which they didn't like a bit so that it was more harshly criticised than anything I have ever done.
> Looking back I think some of it but not nearly all was justified and comfort myself with the thought that Mr Hake who did the criticising is not very contemporary in outlook. They haven't asked for one but I shall do another church design as I can't bear
>
> *(p. 2)*
> them to think my design was due to incompetence but merely to a certain train of thought I was following. I shall do an entirely different one which will suit them (but not me).
> Our housing estates are progressing, I have designed a number of fairly convenient but rather soulless — soul less? terrace houses with three bedrooms but I hope to make them rather more attractive by experimenting.
> The great problem which may appeal to you as you live in a terrace is — If the corporation will not allow a road at the back of a long terrace for service vehicles dust carts etc how are you in small houses with limited space, to afford access to the back of the house for people to bring through dustbins pig-buckets etc and put bicycles lawn mowers etc away in tool sheds in the small back gardens? The answer at first appears to be make a passage every so-many houses through to the back but this idea has been sat on, as we are told that these passages belonging to no one become congested with dirt and rubbish, are dark and unattractive, and generally undesirable. At present I have this arrangement.
> [Plan of the ground floor: kitchen, larder, dining recess, living room, stairs, W.C., "back door" and "Front door".]
> There is a separate back door on the left with a tiled (shaded) passage through the kitchen (where the arrows are) and dirty boots don't matter along there.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> You may wonder at the words "dining recess". It is at present a scheme much in vogue in small houses where there is insufficient space to have ~~[struck]~~ decent sized rooms (dining and living) downstairs to make a really decent sized living room with a small recess off it just big enough to hold a table and which is next to kitchen so that a service hatch can be used to pass things through.
> [Pen-and-watercolour drawing: three people at a table in a dining recess with a curtained window, a hatch behind the man facing; an armchair, fireplace and a cat in the living room; pink floor.]
> These people are supposed to be sitting at the table in a dining recess having a meal — behind the man facing you is the "hatch" to the kitchen. The woman on the right seems to have a black bandage round her jaw — perhaps she has toothache. Anyway you see the sort of idea.
> Incidentally it must be a very awkward shape for carpeting I should think but perhaps you don't carpet the dining part, which of course you could curtain off if it had a window of its own.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> Mrs Pym who rang me up last night, tells me that Nancy will be coming down the Saturday after next and will show the wedding and Italian films: I am invited to tea. So I should think its pretty definitely next week end for me for home — the weather is the principle factor that deters me but I must ride that bike soon or it may deteriorate. I hope to make it a decent long weekend and not one of these wretched 'gone again in five minute' ones and would aim at coming down on Friday afternoon — Mrs Pym tells me the trains are dreadful she told me this on the 'phone while Mrs Thomas was talking in the background and I couldn't concentrate and had a vague impression at first that it was the 'drains' for a split second.
> Mr Thomas is having a Batley Concrete Garage (about £60) and I am doing his garden plan to submit to the surveyor.
> My room has a leak from the roof above the mantlepiece — it is the direction of the strong wind to day.
> [Small sketch of his room: "General impression from the door end of the room."]
> The water came down from above the picture rail behind the picture onto the mantlepiece and stained the white paper above the picture rail — it is a pity.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Mummy dear, I must apologise for an omission I made in my last letter Anthea sent and again sends her love and she told me to say she wore "take two taffy" to Mrs Pym's for tea. She is very pleased with it but only wears it on special occasions, as yet, with her new skirt.
> Michael, who has disposed of the cowbell which was easily rectified by the garage and was as Philip said something to do with the starter engaging spring, has spent the afternoon happily in the basement with the door from 'Jemima' the name which he has recently given to his car. He has been fitting new felt linings to the window so that they shan't rattle.
> He had a great deal of trouble over the bill for his engine rebore but all is now well with Michael actually in pocket over the transaction. Since it is rather a lengthy tale I will reserve it until ~~[struck]~~ I see you.
> I wonder whether to-morrow you will be re-directing an envelope to me telling me the Air Force wants me for a fortnight in the summer — I hope not but 15 days isn't too bad as long as it isn't at an inconvenient time.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> It is now 8.20 on Sunday evening — the post has gone except from the general ~~[struck]~~ where unless it is different on Sundays it goes at 10.30 pm — I have promised Anthea to go and see her at 8.30 when we will have supper — cottage pie — so I shall have to put on seven league boots to do half an hour's journey in ten minutes.
> I spent all of yesterday swotting structural mechanics which is pretty good for me — arithmetic. Eames has been sleeping all day until 8.30 pm. more still except on test match mornings at 7.30 when he wakes up until the commentary is over and then goes off again.
> Oh dear it does sound rough and wet outside I wish I hadn't left my hat in Exeter.
> I must go really I've only five minutes left before I go to Anthea and I must fly to the Post Office
> Goodbye for the time being
> hope I may see you next week end
> I WILL LET YOU KNOW IF I AM COMING if I don't, assume I am not
> Lots of love
> Your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* Uncle Herbert has just died. He may be recalled to the RAF reserve for a fortnight in summer 1951. The coloured drawing is of a "dining recess". The Batley concrete garage and the garden plan are for his landlord, Mr Thomas.

### 09. Thursday 17 May 1951 (Whitsun traffic)

*Headed "Thursday". In the folder postmarked 17 May 1951 and redirected to his parents on holiday at Seaton. He rode back from Topsham after Whitsun (13–14 May 1951).*

> *(p. 1)*
> Richmond Park Rd
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> Thursday.
>
> My dear Mummy & Daddy
> I did enjoy my rather short visit to you at Seaton, though I wish the weather had been better. I am afraid you must still be finding it cold — to-day in Bristol has been a curious mixture of yellow purple thunder cloud and a few little showers but now (6.45) it's so bright with big clouds and bright blue sky, and a cold wind from the north east.
> After that ~~evening~~ afternoon on which I left you I went over to Anthea and again the next day and left for Bristol just after tea. Anthea has a wooden hut in the garden made of unseasoned elm — I think it will warp badly. It's now almost complete and is about 10' 0 square with windows on three sides. To-day, Thursday, she must have been into Exeter for her X ray.
> I left Topsham by cutting across over to
>
> *(p. 2)*
> Pinhoe, past the electric power station on the Honiton Road. Directly I joined the Taunton road I realised that the traffic returning was much heavier than going down though still not very heavy. There were processions of about eight to ten cars at a time but these might be, I should judge, any way a mile apart. As I moved on from Taunton to Bridgwater the traffic thickened until one saw few breaks in the traffic all heading northward.
> At Churchill, a village near the turning to Cheddar after you have climbed the first hills from the Highbridge road, there is a road junction which leads down to Weston-Super-Mare.
> This is not the normal Bristol–Weston road but an agricultural show at Long Ashton just outside Bristol on the usual Weston road had caused motorists to decide that they would come from Weston back to Bristol via Churchill on the Bristol Bridgwater road. This meant that the road from here on was taking a double load of traffic to Bristol and there they were from Churchill onward nose to tail going forwards at about 10 miles an hour — a stream of cars
>
> *(p. 3)*
> stretching six miles to the top of the hills above Red Hill. I stayed with them some time, starting and stopping and then as the "down" side of the road was entirely free of them, I went up the line of traffic, along the white line passing stream upon stream of crawling cars winding tortuously up 'Red Hill'. At the top the traffic speeded up and I was in a stream nose-to tail doing about 30–35 miles an hour At last I came to the head of the column and left that stream of traffic only to come on another ones tail a few miles out of Bristol. Progress through Bristol was agonisingly slow. I got back about 9.15 pm. Very tired to find Eames who had also arrived back about an hour before also very tired. Since then nothing much has happened I am still working on the farm (design) — still trying to catch up with back work. Eames has been told that he will not be ~~granted~~ given the grant next year but must do the fourth year again at his own expense and will then be given the grant for the fifth year. This means one year extra for him — this is because he has been
>
> *(p. 4)*
> getting so much behind with his work. This provides a grim warning to me and I am doing my ~~[struck]~~ best to consolidate a fairly shaky position. Michael doesn't appear to mind the idea of doing a further year a bit but I am not at all keen on the idea both from a time point of view and a financial point of view — if there is any failure I would rather it was after I had completed the five years course in the final — because I would then at least have completed the school syllabus and would know the things I should need to improve.
> So I must "get to my muttons" as I seem to have heard you say Mummy!
> Oh by the way, I forgot to turn right early enough coming to Seaton, I found on my return journey, and went too far towards Lyme Regis that's why I came down such funny little roads that I remarked on to you Daddy. Well I hope you all feel much the better for your ~~[struck]~~ holiday and that the journey goes all right to Exeter.
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* Anthea's elm hut in the garden at Topsham, where she lay outdoors (she had TB, which her own memoir says lasted until she was 25), and her X-ray in Exeter. Michael Eames must repeat the fourth year.

### 10. A Wednesday, c. 23 May 1951 (Mr Hill's 'startling suggestion')

*Headed "Wednesday". It follows the Seaton letter ("I posted it on the Thursday") and hopes for a visit home on "the 15th Friday or the 16th June" (15 June 1951 was a Friday), so probably Wednesday 23 May 1951.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Richmond Park Road
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> Wednesday.
>
> My dear Daddy & Mummy
> I wonder how you are and whether the journey back from Seaton went all right. I wonder whether my letter was waiting at Exeter when you got back or whether it was re-directed to Seaton. I posted it on the Thursday.
> Eames has to do the fourth year again — I am not sure if I told you. I am in such a shaky position in some ways through having a lot of back work that though I don't think that I would be compelled to do it again I have wondered what I ought to do. I asked Mr Hill our year master and he made the following suggestions.
> He said most people don't get through final in all subjects first go in fact its very unusual. He said that in view of the amount of work I have to do to catch up I shall find it very much a struggle and that it will be a toss up whether I get through next summer at all. In all probability in company with a dozen or so others I should either just get through on a few subjects or not at all. In either case I should have to take either the rest or the whole the following summer. He made the following rather startling suggestion to me. Before telling you however I must explain that the fourth and fifth year are really an indivisible swotting period for final exam. He said why don't you leave the school at the end of this summer term and get a job
>
> *(p. 2)*
> in a Bristol office (at £5 a week) and work until after Christmas getting practical experience, confidence in your ability to make a living, and speed in draughtsmanship and then at your own expense for two terms, Easter and Summer, come back and complete the rest of the fourth year. You will then have had most of the lectures twice, have done a good years work of drawings (which have to be submitted to the R.I.B.A as testimonies of study) and you would do well in fourth year exams instead of scraping through. > Peter said (he lives in Bristol) "Oh that's my dentist — I used to go to an awfully nice man called Wyatt there." "My family all went and we did like him."
> So now he goes to Brookes as a private patient, not as a National Health one.
> Anyway Michael is going to investigate.
> The Tilleys have withstood all the cold weather and have probably really saved me pounds.
> I have seen more of the Florence heater which I mentioned before which runs on oil — its very neat and gives a good heat. it runs for 29.7 ~~minutes~~ hours on a gallon of paraffin. Its not a pressure heater like the Tilley and so can be filled while its alight — it is also enclosed like a convector and so can be used in the bathroom or hall.
> We have discovered that we can have our oil delivered here so all we do is take the empty can to the shop and they deliver it free of charge.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Friday.
> Thank you very much for your letter Daddy.
> Thank goodness the weather is so much milder and pleasanter even if there is a sprinkling of rain — it makes everything seem less of an effort.
> Work is at full pressure now for time is getting very short.
> I hadn't realised that car skidded or may have — it puts a different complexion on things.
> I am sorry to hear about your eyes Daddy, I hope they are better soon.
> We seem to have had a very little snow just before the weather got warmer — not as much as you.
> I am glad to hear how the enlarger is passing its tests with good marks.
> I am sorry to hear about Christopher and Nancy's influenza. Touch wood! we haven't had anything although everyone else round us has. We've been married nearly eight weeks and are very happy.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> Auntie Muriel and Uncle Basil seem to be going in for heating and hot-water appliances in a big way nowadays.
> I have got a few leaflets which I will enclose (recently obtained from the coal utilization council office who recommend the "Tor" slow burning grate).
> Anthea has been helping me quite a lot with my work and doing the housekeeping but doesn't seem any the worse for it. On the contrary she seems in very good form.
> I made a thing one Saturday afternoon for hanging clothes. Being a furnished flat we can't stick things in the walls and so it required some thinking out the problem was this.
> We had a wardrobe which was terribly overcrowded with both our lots of clothes — where to hang some more?
> I finally decided on the space between the wardrobe and the wall [small sketch of the bedroom wall with the wardrobe, an arrow marking the gap].
>
> *(p. 7)*
> so I got some wood from a place I know of where you can buy as much hardwood as you like — no license — ready planed to the sizes you want. The wood was all about 2" × ¾" and I got 24'—0" of it. Then I cut it with the little saw which Wiggs gave me for Christmas which worked very well.
> I made two uprights to go against the wall with a cross bar like this [sketch: two uprights joined by a cross bar]
> then I made two parallel top pieces joined by a bar like this [sketch: two long rails joined at one end]
> I screwed all joints with two screws.
> then I made a hole here and another one here [arrows to the two pieces]
> and I put them up in position ~~[struck]~~ as you will see on the next page with a 1/- broom handle seen in a sale fixed between those two holes indicated by the arrows above. Nothing was screwed or attached in any way to the fabric of the room (there is no picture rail)
>
> *(p. 8)*
> here it is.
> [Pen drawing: the finished frame between wall and wardrobe, with coat hangers and a jacket hanging from the broom-handle rail.]
> I am sorry thats very badly drawn but the idea is that the horizontal bars rest on the top of the vertical bars on the left and are shoved towards the wall [small sketch: the rails, "← shoved"]
> they are shoved there by the wardrobe which is hard up against the other end. [small sketch: the rail ends resting on top of the "wardrobe"]
> I must stop now or miss the post
> With lots of love to all of you from both of us
> David.

*Notes:* The "frightful disaster" was a fatal accident in which a car mounted the pavement, evidently involving someone his parents knew. Mr Wyatt and Brookes were Bristol dentists. Auntie Muriel and Uncle Basil were in the heating business. The drawings show the clothes rail he made between the wardrobe and the wall. The `0 87 k` and `0 93 1` files are rotated copies.

### 28. Sunday 12 April 1953 (married: Meteors over Filton)

*Undated; "Yesterday, Friday" and "to-day (Sunday)". In the folder postmarked Monday 13 April 1953. They have just returned from the Easter holiday (Easter Day was 5 April 1953).*

> *(p. 1)*
> Vyvyan Terrace
> Clifton
> Bristol.
>
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I arrived back at six o' clock after a journey without incident to find Anthea already in possession having arrived about half past four.
> My bike did not however come up entirely without protest. there was something which made me feel that all was not well with the works.
> We had a nice time at home and enjoyed the holiday very much. I am so sorry that I went to bed directly I arrived as it must have been rather a "dull haycart" for you and extra work.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> On Wednesday Anthea and I went off by bike for a short ride to get some fresh air — we went down to the Severn and as far as the Aust ferry and then round in a loop and back by the Gloucester road past Filton.
> Just before we reached the Gloucester road we stopped to look at a little village called Olveston. When we returned to the bike it declined to start — ten miles to Bristol!
> I did some things to it and eventually with a very irregular heart beat it made its erratic way towards Bristol.
> When we came to the part of the road which passes the edge of Filton aerodrome — seven meteor jets one after the other
>
> *(p. 3)*
> took off and roared across our route about forty feet above the road like this.
> [Pen drawing: two Meteor jets roaring low over the road, a hangar with aircraft beside it, cars and pedestrians; in the foreground the backs of David and Anthea riding the motorbike, registration MAE 684.]
> Anthea on seeing this drawing has insisted that I put ourselves in riding on the bike — so here we are. ←
> We made rather jerky progress all the way home and on Thursday I spent a long time on repairs.
>
> *(p. 4)*

> Coming back we were in the rush hour in Bristol in four streams of traffic and I think I prefer the Champs Elysées & the Arc de Triomphe.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> The enlarger travelled up here on my bike quite safely and I expect to start using it shortly. The weather is much colder and we have one 'Tilley' in operation.
> Bristol seems about the same except that the Chestnut tree outside the sitting room window is beginning to have leaves.
> It has been a poorish day to-day (Sunday) but is better now 6.30 pm so we are thinking of taking a walk.
> My bike lives under waterproofs in the back garden
> with love to everyone
> ~~[struck]~~ your loving son
> David.

### 29. Wednesday 17 June 1953 (notice to quit)

*Headed "Wednesday". In the folder postmarked 17 June 1953.*

> *(p. 1)*
> Vyvyan Terrace
> Clifton
> Bristol.
> Wednesday
>
> My dear Mummy & Daddy
> I got back on Sunday a quarter of an hour early. The bus only waited a few moments at Weston and after leaving Exeter at 6.5 it reach Bristol at 9.15. It was a very nice run. The weather got clearer and the clouds higher but the roads were wet.
> Since then we have had very dull and sometimes wet weather.
> We received rather a bomb-shell to-day about our flat. We have to leave as they want to make use of the bedroom themselves and turn the sitting room into a bed-sitting room to let.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> They have given us (quite correctly under our agreement) a month (4 weeks) notice to leave. This will bring us to Tuesday July 14th. The end of term is on Thursday 16th but I don't suppose the last two days are very important.
> I am already engaged in looking for a job and may or may not have got one by then — probably not though I can't tell.
> Consequently we may be on the point of departing to any town anywhere for a job and there finding a flat but until I get the job we shan't know.
> The result is that we shall want somewhere to live for the few weeks — I hope. between July 14th and when I get a job. This will be a problem as ~~[struck]~~ no one in Bristol would rent us a flat without
>
> *(p. 3)*
> a guarantee that we would stay 3 months I think. If we do that I might get a job in Southampton 3 days after I have given the guarantee, and then I should be stumped. One thing does cheer us we are still solvent and if we live carefully I could remain unemployed until the end of next January if I had to.
> Anthea's mother has asked ~~[struck]~~ me to try to let Anthea come down if I would spare her for a rest at any time and I wondered if, as they expect to see us sometime this summer, we might do what we did at Easter and come and stay first with you and then with them at the end of term.
> If you could bear to have us for a fortnight, we could go to them also for
>
> *(p. 4)*
> a fortnight and that would give me eight weeks from to-day in which to find a job and then a flat in.
> If Anthea's family were willing as they have storage space ~~[struck]~~ we could take our big things to them and I thought if you didn't mind I could put my text books in my old room at the top and my clothes in the wardrobe.
> If I hadn't got any ~~thing~~ job in a month we would take a flat in Exeter while I went on looking — because Exeter would be just as good if not better than Bristol to look from.
> If you think that this idea of coming first to you for a fortnight and then to them would be all right (a fortnight on July 14th) please could you let me know — but PLEASE if you think
>
> *(p. 5)*
> it isn't don't be afraid to say so and we will start flat hunting here.
> If it seems an awful lot of work say so. It certainly was rather a knock out for us as it was completely unexpected. One reason why I am not anxious to hunt for flats just at the minute is because both of us are trying desperately to get our work done in time Anthea for the external examiner. I for the R.I.B.A. testimonies submission both on June 30th.
> Must stop now if I am to catch the post. I did enjoy seeing you
> lots of love
> your loving son
> David
> Anthea sends her love.

*Notes:* Anthea, at the school of architecture too, is preparing work for the external examiner; David's RIBA testimonies of study are due on 30 June.

> *(p. 1)*
> Vyvyan Terrace

> Bristol.
> Sunday.

>

She had brought Christopher's France photographs or rather some contact prints he had dashed off, and Anthea was able to see her niece smiling benignly under the sun at Nîmes
>
> *(p. 3)*
> I suppose Mrs Pym stayed three quarters of an hour and we did enjoy her visit, and then after telling her of our impending departure from this flat and discussing all the family news including Mary Pym writing home from the Acropolis in Athens on her way to Chios, Mrs Pym was off once again in AHU 133 — the Austin.
> Mummy, dear, thank you for your invitation about coming home.
> Just after I wrote my S.O.S. Mrs Thomas came to the rescue.
> She has suggested (and we have accepted) that we should go to my old flat which Michael will have left, for a month. This will take us to about August 14th – 18th.
> I thought that this seemed better as Philip might be descending on you in July so have agreed to this.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> It is only until mid-August because the Thomases will be having some relations to stay after that.
> We shall have the whole flat. What I feel is this — surely I shall by mid August have got somewhere in looking for a job — if not, the same situation will then arise again.
> So please provisionally can we come for a fortnight but at the latter half of August instead?
> You see I don't want to do what James and Mary did to Anthea's family and stay and stay all the time, and if it was a month later I should have had more chance of a job.
> Accompanying this letter is a tile which blew off the roof which I
>
> *(p. 5)*
> thought might be nice for a picture by your bed and an ornament.
> The ornament is meant for looking at in different lights when things are dull. I hope they don't bust in the post. By the way the tile is part of the Bayeux tapestry, now produced in tiles, — perhaps it is one of the Bayeux Tilery!
> You can get lots of others but I liked this best and hope you do too. I must stop now and put my nose to the grindstone — how painful.
> with tons of love
> dear Mummy
> from
> David.
> P.S. Anthea wants to add a postscript to this but she's at the school and I must post this or it won't go in time.

*Notes:* Nancy is expecting her second child. Christopher Pym's France photographs. "James and Mary" are evidently Anthea's sister Mary and her husband James Farrant. The present was a Bayeux Tapestry tile.

## A letter from Philip Tilden

A letter **to** David from the architect **Philip Tilden** (1887–1956), written from Wortham Manor, Lifton, on 11 October 1948 and kept with these letters. David had worked with Tilden ("your erratic visits to us"), and recalls staying with him at Bowood in the June 1952 letter.

> *(p. 1)*
> Wortham Manor Lifton.
> Oct 11 /48
>
> My dear David
> What an address?! Meridian Vale.
> Your photographs arrived while I was away, & when I got back I had to go to bed.
> We were delighted with them & I shall keep them together with the others to remind me of your erratic visits to us.
> Thank you from us both — many times over.
> How are you getting on?
> Send one of the façade of the house if you can — please.
> Nothing has happened at Bowood yet — all things today take an age to get going.
> I wish I could think that you learnt anything with me — perhaps one thing — namely that it isn't necessary to have a huge office to be an architect after all — I do a great deal myself.
> With our greetings yours Philip Tilden
