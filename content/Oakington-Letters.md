*← [Silver Family Archive](./)*

From the summer of 1945 [David Silver](David-Silver) was stationed at **RAF Oakington**, near Cambridge, as **AC Silver** of 4206 S.E. These six letters to his parents at **Tresillian** in Exeter run from the last days of the war to January 1946, between his [Little Horwood letters](Little-Horwood-Letters) of 1944 and his [Henlow letters](Henlow-Letters) of 1946.

On the night of **11 August 1945** he waits for the nine o'clock news, hoping "we don't accept Japan's peace feelers until they come right down off their high horse and let us do what we like unconditionally", and tells his parents that his brother Christopher "is not very far away by air. My work is very closely connected with where he is." Coming back from leave he cycles through blacked-out London past St Paul's, "standing very stately and silent in the darkness". He goes to a Harvest Festival at Great St Mary's, where the vicar seizes his hand: "Good Morning, My Boy". He watches a ginger cat stalk a water rat on the Backs. By December he has arranged **building-construction classes at the Cambridge Technical School** and is asking about a grant, the first steps towards the architecture that became his career.

The longest letter is a comic account of a Saturday in Cambridge in November 1945 with **Barbara**, a relative he was meeting for the first time: a bus full downstairs, "Conflict" at the Regal, a scrum in Heffers, and hot buttered toast and "some very bad tea" in a faded gilt restaurant. "It made me realise how glad I'd be to get home … I'd feel as though I'd like to live in a house with a high wall round its garden and be quite cut off from the world."

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. None of the letters has a full date, so each has been dated from the events he mentions; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it.

## 1945

### 01. A Thursday, c. early August 1945 (back from leave)

*Undated ("Thursday"). He is back from "a lovely leave", and his brother James will be at Hill Farm, Brent Eleigh "until the end of August". He proposes a 48 "the Saturday after next", and the next letter (Sat 11 Aug 1945) arranges eleven days' leave, so probably late July or early August 1945.*

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] Any time when you aren't terribly busy not necessarily at once, could I please have my pyjamas from the wardrobe in Wiggs's room. I forgot them.
> [Page number circled:] 1
> 1587757 AC Silver
> 4206 SE.
> R.A.F. Oakington
> Cambridge
> Thursday
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I got back here at about 7.30 am. The train fortunately for me ran from Cambridge to Oakington to just the other side of the camp.
> I was able to cycle to the guard room and then breakfast.
> I felt a bit tired as I had been unfortunate at Liverpool St. The train from Exeter had been late and did not come in until 11.20 pm. So I got on my bike and crossed ~~[struck]~~ Waterloo bridge. To my surprise the Victoria Embankment runs under Waterloo bridge and there is no
>
> *(p. 2)*
> way down from the bridge for a bicycle unless one carries it, so I went round Aldwych and down Fleet Street (noticing the Daily Telegraph's imposing building) Then up, I think it was, Ludgate Hill to St Pauls standing very stately and silent in the darkness It seemed to be tremendously high when I was at the foot of it as it was a dark night and the roof merged into the sky.
> Then passed many policemen and the Bank of England to Liverpool St.
> I went in search of the beds I had told you about and found
>
> *(p. 3)*
> [Page number circled:] 2
> that the beds had gone. About three o'clock after sleeping in a waiting room chair I saw a notice which said "Forces Luggage and Rest room opposite Taxi rank". I searched for some time and could not find it. Finally I discovered it. It was served by a very small and dingy looking entrance. Inside were double decker beds and arm chairs much better than the waiting room. There was a book in which you signed your name and the time at which you wished to be called. I had already deposited my bike in the station cloakroom which will open by request at any time.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I slept till about four and then caught the train to Cambridge in which I slept all the way.
> By dinner time I was pretty tired but fortunately while I had been away they had extended the dinner hour to an hour and a half. So I slept most of it. Then I was lucky in that I was given the job of organizing an oil bowser (400 gals) for refuelling and this bowser required two hours filling at which some one must be present while it pumps itself full in case the engine stops. As this was by an oil pump out in a field
>
> *(p. 5)*
> [Page number circled:] 3
> the chap who was with me (who'd also returned off leave) and I both lay down in the grass and fell asleep in the sun, only waking when it was nearly full.
> So you see I didn't do so badly and even had the energy to go to "The Keys of The Kingdom" Cronin's novel made into a film, about a missionary in China. It was excellent and as it was the last night it was being shown at the camp cinema I didn't want to miss it.
> So I went to bed and woke yesterday feeling quite all right.
> Yesterday showed a change in the weather and both yesterday and to-day there
>
> *(p. 6)*
> has been a steady drizzle most of the day.
> "The Shakespeare murders" intrigued me all the way to Surbiton. I never suspected the criminal but had an entirely false suspect in my mind although I would have bet you anything I was right before I found I was wrong. (very complicated)
> Now here is some news - to my surprise and pleasure when I got back I found that we were to have a forty eight hour pass and a day off per fortnight to be taken separately.
> To-day I see my party is on the list to have a forty eight
>
> *(p. 7)*
> hour pass starting at four o'clock (afternoon) on the Saturday after next and lasting till 0800 hrs on the Tuesday morning. So I think if it's convenient I might see you the week end after next - if it's convenient and you aren't rushing around looking after Auntie Muriel or Philip or James or Wiggs or anything. If it isn't perhaps I could go to Arthur or to James at Hill Farm. He's written ~~[struck]~~ to ask me over but I should only like to go for the day. He said he'd actually be at Hill Farm Brent Eleigh Sudbury until the end of August.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> I thought if I went to Arthur I could go to the Central YMCA in London for Saturday night and go out to Chelmsford on the Sunday morning returning on the Monday afternoon after tea. But I'd much rather come home if it's all clear.
> The rain drizzles down and all the buildings look very square with windows for eyes and doors for noses. [Drawing: a flat-roofed block with two windows and a door between them.]
> It was a lovely leave.
> I've written to Jane about the car and I'm thinking of trying for a starting handle in an enormous second hand place in London I've heard of
> with lots of love
> your loving son David.

*Notes:* He numbers the sheets 1–3. "The Shakespeare murders" is a detective novel. *The Keys of the Kingdom* (1944) is the film of A. J. Cronin's novel. Jane (the car) and Arthur (Chelmsford) recur in these letters.

### 02. Saturday 11 August 1945 (Japan's surrender offer)

*Headed "Saturday night". He hopes "we don't accept Japan's peace feelers" until Japan surrenders unconditionally (Japan's offer was broadcast on 10 August 1945) and waits up for the nine o'clock news. The envelope postmarked CAMBRIDGE 13 AUG 1945 probably belongs to it.*

> *(p. 1)*
> R.A.F. Oakington
> Cambridge
> Saturday night.
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> Thank you very much for the letters, the timetable and the shoes. I keep getting letters directed on to me from Leuchars.
> I wonder what on earth that was that made Kit say 'something had happened'. I wondered if he flew, in which case, I should not be surprised at anything happening.
> Now here is the latest news up to date on my coming home. Providing nothing holds me up - I may arrive at about 10.15 on Tuesday night for eleven days. If however something does I may not come in until twelve midnight
>
> *(p. 2)*
> There appear to be three possible trains. Two would bring me home sometime just after ten or so, and the last just before twelve. In the event of the first I imagine you'll still be up at ten but if I don't come before say 10.30 do go to bed. I shall then probably creep in about midnight if you'll leave me some way to get in.
> If however there is any momentous news on the wireless and I don't come please don't worry. I'll try to phone you if that does occur. The authorities seem generally to herald any important news as an excuse for a Forces Travel ban, so that's what I mean when I say it's possible I might not come.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> I rather hope we don't accept Japan's peace feelers until they come right down off their high horse and let us do what we like unconditionally.
> I too saw that film about Chopin in Dundee about a week before you saw it. I enjoyed the music. I thought George Sands was a bit Hollywood-ish and Chopin not very real and I couldn't stand the old music teacher.
> What a pity, Daddy, you'll be so busy, still everything seems so good in the news that perhaps everyone will soon come home and we'll all have a holiday together.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> It might satisfy you to know that Christopher is not very far away by air. My work is very closely connected with where he is.
> I am waiting for the nine o'clock news before finishing this letter. The day has passed so slowly and tomorrow will be slower. There is a programme on with seagulls and waves and music, - Desert Island Discs. I've just found out that this is the Light programme, that it's now 9.15 and that they don't seem to have news. So goodnight and I shall see you on Tuesday night or Wednesday at breakfast
> with love, your loving son.
> David.

*Notes:* Letters were being forwarded from Leuchars, and he had seen the Chopin film (*A Song to Remember*) in Dundee, so he had come to Oakington from RAF Leuchars in Fife. "Christopher is not very far away by air. My work is very closely connected with where he is" is as close as he could come, under censorship, to saying where Kit was.

### 03. A Sunday, autumn 1945 (Harvest Thanksgiving)

*Undated ("Sunday"), on RAF crested notepaper. A Harvest Thanksgiving service at Great St Mary's, Cambridge, and "a week end 3 times running" after recent leave: probably September or early October 1945.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC Silver
> 4206 SE.
> RAF. Oakington
> Cambridge.
> Sunday
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> I got back rather weary after having to stand all the way from London to Cambridge on the 4.30. I forgot that it was the end of the weekend and so I only left the bed I was resting on at 3.30 am and found the train already packed. Some of us waited at the front end which had no engine as yet, but no coaches were put on.
> However I didn't feel too bad by mid morning, and after lunch and an hour's siesta felt quite all right. We had an inspection going so it was lucky I had got some rest at Liverpool Street.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> The train from Exeter to Waterloo was a very good one and I think I was in London at about 9.15 as far as I remember.
> Well here's some news, if it is suitable I could come home again next week end. I should have it one day later that is to say this time I should come on the SATURDAY NIGHT or early SUNDAY morning and have Sunday and Monday at home instead of Saturday and Sunday. It's pure good luck that they've give me a week end 3 times running. Some people have had mid-week 48s each time. But if it's going to be an awful
>
> *(p. 3)*
> strain please say and I'll do something else: Would Phil & Dorothy be there still? I should imagine they would. Wiggy would have gone back I fear. I must warn you that they would not give me a ration card this time as I had one for 72 hours last time. We get a 72 hr card every other 48.
> [Diagram: three 48-hour passes in a row — "first 48 hrs", "second 48 hrs", "third 48 hrs". The first and third are drawn as "1 2 3 day card" boxes, with the third day of the first card and the first day of the third card shaded, and arrows carrying those shaded days to the "2 days" of the second pass. Marked "Q.E.D."]
> I don't know if you can make out what this diagram is meant to be but it doesn't matter if you can't.
> I was given today (Sunday) off and a 24 hr pass.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> [Top right:] P.T.O. after you've read this
> So I went to Cambridge last night slept at the Y.M. Spent part of the morning looking at the, very good, closed shops and the rest at a Harvest Thanksgiving Service at St Mary the Greater or something. It was a nice service. I was just going out when I saw the vicar rushing along in my direction ~~[illegible]~~ smiling. I didn't think he was smiling at me so I turned and joined the queue going out of the door. I was flabbergasted when he put one hand on my shoulder shook hands with the other (hand - not shoulder) and said "Good Morning, My Boy" in a jovial voice. I said good morning and fled. (They often do it with service people it's with surprise at seeing them) P.T.O
> With lots of love your loving son David
>
> *(p. 5)*
> P.S. I went either onto - to - or in the Backs which is a part of Cambridge by the river in the afternoon. There was a splendid ginger cat lying on the path on the river bank looking into the rushes. It was a very nice looking half persian. It was very intent on something and was swishing its beautiful tail from side to side.
> I looked but could see nothing. I was just going when the cat made a dive at something by the waters edge, a water rat (pretty old I think) swam slowly across the river looking like a minute beaver and dragged itself up the other bank into the undergrowth. The cat ~~was~~ watched intently and looked a little like Timmy when he'd missed a bird.

*Notes:* Phil & Dorothy are his brother Philip and Philip's wife; "Wiggy" is Wiggs. The diagram explains how 72-hour ration cards were issued for every other 48-hour pass. Timmy is the family cat. The blank backs of the sheets (scans 0 6, 0 8, 0 10) are not shown.

### 04. Sunday 18 November 1945 (a day in Cambridge with Barbara)

*Undated ("Sunday"). The newsreel "of the Cenotaph" is the first post-war Remembrance Sunday (11 November 1945), so the Saturday he describes is 17 November 1945. He comes home on a 48 "a week TONIGHT, SUNDAY" (25 November).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC Silver
> 4206 S.E.
> R.A.F. Oakington
> Cambridge.
> Sunday.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> Thank you very much for your letters. On Saturday I went into Cambridge at about twelve by bike and had lunch. I was going to meet Barbara at 2.30. There was not a good selection of films at all and there were two plays St Joan and Priestley's "They came to a City" but these each started at 2.30.
> There was also a Mozart concert at four by some unknown orchestra (possibly connected with the University).
> I didn't know how long Barbara could stay or what she would like
>
> *(p. 2)*
> to do or where we could have anything to eat. (I never go to anywhere except the Y.M. myself). So I decided to leave a lot of it to her.
> I waited in the Station entrance hall and the 2.19 came in at about 2.35 so I was glad I had booked seats for either of the plays which began at 2.30.
> I waited for someone dressed in a reddish brown tweed costume who looked a little like Lavender. Presently she dashed out, one hand forward as if to shake hands with me across the entrance hall. We hadn't the slightest doubt as to each other's identities.
> If you just glanced at her quickly and looked away again you'd think at once of Wiggs though if you tried to analyse why the differences would
>
> *(p. 3)*
> far outweigh the similarities.
> She has to some extent Auntie Lillah's voice only without the plaintive note which Auntie Lillah employs. She seemed in no way a stranger and I am sure anyone watching us would never have guessed we'd never met before.
> I piloted her to a bus which was full downstairs, I discovered to my horror that all the seats upstairs were occupied by one person each only so that we had to sit behind each other. I was in front and made various remarks at peril of permanently fixing my neck so that I could only look backwards.
> We went to the middle of the town which was of course very crowded being a Saturday, (which was something I hadn't foreseen)
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I asked her how Uncle Herbert was and apparently he returned from the nursing home(?) looking awfully thin and ill but is much better.
> When we got off the 'bus Barbara set off up a side street at great speed (she walks very quickly). I don't know whether she knew where we were going but at the time I didn't. I asked her what she would like to do and without giving her much time to reply told her what there was to do.
> All the time we were shouldering our way through crowded pavements in narrow streets. All the films except the ones I was interested in faded from my mind. So I kept saying
>
> *(p. 5)*
> there is "Conflict" at the Regal and something or other no good at all at the Victoria and another thing which I've seen at the Victoria and which is absolutely awful. It didn't give ~~[struck]~~ Barbara much chance, we seemed destined for "Conflict", about which I knew nothing, which was an advantage compared to the others which I knew were all rotten. It was to start at three according to the evening paper.
> We passed the outside of Heffers (the bookshop) and I remarked that most of the time I spent in Cambridge was spent there.
> Barbara said that she also spent a good deal of time there. So as we had as I thought then half an hour
>
> *(p. 6)*
> to spare we went in to look round. We couldn't see anything as there was a rugger scrum going on inside. Barbara seemed awfully good ~~[struck]~~ at practically knocking people over.
> After we'd fought our way to the art ~~[struck]~~ department and found nothing much I discovered that it was ten to three so we fought our way out again. "Conflict" seemed to be the order of the day.
> We reached the cinema in about five minutes and got a seat thank goodness without having to wait.
> I noticed just as I'd bought the tickets that it wasn't due to begin until 3.15.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> We seemed to be in the middle of a horrible film about American 16 year old children. The film must have been made for 10 year old children. It was awful, fortunately it finished suddenly with a sedate meeting of some shareholders or governors or something being broken up by the entrance of a drum and bugle band and two horrible people embracing.
> There was a ridiculous food fleet, the point of which I've forgotten but which consisted of them bending over to pick things up on a canal bank and being pushed in. Barbara laughed so much that she started to cough which apparently has something to do with asthma which she gets.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> Then we saw the news which I'd already seen, of the Cenotaph. Then "Conflict" began, I had an idea that it was about spies and the war but it wasn't.
> It was about a man who started to hate his wife and fell in love with her younger sister so he killed his wife so that he could marry the sister, but he was caught. Stated like that it doesn't sound at all an attractive afternoon's entertainment. By very good acting and directing and unexpected turns in the plot it succeeded in being a very good film, which I and I think from what she said, ~~[struck]~~ Barbara enjoyed very much. It may be a little ~~[struck]~~ hard to believe that such a thing could be good but
>
> *(p. 9)*
> if you condensed any good detective story it couldn't sound very attractive in just a few lines as I've described this film. (Raffles would just become a dishonest man).
> Barbara seemed to expect something else or else she wanted to see the beginning of the film ~~into the middle of which~~ we first saw. So I got up at the end of "Conflict" and she stayed put So she got up and I sat down so then I got up and we went out. It was almost dark and I was wondering about tea I knew of one large restaurant to which I'd been a long time ago (when I was on the way up to Oulton from Buckinghamshire) It looked a bit like Dellers outside but I didn't
>
> *(p. 10)*
> remember it inside. When we got there through the glass doors we could see long tables laid as if for some dinner. We stood there for a moment wondering whether the people at little tables at the other end where having tea when an R.A.F. officer asked if he could help. So I asked him whether ~~[struck]~~ the restaurant was open. He couldn't understand what I said and ~~[struck]~~ he asked if we wanted the Thé Dansant I said no we only wanted tea, I don't think he understood again as things turned out as he said go out and round the block and come in through the opposite of the building and go up the stairs
>
> *(p. 11)*
> This we did until we reached the foot of the stairs, from the top of the stairs was the sound of a dance band. It was the dance. A man at this end belonging to the place directed us back to the front again. This time we marched in past the laid tables to the little tables. It was awful. It was large and had been decorated in gilt. All the decoration was in need of renovation It had been about like Colsons or Bobbys but had gone down owing to the war.
> We had to sit at an empty table for four as there was nowhere else. It was so full. The waitress was ages and presently two Waafs came
>
> *(p. 12)*
> shared the other half of the table. To make matters worse it appeared that although Barbara seemed at first not to know Cambridge at all she knew three quite good places where we could have had tea. It was too late however to change as it was 5.15 when we got there and the other places might have been fuller.
> The crowning blow was that when the waitress did at last come she announced they only had hot buttered toast. We had this and some very bad tea.
> If only I'd realised what it would be like being a Saturday I would have found a better place and reserved a table.
>
> *(p. 13)*
> I still couldn't make out when she was going back and didn't like to ask in case she thought I wanted her to go. I was just wondering what on earth we could do now when she announced that her train was either at 6.15 or 6.30. I suggested that I should go round to the Y.M. and find out. This we did. According to an old lady who kept the time table on a string out of reach it was 6.24.
> Barbara then suggested that we might walk to the station which we did. There after a lot of shilly shallying we found that the train was at 6.35. So since it was then only 6.10 we got into an empty compartment of the waiting train and
>
> *(p. 14)*
> just talked which was a relief as we didn't have to rush round or do anything particular. So I saw her off and rode back to camp. We decided that we ought to arrange it again and I decided to plan it out better next time.
> So ended a most harassing but nevertheless fairly enjoyable day. It made me realise how glad I'd be to get home and out of this rush of people dashing everywhere for entertainment. I'd rather read a good book than see 99 percent of the films going round to-day.
> People dashing about and trains and stations and buses
>
> *(p. 15)*
> and cinemas and queues make me so tired I'd feel as though I'd like to live in a house with a high wall round its garden and be quite cut off from the world.
> Barbara was very nice and I would like to have met her either at their home or ours better than in Cambridge.
> I am coming home a week TONIGHT, SUNDAY, and will arrive in the early hours of Monday morning as I do from 48s, I have 24 hrs travelling time isn't it good?
> I am looking forward to it tremendously.
>
> *(p. 16)*
> I saw "the Affairs of Susan," George Brent & Joan Fontaine, at the station cinema a few days ago. Arthur had said that it was very good, it was too, in a rather artificial way.
> I am remembering that about the Diary, Daddy. You are the first person who has contributed any guide as to what they want. Do you want a pencil, Mummy dear? That used to be the stock request when I was small.
> With tons of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* Barbara is a relative he was meeting for the first time: she reminds him of Wiggs and Lavender and has Auntie Lillah's voice, and he asks her about Uncle Herbert. *Conflict* (1945) is the Humphrey Bogart thriller. Dellers was a well-known Exeter café, and Colsons a department store in Exeter.

### 05. Friday 14 December 1945

*Headed "Friday". He saw *Caesar and Cleopatra* at the Odeon, Marble Arch, where it had opened on 11–12 December 1945, "this morning", and is coming home "a week to-morrow night" (Saturday 22 December, Christmas leave).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC Silver
> 4206 S.E.
> R.A.F. Oakington
> Cambridge
> Friday
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> I shall be coming home a week to-morrow night if all goes well. I shall probably go by car to Bristol and then to Exeter by train.
> I came back off leave as usual and got to bed at about one That was on Wednesday morning and on Friday night I spent a very restless night without knowing why, on Saturday morning I went to work with a very sharp pain inside.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I retired to bed feeling as if I wanted to be sick at midday with a temperature of 99.7 or 8. The M.O. gave me some medicine with Kaolin in it and said that something I'd eaten had disagreed with me He put down Gastro ~~enteritis~~ enteritis on the report. That night my temperature was 102°. In the morning I was normal They kept me in bed with only drinks until Tuesday. ~~[struck]~~ Then they let me go cured.
> On Wednesday night I set off for a forty eight to Cambridge. I spent yesterday, Thursday, in Cambridge.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> There I arranged to go to the Cambridge technical school next term for building construction lessons, 3 times a week in the evenings. I then made an appointment and saw the regional appointments officer He couldn't be very helpful about my grant I want but I know a little more than I did.
> Then I caught a train to London where I spent the evening reading a book (which might equally well have been done in Cambridge).
> In the morning (this morning)
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I walked up St Martin's Lane joined Charing Cross Rd and went into Foyles bookshop, there I bought for a very moderate price the book I've had on order and not yet received on Malta. The order went to the Central R.A.F. Library in August.
> I then walked up to Oxford Street, then all the way along Oxford St going west to Selfridges. I went into Selfridges for the first time, there was plenty of stuff there for sale ~~[struck]~~ anything from a tin trunk like Kit's (£6 5 0) to a toy wheel barrow.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I also looked in at the His Master's Voice "head" shop in Oxford street. I walked up to Marble Arch and along the Park Lane side of Hyde Park, it was so misty I couldn't see properly what the park looked like. Took a tube from Hyde Pk Corner back to the Strand where I had lunch. Then back to Marble arch by tube to the Odeon to see "Caesar and Cleopatra" Bernard Shaw's play as a film at the end of that back to the Strand for tea and then since I had an hour to spare before my train went
>
> *(p. 6)*
> from Liverpool St, I walked all the way there. Charing Cross Rd, Shaftesbury avenue New Oxford St, St Pauls, Bank and Cornhill. Caught the 5.48 and here I am back in camp writing this just before going to bed at ten to eleven.
> By the way before I forget seen in the tube
> [Drawing: a copy of a Tube advertisement — a uniformed woman carrying a case labelled "PHONOTAS", with the words "DO WE CALL ON YOU TO DISINFECT YOUR 'PHONE / IF NOT RING HOLBORN 5000 / PHONOTAS LTD / HIGH HOLBORN", and "(1257)" beneath.]
> with lots of love your loving son
> David

*Notes:* He arranges evening building-construction classes at the Cambridge Technical School for the new year and sees the regional appointments officer about a grant: the first steps towards architecture. His Tube-poster sketch is of an advertisement for Phonotas Ltd, telephone disinfectors.

## 1946

### 06. A Wednesday, c. mid-January 1946 ('flu)

*Undated ("Wednesday"). He has a 48 on "25 Fri Jan and 26 Sat" (25 January was a Friday in 1946) and expects leave "from about the 24th February", "about six weeks" away: so mid-January 1946. He is now an LAC.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 LAC Silver
> 4206 S.E.
> R.A.F. Oakington
> Cambridge.
> Wednesday
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> I have been in bed all since I last ~~saw~~ wrote to you, with 'flu but I am up now and all right. There is an epidemic of it in the camp and it caught me or I caught it. It wasn't too bad, my temperature didn't go much above 100° all the time.
> I went to work for the first time yesterday and to-day is my day off.
> Of course I needs must have 'flu the week I was going to start those building construction classes.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I was to fix it up a week ago yesterday (Tuesday). But I was in bed. So last night I rode down to Cambridge, thinking that the building constr. teacher would be there every Tuesday
> They told me he was in class at seven and that I must wait till eight thirty to see him. I went off to the Y.M. and came back at 8.30, when finding that he wasn't in class after all it turned out that he'd not come at all that night - so I rode back, fourteen miles altogether for nothing.
> I am going into Cambridge again and hope to see him to-night. Apparently this technical school is much bigger than Exeter Art School. They do building constr up to third year
>
> *(p. 3)*
> as far as I can make out and have different people teaching different parts of it. So if I can get to those classes it might really help a lot. It's going to be an awful struggle to get there three times a week.
> That chap who goes to London gets away early in the afternoon but I can't get excused any time.
> I have a forty eight on 25 Fri Jan and 26 Sat but that's not much use for coming home. They never seem to give me week ends.
> With any luck I shall get my leave in about six weeks, I mean to get it from about the 24th February if I can - that's when it's due.
> By the way I got those certificates.
> I enclose two drawings. I don't quite know why.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> One's a bit macabre, though it was meant to be funny. The other one isn't even meant to be funny. I don't really know why I send them only I'd like to show them to someone now that I've taken the trouble to do them.
> I wonder how things are going about as usual I suppose.
> Although I've had such a lot of leave recently I feel as if I could do with some more right away, this year started all wrong with me.
> I am sorry that this letter is so depressing it's this beastly 'flu I expect
> With lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] P.S. about the episcope those lights will work like that BUT at only half their normal strength (ie in series)
> [Drawing, bottom right: a lamp flex hanging from the "ceiling" through a "light socket" to a two-way adaptor holding two bulbs, with the note "The other way is better →".]

*Notes:* The two drawings he enclosed are not with the letter. The P.S. about the episcope is a wiring tip: two bulbs in series on one flex will each light at only half strength.
