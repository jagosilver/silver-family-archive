*← [Silver Family Archive](./)*

A week after D-Day, [David Silver](David-Silver) arrived at **RAF Oulton**, near Aylsham in Norfolk, as **AC2 Silver** of 1699 Flight, a unit that trained crews on the B-17 Flying Fortress. These thirteen letters to his parents at **Tresillian** in Exeter run from June 1944 to Easter 1945, between his [Little Horwood letters](Little-Horwood-Letters) and his [Oakington letters](Oakington-Letters).

This is the year he set out to become an architect. On the education officer's advice he writes to the R.I.B.A. In August a **War Office correspondence course in the History of Architecture** comes through, with a Cambridge lecturer as his tutor, and through the autumn and winter he buys a drawing board and T-square in Norwich, draws Egyptian temples in a borrowed office in the evenings, and wishes he had more time: "it would take me a year to do a couple of months work at peace time speed." By day he sits "in the mud on a tin can under a tarpaulin", hates being an AC2, and longs for a commission in meteorology.

He cycles the Norfolk lanes, counts poppies and skylarks' nests, and is moved by Norwich Cathedral. He finds his hut-mates talk only of "football, riveting, aeroplanes, and beer". He hitch-hikes back from leave through the night on petrol lorries and a milk lorry, "seated on a churn". He hunts Norwich for a birthday present for his mother and is defeated by a shop assistant with a green jug. He draws the coast at Treknow, where his parents are on holiday, to settle an argument about a headland. And he imagines what record he would ask for if he were posted overseas: a voice saying "This is Exeter, Exeter St David's, the train standing on No 1 platform is for Torquay, Newton Abbott and Plymouth."

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. Most of the letters are dated from the events he mentions; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. One sheet is missing and two pages were damaged in scanning. A letter from RAF Cosford and two pages from his Bruton schooldays, which were filed with these, are given at the end. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it.

## 1944

### 01. Saturday, c. 17 June 1944 (just arrived at Oulton)

*Headed "Saturday Night". His first letter from Oulton, "after several days of shunting around" since leaving Little Horwood (where he expected a new address on 6 June), and censored (censor's signature at the foot). He has nothing for his mother's birthday (22 June), so probably Saturday 17 June 1944.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> 1699 Flight.
> R.A.F. Oulton
> Aylsham
> Nr Norwich
> Saturday Night. Norfolk.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> Having just telephoned you I am now going to write a letter. After several days of shunting around I seem to be settled in and now I must try to get this letter censored and off to you. I expect your letters will be censored to me as well.
> I am so sorry that you got those note books back again just after sending them, could you hang on to them for the time being.
> Writing a lot of letters isn't very popular round here, probably because it means censoring a lot. Anyway I owe one to Mr Martin, James, Nanny, Kit
>
> *(p. 2)*
> and the Newhouse matron who wrote and told me that two of my companions in the study had been killed in the war.
> I mustn't mention any service matters but it doesn't really matter as I loathe everything to do with the service anyway. The country round about is beautiful, very much more so than I had expected and it is very much more wooded.
> I regret my movements about only in one respect, no sooner have I really settled in and got comfortable than I have to start all over again.
> My bicycle though feeling its ten or possibly eleven years of life is going quite well and saves me a good deal of rather dull walking.
> To-day I picked a sprig of honeysuckle which I have in my button hole (pocket). I am sure that the inmates of my hut none of whom I have ever seen before
>
> *(p. 3)*
> think that I am rather peculiar because I like reading and flowers. However it's nothing to what I think of them so I don't really care. If I had only my painting materials there would be some marvellous landscapes, but anyway I am in need of new brushes and more paper, both of which are practically unobtainable so it doesn't matter. There are a number of very good architectural subjects round about.
> Aylsham is a very very small town, it calls itself a town but it is no larger than Bruton. However it does boast a cinema, a very large number of "pubs" a post office, stationer, two chemists and perhaps most prominent of all, at least to the nose, a fish and chip shop, which should I want it, I could easily find from at least ten miles away.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> Cromer is ten miles from Aylsham, whether it is in a defence zone or anything like that I know not.
> I miss my regular day off ~~[struck]~~ and the possibility of future visits to London. I spent such a long time evolving wonderful schemes to occupy my free time, all to no purpose. Perhaps one day I shall go to Norwich but however interesting it may be it can never take the place of London.
> I did see Song of Bernadette at the New Gallery, I had a very good seat in the Dress Circle and thoroughly enjoyed it.
> There is a music circle here very different from the last which I used to look forward to very much. Nevertheless though the music is lighter and the technical arrangements to say the least - unusual - I enjoyed it, particularly the commentary
>
> *(p. 5)*
> given by a Squadron Leader in between records.
> There doesn't seem to be the usual well equipped reading room which I always used to like, here. However I will say that the R.A.F. do pretty well on the whole with regard to books and films and perhaps I was spoilt on my last station.
> In a few minutes I shall go to bed as my eyes are becoming so "hazy" that I can scarcely see the page. This happens to me, not infrequently nowadays, and I attribute it in part to the R.A.F. glasses which are not as good as my own. There are no baths here which I consider a very poor show. However if I was over the other side of the channel, there would be no beds as likely as not.
> It's about time we finished this
>
> *(p. 6)*
> war so that I can get in some of my time in the army of occupation prior to demobilization!
> I must do my laundry up before I go to bed so must stop now before the lights go out. I am afraid I have nothing for your birthday my dear Mummy, and if I had I should have to have it examined by the censor and would not be allowed to send anything with it by way of a note How irritating it is! as it makes one feel like a criminal. Fancy if I bought you a book or a picture it would have to be examined for security reasons! I shouldn't be surprised if censorship cuts down the number of parcels for the post office better than any poster campaign
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left, censor's signature:] E. Butt[?]

*Notes:* The Newhouse matron (his house at Bruton) had written to tell him that two of his old study companions had been killed in the war. Mr Martin was a Bruton master. *The Song of Bernadette* he saw at the New Gallery cinema in London before leaving.

### 02. Tuesday, c. 20 June 1944: a birthday letter to his mother

*Headed "Tuesday", wishing his mother "many happy returns of your birthday" (22 June), on good notepaper. Kit is at home with her. Probably Tuesday 20 June 1944.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> 1699 Flight.
> RAF Oulton.
> Nr Norwich
> Norfolk.
> Tuesday
> My dearest Mummy
> This letter is to wish you many happy returns of your birthday. I am writing to-day to make certain that it gets to you in time.
> I do hope that you have a lovely birthday and I am so glad that Kit is at home with you to celebrate it. I have nothing for you I am sorry to say as it seems very difficult to get anything from here. Had I only known that I shouldn't always have the advantage of London shops I should have bought you a present ages ago.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I tried in Norwich yesterday but although it is an old town and one would expect good book shops etc I don't seem to have seen any there.
> It seems to consist of a castle, river, a number of music shops, three forces canteens and a lot of americans overshadowed by a lovely cathedral. I didn't really look at anything I wanted to, when I got there as one seems to be wrestling with one way streets and light signals and Americans directly you get into the rather narrow streets. I went into an antique shop that had a lot of glass in its window and asked if he had any sets of tumblers of good ordinary glass, not cut glass. He had grey hair, a greyish moustache (the man in the shop) a grey suit but no tumblers.
> Then I went into a sort of Colson's
>
> *(p. 3)*
> on a smaller scale, where there were some passable tumblers of ordinary thin glass, I asked if there was a set of them and the woman seemed immediately to have an insane desire to fetch the jug from upstairs. I foolishly tried to calm her, it only affected her more deeply and she rushed off upstairs to search for the jug.
> Downstairs a doubt was creeping into my mind about the tumblers. I began to dislike them. In my mind's eye I saw Daddy with a disgusted look on his face putting one down on the table after drinking from it. I decided to have nothing further to do with glass. On reaching this decision the woman came downstairs
>
> *(p. 4)*
> brandishing a horrible green jug. "This isn't the one" she said by way of defence as she met my eye "This is a green one like it". I thought, what's the good of that, to myself and decided to go. But she wouldn't let me, she entreated me to wait while she sent for the plain one. It was rather hot and she had horrid teeth and the green jug looked horrible so I said I'd rather not. She looked a little hurt and said that perhaps I could come in and see it later before they closed at 5.30 pm. I said that it was possible and made my escape. At 5.30, coward that I am, I was sitting in a very nice seat, in Norwich's ABC cinema, watching my old friend Spencer Tracy.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> He was in a film with a very un-promising name. "A guy named Joe". It was only Spencer Tracy's name that attracted me, but though impossible and very weak in parts, I thoroughly enjoyed it. My mental ability is on the decline and I think very possibly on my discharge from the air force I shall seek sanctuary in Exminster! I find life rather dull, a triple cold and very lacking in knowing anybody worth knowing. The whole of the time I have been in the air force I've scarcely met a soul who was in the very least bit worth talking or listening to.
> They will talk about such terribly uninteresting subjects as, football riveting, aeroplanes, and beer. None of which subjects really interest me.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I picture you picking flowers in the garden on a lovely summer day, perhaps the most becoming occupation of any. I do hope it's fine on Friday for you.
> Do tell me what you would like [torn away]. I liked Ca[mbridge? torn away] good shops and was nice, rather like Exeter was once. I wish I had a lovely book of paintings or poetry for you. I should like to do some more painting, I miss it rather.
> I think we shall finish with Germany soon.
> With lots and lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* Colson's was the Exeter department store. "Exminster" is the Devon county mental hospital, a family joke. A strip of tape has torn away part of two lines on the last page; "Ca…" is probably Cambridge, which he passed through on his way to Oulton.

### 03. Thursday to Wednesday, July 1944

*Begun "Thursday" and added to on a "Monday" and a "Wednesday" ("it must be well over a week"); "Considering that this is July and the East Coast".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC2 Silver D.R.
> 1. [sheet number]
> 1699 Flight
> R.A.F. Oulton
> Nr Norwich
> Thursday. Norfolk.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> My mind begins to turn to Summer Holidays again. Sometimes I almost catch the sound of the sea but it's really the wind stirring the tree tops close to my hut.
> Talking of summer holidays - I did have a bathe the other day which I quite enjoyed although a large piece of inland water does not compare favourably with the Atlantic breakers.
> Thank you so much for your
>
> *(p. 2)*
> letters.
> The poppies in this part of the world have been marvellous, covering whole fields. I have known of one skylark's nest, one partridge's, and two house martins. In fact I've watched the progress of the young birds as they've grown larger and larger.
> Today all the colours are so vivid that if you close your eyes they leave a burning impression on your mind. They are the colours which you never believe to be possible in Dufaycolor. I see a notice about sketching and photography but as far as I can make out it only applies
>
> *(p. 3)*
> [Sheet number:] 2.
> at any rate as far as sketching is concerned to military objectives R.A.F., army, and naval things. I doubt very much whether anyone objects to my drawing trees.
> The library must have opened just after I arrived, as now it seems to be fairly well under way. I can see Punch and the Sphere and other magazines and papers almost any evening. They have asked for volunteers to help in opening the library ~~[struck]~~ in the evening so I gave in my name as ~~[struck]~~ the only way you can get any idea of what's happening in the outside world is by living in the reading room.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> Monday.
> I enjoy riding round the country in the evenings and have ridden about a hundred and twenty miles this week past. There's a one inch map in the reading room which is very useful. Unfortunately owing to Defence Regulations, naturally, the East Coast is a restricted area. So there are a lot of places which I should like to go to which are 'verboten'. I hope to go to Wroxham which as far as I know is all right. It's only about 10 miles from Aylsham. As you know Aylsham is fairly close to me here.
> I should like to go to Norwich some time and see all the things worth seeing, but I don't know enough
>
> *(p. 5)*
> [Sheet number:] 3.
> about anything to know what to see. I wish I knew some more about architecture I've written to the secretary of the R.I.B.A. on the advice of the education officer. I wanted to find out about War Office Postal Courses to prepare for R.I.B.A. exams. It is possible, I find to take both the intermediate and final examinations if one is a registered probationer of the R.I.B.A. The trouble is, ~~what~~ about the preliminary exam? Had I only known this at first I might have done something different at the Art School to prepare for it. The real trouble is that they ought to have known all about it and to have suggested it in the first place.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> There seem to be no more good sable water colour brushes in the world.
> I can't think to what "scene in the hairdresser's" you refer, Mummy dear, in speaking of a drawing in your letter. I remember the people with egg shaped heads and ~~[struck]~~ am so glad that at last someone appreciates the joke which I remember thinking at the time was very funny. (It must be two years ago.)
> Please tell Christopher that he is welcome to paint 100 ft square pictures with my materials if he so wishes. I am so glad he does take the opportunity of a spot of painting as I know of nothing whatever in the world that is so pleasing or so utterly
>
> *(p. 7)*
> [Sheet number:] 4.
> satisfying.
> Has he taken a look at the book which I showed him when I was last at home, The twenty five shilling one about Water Colour painting, I am sure he would find it very interesting and at once encouraging and at the same time agonizing in its excellence. (because one can't paint as well)
> The woods are marvellous round here and were it near home I could spend many a happy day covering yards of Whatman.
> There is a good shop in Norwich but it has not decent brushes although it has imperial sheets of paper.
> I do hope Christopher does some drawing and painting as it
>
> *(p. 8)*
> make me feel a little as if I am; if I can hear of his experiences.
> I still find some colours very perplexing and difficult to distinguish from others. I find that whereas I was writing this in a crowded Naafi I am now alone so conclude that I am late for work - it is lunch time.
> The music circle has failed me or Ensa who supply the records. I wondered what was wrong with Ensa and now I find that it is run by Naafi which is the rottenest organization and the most incompetent I have ever come across in the whole of my life.
>
> *(p. 9)*
> Ensa gave a free showing of "The Young Mr Pitt" here. It was just about all it was worth as the film broke down five or six times and the sound track was very poor. It must have given people who hadn't seen it properly a very poor impression. Properly shown it's one of the best I've ever seen.
> Considering that this is July and the East Coast I am disappointed in the weather. I thought Exeter was the only place with hot steamy thundery weather.
> I am now drinking a mixture of coffee and tea as I omitted to wash my cup free of tea this morning and inadvertently had some coffee poured on top of it. It makes no difference however, one can only ever tell that it's coffee because it costs 1½d instead of a 1d
>
> *(p. 10)*
> [Sheet number:] 5.
> Please tell Captain Silver that I am sorry he is still anchored to the sofa by his plaster hoof and that I hope he will soon be able to do without it. I remember drawing a leg with the bones in it once but don't remember much about it except that I drew the toe bones like this (from the side) [tiny sketch] which seems to have been very simple. Benjamin West made paint brushes out of cats fur - you say in your letter Mummy, I hope that the cats round Oulton won't look very bald in the near future.
> I've just finished the Sea Tower by Hugh Walpole, I can't say I thought it very good. Hugh Walpole is always exactly the same and although this book is present day instead of "30 years ago" his favourite time, it is written exactly as if it were "30 years ago"
> This is a rotten letter isn't it? I keep being ~~interpted~~ interrupted.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> [Top:] P.T.S. [?]
> Wednesday
> You must be wondering when on earth you'll get a letter from me by now as it must be well over a week. It's my fault as you have to get letters censored on the way to work before 9 a.m. and I keep forgetting to put this one in, so I add a bit more on each time.
> I'll post it by nine a.m. tomorrow.
> I should think my teeth need looking at as nothing has been done to them since I left home. I've now read "the Director" by L.A.G. Strong. It was very well reviewed I believe, in the Sunday Times. It's quite good but not as good as I expected and the end is rather disappointing. It's about an extraordinary film director in Ireland and smacks a little of George Birmingham though it isn't funny.
>
> *(p. 12)*
> If (this) is like (the rest of Norfolk), I like it. [The two phrases circled, with arrows.]
> I did see Felicity some time ago but have only a very sort of Ponds Vanishing cream impression of her. I could get to Chelmsford via Ipswich but am not allowed to as it is banned.
> Fancy Dinah being accepted for the WRNS. at least - I mean fancy her being old enough I should think the W.R.N.S. is about the best of the womens services to be in - at least I never see them sitting outside pubs drinking beer with Americans. She ought to get a commission if she's old enough.
> What's the matter with me, why can't I get one? I hate being an AC2 and I feel perfectly certain that I could do meteorology or photographic intelligence. What in the world they put me in such a totally unsuitable job for I don't know.
> It seems a long time since I was at home
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> P.S. Have seen Mrs Cavendish's cows [?]

*Notes:* He numbers the sheets 1–5. Sheet 5 ("Please tell Captain Silver…") has been placed after the coffee page: the order of the unnumbered last sheets is uncertain. "Captain Silver" with his "plaster hoof" is Christopher (Kit), then laid up with an injured leg. "I still find some colours very perplexing and difficult to distinguish from others" is his own note of his colour vision. He wants a commission in meteorology or photographic intelligence.

### 04. c. late August 1944 (incomplete)

*The first sheet is missing (probably the empty scan 0 133) and the letter breaks off at the end of sheet 8. He has nine days' leave "from after duty hours on the 6th of November 11 weeks or so hence. That is a Monday" (6 Nov 1944 was a Monday), so written about 21–23 August 1944, just back from leave.*

> *[One or more pages missing here.]*
>
> *(p. 1)*
> 2.
> I didn't find that out until too late and so took the 12.50 to Waterloo. It was timed to get in at twelve minutes past five My, last but one, train for Norwich leaving L.P. St at 5.6.
> In despair I turned to the Argosy to take my mind off wondering whether I should be late. I had read it all before!
> The train owing to delays did not reach Waterloo until about 5.45. The last train until 10 pm had left for Norwich. I got my pass signed to say that my train was late (I might have caught the five to six to Norwich if it hadn't been late) I took the first train to Ipswich arriving there at about 8.45.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> That's as far as it went. 3.
> I then started to hitch hike from Ipswich to Norwich. I got two petrol lorries which carried me between them about 15 miles, and one lift in a private car of about 5 miles. After that I got stuck, nobody would risk stopping to pick one up at 11.30 at night although they saw me. It was more of a hike than a hitch and I walked to Diss Station where I caught the original ten o'clock from London at 2.10 am I arrived at Norwich at just after three. I was supposed to be back at midnight.

I explained my difficulties omitting the hitchhiking to the S.P.s and rightly, they let me go without a word. If the train hadn't been late I should have been back by 9.30 pm the previous night.
> It was lovely to come home and here is some more good news. All being well
>
> *(p. 4)*
> 5.
> I shall get nine days leave as from after duty hours on the 6th of November 11 weeks or so hence. That is a Monday.

> Now some more good news my War Office course for History of Architecture has come through, My first papers, and I am to be the pupil of an F.R.I.B.A. lecturer to Cambridge University etc. etc He will henceforward deal with all my work and all correspondence will be between ~~him~~ he and ~~me~~ I ? his name is L. ### 05. Friday 1 – Sunday 3 September 1944

*Headed "Friday": the Allies "are crossing into Belgium" (2–3 September 1944), and it is "66 days till I come home" (to 6–7 November). The Sunday postscript says censorship from the station has just stopped and the coastal ban is lifted.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC Silver D.R.
> 1699 Flight
> R.A.F. Oulton
> Nr Norwich.
> Friday.
> Dear Daddy and Mummy
> Thank you very much for the parcels letter etc.
> I went to Norwich on Monday to see whether I could get a drawing board, T square, books and cartridge paper. I got the cartridge paper. I have to get a permit for the board I can't get the T square till I know the size of the board and I've ordered the books. Christopher's book is one of the ones recommended for the course.
> I had lunch in a jolly nice restaurant which I had noticed on previous visits.
> There was a combined atmosphere of Elands and the antique shop in
>
> *(p. 2)*
> [Top half illegible: scanner fault.]
> exhibition of Ballet Design. The original sketches for ballets scenery etc. It is a travelling exhibition and should it come to Exeter it is really well worth seeing. It was interesting to see the different mediums used - from pen and wash to poster colours. The latter were very popular, probably because you can paint out mistakes and they give an appearance of solidity
>
> *(p. 3)*
> That museum is worth a visit every time I go to Norwich.

### 11. Friday 1 – Saturday 2 December 1944

*Dated "Dec. 1st" and continued "Saturday". The last four pages ("How do you like this writing paper?") are an extra sheet sent with it: Christmas presents and "next leave … in 30 days" fit the same week.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 AC Silver. D.R.
> 1699 Flight.
> R.A.F. Oulton
> Nr Norwich.
> Dec. 1st
> Dear Mummy and Daddy
> Thank you very much for your letters the bicycle pump and the braces.
> I am so sorry that I never looked in the air raid shelter, particularly after you mentioned it. I can't think however I forgot. I could have done it that last day easily.
> What a lot of water too, I could kick myself. I don't like to think of you without a serviceable shelter although as a matter of fact judging by the past year or so I don't think you need worry. Would the N.F.S. pump it out? I dare say they might jump at it as they can't have much to do but how would they get a pump round there. I suppose they could lay a pipe from the front garden where the pump would be.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I am writing this letter in my newly acquired "study". The other chap has just gone to the Salvation Army Canteen and I with presence of mind have avoided the need for going with him by having something to eat before at the Naafi.
> The dental officer's name is K. W. Mays. I went to see him as I had a very painful ulcer in my mouth at the front which didn't get any better and made mealtimes miserable. It was about this size O and slightly yellow in colour and was just in front of my two front bottom teeth [tiny sketch: lower lip, ulcer, tooth].
> He put some stuff on it, told me to come back again if it hurt and I went. It went too. He was quite pleasant and I see where all the dental equipment goes. It would have done credit to your surgery. I can still find the place where the ulcer was but it hurts no more. Other people had told me how he'd got rid of ulcers for them. He looked at my teeth and used a probe on one or two but never a word said he.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> Saturday
> I am glad I didn't post this letter as I got yours to day. What a muddle the gardener made of that business about the Laburnum and Lilac. I am glad you scored off him over the apple crop. Incidentally he gave me an 'Ahple' just before I went. I never eat it, I don't know what I did with it.
> How just like James to send you all that glass. What would you like for your birthday and Christmas, Mummy dear, there aren't any nice little books anywhere. I believe the Exeter shops are better than the Norwich ones. The shopping centre has not much been affected by the bombing here either. I've thought of a little experience which might amuse you and forgotten to put it down several times. I had it on the way back from Norwich last week. I got into the bus which was almost full. The seats are not as in our buses but are in one long line facing
>
> *(p. 4)*
> inwards with their backs to the windows. There was only one place vacant up at the end and I went to this. An oldish farmer-looking man moved up a little and made a place for me. Notice, he was on my right. I sat down and as is my subconscious custom swung my left leg across my right knee. This would have been all right if his habit had been the same as mine but it was in fact the reverse. He swung his right leg across his left knee. His right foot and my left foot met in mid air between us. There was a fairly loud thud as boot met boot. The rest of the bus shared the entertainment and he and I both smilingly apologized to one another. For five minutes after this I had to suppress hysterical giggles inside me as I re-told the story in my mind ready to write in this letter. Perhaps it won't amuse you but I thought it rather funny
> with lots of love. David.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> How do you like this writing paper? I could get you some if you want.
> I had a look at the shops on my day off and could find nothing suitable for Christmas for anyone. No good books, although there were stacks of useless ones. They were mostly of the "How to keep Bees" and "A Hundred Handy Hints on Hens" variety.
> There were other books but nothing really worth having. The architecture though temporarily renewed with another lease of life is difficult with no one to correct you as you draw. That year at the art school though not as useful as it would have been in peace time else where nevertheless had its points. I would never have had the courage to start on a course like this before it.
> I have not heard anything about this
>
> *(p. 6)*
> met. course yet but have not lost all hope. I think I'll get a commission in the end if the war is long enough as I fear it will be, but I can't imagine how or what in. I seem to be more confident now than I was when I first came into the RAF and I feel as if I could order anybody about and not stand any nonsense if I wanted to and not just be able to order public school boys but anybody.
> It used to embarrass me when people noticed that I didn't speak in the same way as they did but now I don't care a straw what they think and I am not interested in anything my companions have to say. It's funny but I feel a lot better able to look after myself.
> But at the same time it's made me a lot less confident ~~[struck]~~ as a civilian so that I am awfully self conscious if I go into a restaurant or anything like that. However this isn't exactly news but the trouble is that there isn't any I just go on being here and nothing happens
>
> *(p. 7)*
> that I can tell you about.
> We've had a day or two of not bad weather but now it's going stormy again.
> I don't know whatever I'll do if and when I get posted from here as I have so much equipment in the way of drawing boards T squares books etc. I could get home on my day off for from 12.0 midnight till 5.0 the next evening and I could but it would be rather expensive for so short a time, it's about 600 miles home and back. I have visiting Philip and Kit in mind but so far have not had a chance of seeing the time table. I wonder if Kit would hate it if I did in uniform. I do wish I could get a commission. If only there were the courses I could get one in my own job.
> I am afraid this is a very
>
> *(p. 8)*
> poor letter. If I get my next leave it will be in 30 days time, not so long, if I am not posted on a course before then. What a rotten interview that was at Penarth. I've seen people since who'd been there, whom an officer interviewed, I wish he had me.
> If you were sitting here with me you'd be amazed at all you can see and hear. The cigarette lighter manufacture is doing well hundreds of new lighters leave the R.A.F. lighter manufacturers daily also wooden engines, small blackboard and easels, parrots that swing on a bar and other toys, the station is in full production. Air raid shelters are packed with big red wooden engines such as Christopher and I used to have. Somebody's children will have a good Christmas this year as they are well made and brightly painted in standard R.A.F. dope!
> I'll close down now I think
> With love
> your loving son
> David

### 12. Tuesday – Thursday, late January 1945 (snow)

*Headed "Tuesday" and "Thursday". He has applied for leave from 7 March and will hear "about February 8th (Phil's birthday)"; "How the Russians are going ahead" (the Vistula–Oder offensive, from 12 January); roofs off and four-foot drifts (the great snow of late January 1945). Probably 23–25 January 1945.*

> *(p. 1)*
> R.A.F.
> Oulton.
> Tuesday.
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> There doesn't seem to be much news this week. The chief thing is that I've applied for leave for March 7th for nine days. We had to fill in a form at short notice and so I pushed it forward as far as I dared. I hope this won't clash with anything. I think I'll get it then all right.
> The Education Office has organized a room, with a fire for those on correspondence courses (I seem to be the only one who works at his) and so I've abandoned the compass adjuster's office to my relief as his reminiscences were getting to be more than I could bear.
> I suppose you're having the same weather as us. We've had roofs off due to the wind and four foot drifts of snow
>
> *(p. 2)*
> Thursday.
> Still snow hard-packed on the road sometimes 8" thick and in deep drifts in the fields. Snowing again now. I am once more in the hut which once proved such a mystery to you, I don't expect to stir outside again to-night. It was bitterly cold and damper this morning.
> I shall know definitely whether my leave will be granted for March 7th about February 8th (Phil's birthday). I quite expect it to be. My application for meteorologist has gone through to Records Office and so it rests with them. I shall stir them up via the meteorologist office if I don't hear anything soon.
> How the Russians are going ahead. Talking of going ahead I wish I could get on with my Architecture. I am lucky if I get four nights a week free.
> No doubt you have seen things in the paper about 90% of the R.A.F going overseas. I think that there's little doubt that I shall be among them and I imagine it will be in the first half of this year so that's one of the reasons why I am pushing my leave
>
> *(p. 3)*
> back as early as I can in the quarter. This leave will be the third week in the quarter and so you see I am not doing badly considering my first leave was the 12th and last week in the quarter.
> I am doubtful whether I shall get another one in after that before I get embarkation leave but if I do it will be in the middle of May. I heard something on the wireless to-day about recordings for forces overseas ~~forces~~ it went something like this "Now here is a recording specially made for Cpl John Smith in Ceylon. He asked for "thrushes singing in the rain, so here it is" etc It made me wonder what I'd like if I was out there and I decided upon a very much less romantic thing. I thought I'd like to hear a voice saying "This is Exeter, Exeter St David's, the train standing on No 1 platform is for Torquay, Newton Abbott and Plymouth, This is Exeter, Exeter St David's" etc I always like hearing that as I dash out of the train and hurry along through the crowd of people jostling to get out and think how to them it's just another station.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> The snow is getting again quite fast. Last night I went down to open the library at the spot which used to be another mystery to you. Another chap went with me on his way to the music circle. We came off twice (we were riding bikes - in case you didn't know and thought we were riding horses) The roads are very treacherous. So Wiggs has moved again I should think she's a bit chilly where she is now, anyway you'll be able to tell pretty well what sort of weather she's getting.
> I want to try to save £50 this year if I can which will make my total capital about £125 I think.
> I wish I got more drawing practice although from what I can make out other architectural students are not for the most part exceptionally good at freehand drawing but for that matter there are not a lot of architects who are really good at design. Did I tell you I got two good books by The E.U.P. on roofing and constructional details, quite cheap too on good paper,
>
> *(p. 5)*
> I am getting quite thrifty in my old age and know my own mind a lot better than I used - the effect of looking out for yourself I suppose in the R.A.F.
> I see Tommy Handley has 'flu.
> I didn't go to Norwich on my day off but did a lot of things like tidying up my belongings (which are very numerous and live mostly under my bed) and seeing the Education Officer about the library which is in a shocking state some books having been out since July. I wish I could have a go at putting it right but there's a regular person who does it in the day time.
> I get so fed up with wasting time with nothing to do sometimes during working hours yet not being allowed to go away and get on with my own work. I have now a waterproof one piece suit with a zip fastener down the front.
> [Sketch: a figure in the one-piece suit.]
> Well that's about all for now
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

> I am almost sure that he was the strange looking gentleman who was sheltering from the wind at the end of the Starcross pier in a tin waiting room just before we got that steamer to Exmouth last November Daddy. > His lecture was illustrated by lantern slides and was very good indeed. There were about 200 in the audience of this rather small hall. It was rather like being in the Guild Hall. He told us about Tintoretto, El Greco, Velazquez
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Rembrandt and various others.
> He lectures every Tuesday (a course of ten lectures) and I shall try to go again. I enjoyed it very much so that the time flew and it didn't seem long enough.

> I hadn't been sitting there very long when an old man with a dish cloth appeared and I had to hold my cup saucer and plate in mid air while he laboriously wiped underneath. I felt that this was perhaps ~~[heavily struck]~~ a hint to go so I withdrew to the safety of the reading room.
>
> *(p. 6)*

> I should think it will be pretty brisk down by the sea as we've had a gale warning two days running now. I dare say the white horses are out, I hope it keeps fine for you.

> Well it's an hour less tonight so I think I'll turn in now. 10.45.

## A letter from RAF Cosford

A letter from RAF Cosford, near Wolverhampton (an earlier posting, probably early 1944), filed among the Oulton scans. He has just come back from a 48-hour pass home; "48 hrs on March 4th" is noted at the head and the foot. It will move to its own page when the Cosford letters are transcribed.

> *(p. 1)*
> [Top left:] You Must Not let you know who IN. [?]
> [Below:] 48 hrs on March 4th
> [Top right:] Please address Kit's airgraph and post it next time you get a minute to do so. Cosford.
> Tuesday
> Dear Daddy & Mummy
> I got back here in time by the last train from Wolverhampton.
> I arrived at Bristol about a quarter of an hour late and not knowing which platform my train left from enquired of my old friends the R.T.O. there being no porters about.
> Never again! must be my motto. There were two or three air force people all enquiring about trains to Cosford. I could scarcely believe my ears as I came in on hearing them told that they would not be able to reach Cosford until the next morning.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> They went out and just to make certain I asked again. They told me the same. The 6.55 for Birmingham would leave No 12 platform but there would be no train leaving for Cosford until morning. I began to experience a slight sinking feeling but determined to ask the railway officials. At the Booking Hall I was stopped by the R.A.F. service police. To my relief it was only to ask whether I was the chap they had told to go to No 14 platform because if so, it wasn't No 14 it was No 12. Poor chaps!
> I patiently explained that I wasn't although it struck me the policeman looked as if he didn't believe me.
> I asked if we were allowed to use the civilian enquiries office and I was [?] we are not.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> He became quite genial and directed me to it, asking me later whether I had found out all I wanted there.
> The railway official was of the opinion that life was one big joke and the R.T.O had been invented to make him laugh. He informed me that I could reach Cosford by 12.35 a time I had never heard of before. I told him about the Exeter Enquiry Office and he looked it up again. This time he got a different result and mentioned the trains I already had in mind. Much relieved I caught the 6.55. It was nearly a near thing at B'ham as the other train was waiting.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> however after everybody in our train had panicked and rushed across the bridge to the other side and got into the train, it waited about ten minutes for the train I would have come on at 4.20. Whether or not this made its connection I don't know but anyway I was glad I had taken the early one as it eliminated a certain amount of nervous strain.
> The train to Wolverhampton was very fast and I changed stations there and caught the next train to Cosford having forty minutes to catch it in.
> Over and over again it was worth coming home. I am so glad I did and thank you very much Daddy for paying for the fare and also for the money.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> All the people in my hut were confident that I should not get the connections and that I should not have any time at home. They thought I was mad to go - but that's because they are all Scots. I ~~[struck]~~ think that the feeling about madness is mutual.
> I keep thinking of it all and how lovely it was and being so pleased that I dare think about coming home again as it is not so far off. I still think it's four weeks Daddy, but I'll find out definitely before I post this letter.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> I bet you've noticed this crest isn't in the middle of the page by now Daddy! It's only a Naafi block.
> Mummy, everything was lovely and I am enjoying thinking about the hot water bottle so don't be sorry about that as now I shall always remember that incident and the occasion will stand out in my memory because of it.
> I shall have to cut that hedge again Boss. The soil here is light and strangely, red. James says it new sandstone or something and that Devon is clay on old sandstone.
> I am on magnetos they are awful. I feel twice as good as I did before I went home, as if I'd had a lovely holiday, well I have really!
> with love
> your loving son
> David.

## Two pages from Bruton

Two loose pages from David's schoolboy letters from King's School, Bruton (see the Bruton letters), filed among the Oulton scans: the end of one letter, and a page about the headmaster showing prospective parents round.

> *(p. 1)*
> brush my hair quite a lot as we have firework displays in the dormitory to see who can make the best sparks jump off his hair.
> I go for quite long ~~[struck]~~ rides now, can you tell me anywhere worth going to within a radius of 15 miles of here?
> Thank you very much for all the food and things you brought up on Sunday, and the jellies and drinks were the nicest I have ever had on a Sunday leave. Must stop now I'm afraid there isn't much news.
> with love
> your loving son
> David
> [Bottom left:] I see that Jubilee went down somewhere off our coast.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> He shows the school off to people who may send their sons here like a beastly guide book, and every one loathes the sight of him. While I'm writing this, in the library he has come in with two parents and a minute prep school boy. The boy looks as if he might like to sink through the floor ~~[struck]~~ and I don't wonder because his mother goes into ecstacies over the most trivial things and the beak eggs her on telling her of the beauties of Batcombe and Brewham and they never think for a second whether the wretched boy cares two hoots for Batcombe church tower or not. I wish Tom was headmaster.
