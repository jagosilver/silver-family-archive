*← [Silver Family Archive](./)*

In the summer of 1942, **Edwin Joseph Acott**, then eighty, wrote two long letters on family history from his home in Acton, west London. He had retired after forty-five years with the Great Western Railway. The letters went to his niece **Muriel**, daughter of his brother **Rupert Philip Acott** (1853–1941), a draper who had settled in **Exeter**. Muriel had asked where the family came from. Edwin answered with the **Bakers**, his mother's family of yeoman farmers at Bampton, Oxfordshire, on **16 June**. On **23 August** he followed with the **Acotts**: Edward Acott, an Oxford builder born in 1781; Edwin's father Charles Henry, a printer at the University Press; Edwin's twelve brothers and sisters; and his own life.

The Acotts matter to this archive through **Gladys Lucy Acott**, who married the Exeter dentist Clifford Marking Silver. She was the mother of [David Silver](David-Silver) and [Christopher Patrick Silver](Christopher-Silver/). Neither letter names her, but the Exeter connection suggests she was another of Rupert's daughters, which would make Muriel her sister. That is not yet confirmed.

The letters survive as scans in a folder labelled *"Peter's Letters"*, together with a typed family tree. Both letters are transcribed in full below, keeping Edwin's spellings, his ampersands and his underlining (shown here as **bold**). Original page numbers appear as *(p. n)*, and doubtful readings are marked [?].

## Letter 1 — the Bakers (16 June 1942) — full text

*Six numbered pages, black ink. An "appendix" to an earlier letter of 30 May 1942 (not in the folder).*

> *(p. 1)* 15 Grasmere Avenue,
> Acton. London W.3.
> 16th June 1942.
>
> Dear Muriel,
>
> This is an appendix to my letter to you of May 30th which should begin with "These are the generations of –" but first – I will say that I do not know the Christian name of the father of my Grandfather Baker but I would guess it to have been either John, Charles, William, or Henry – most probably John. I do know that the mother's name was Hannah. I believe the family had been (yeoman) "farmering" people for generations.
>
> **Charles Baker**, my **Grandfather**, was born on August 16th 1792 & died on April 25th 1884 (St Mark's Day). He was married to his Cousin **Mary Baker (née Belcher)** who was born on May 21st 1807 & died on April 25th 1875. She was the daughter of a farmer whose establishment was at – or near – Fyfield Wick, Berkshire.
>
> Their children were:–
>

> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1. | Anne Amelia | 21.2.1828 | 25.11.1915 *(My mother.)* |
> | 2. | Martha Leah | 2.6.1829 | 7.3.1873 |
> | 3. | John | 5.12.1830 | |
> | 4. | Matilda | 24.7.1832 | |
> | 5. | Reuben | 4.8.1834 | |
> | 6. | Thomas | 6.2.1836 | |
> | 7. | Charles Henry | 19.6.1837 | May 1838 *(written "1938")* |
> | 8. | Fanny | 26.12.1838 | 11.10.1840 |
> | 9. | Joseph | 16.10.1840 | *Went to Kansas U.S.A. Died 1904.* |
> | 10. | Mary Ellen | 21.8.1842 | 13.9.1876 |
> | 11. | Albert | 4.12.1844 | 24.3.1845 |
> | 12. | Charlotte | 7.5.1846 | |
> | 13. | Charles | 15.10.1849 | |
> | 14. | Hannah | 23.4.1852 | |
>
> I always understood (from my mother) that there were fifteen in her father's family & I think one child of which I have found no record must have been "Still born".
>
> 2. **Martha Leah** did not marry. When about 30 years old she "drifted" into religious dementia & gradually became permanently incapacitated.
>
> *(p. 2)* 3. **John.** Market Gardener. Found a wife amongst the household staff (Cook probably) of Squire Fox of Bradwell Grove. This Fox was the proprietor of the famous Paragon Umbrella manufactory – I remember him as High Sheriff of Oxfordshire. This Uncle John had one daughter "Frances Mary" who married William Goswell, son of a Twickenham Market Gardener. You may remember this Cousin Mary who lived at Crowmarsh near Wallingford. She is now a widow with one daughter (unmarried) living at St Margaret's, Twickenham.
>
> 4. **Matilda.** A devout member of the "Plymouth Brethren" persuasion. Married to her Cousin John Baker a person of the **very opposite** persuasion and the liveliest member of the Baker family that I ever knew or heard of was my Uncle-Cousin "Grocer" John. He "graduated" in Grocery business at Oxford, was established in a Grocery at Bampton by his Uncle (my Grandfather's brother) William. Failed; at Abingdon. Travelled for a wholesale Grocer, left that to travel for a wholesale Wine & Spirit merchant, then tried his luck as a Grocer at Wallingford, left Wallingford for a similar business at Bampton & finished up as an Old Age Pensioner – at Bampton. Both Matilda & John lived until past 70. They had one daughter who died unmarried aged a few years beyond 30. This Uncle-Cousin John had a brother William & a sister Leah; the latter died unmarried, over than 70, but I know nothing about the brother William excepting that he was a Grocer at Market Lavington.
>
> 5. **Reuben.** A favourite brother-in-law of my father whose name prompted the name of Rupert for your father. Reuben was employed in his early years at Bromsgrove but, later on, met with grievous disappointments of some kind & gave up work entirely; lost self control & self direction but lived to be 80 or thereabouts.
>
> 6. **Thomas** we nephews called "Uncle Tom Peck", why "Peck" I cannot tell unless it was because, at the tip of his nose, *(p. 3)* he had a small pit with a hair growing out of it. He married a personage of evident importance named Jeeves (or Eeves) who died early leaving one daughter. He was married a second time to his cousin Amelia Baker & they had one son whom you will remember as the Tom Baker who was employed for some time in the business at Exeter. I have heard nothing of this Tom Baker since that time; his half-sister (Hannah) left her home when her father married a second time, & went to live with her mother's people, in Wiltshire, & I think she died some years ago.
>
> Uncle Tom Peck helped on his father's farm before he married & did some little farming at Brize Norton afterwards but for many years he was engaged as a Miller – manager of Burford Mill for the important firm of "Jacobs". Towards the end of his life he went into business at Birmingham (Corn Merchant I believe) until he died suddenly in the street in that town.
>
> This Aunt-Cousin Amelia had a brother Joseph the two being children of one of my Grandfather's brothers. This particular Joseph "horrified" all his relations by "running away" & enlisting in the Royal Marines at Portsmouth, a dire offence & disgrace only perpetrated by abandoned villains in those days. This innocent – & lucky – Joseph only did six weeks soldiering, however, when, apparently, it was discovered that he could "write & read & cast accompt" & then he was transferred to the "Army Commissariat" (whatever that may have been) & thence later on to the "Excise". Many years after he retired with a Civil Service pension and the rank of Captain which latter he would never recognize as a title but only accepted in order (as I was informed) that his widow – if any – should receive a Captain's widow's pension. You may remember this very Cousin Joseph as a resident at Honiton Clyst, Devon.
>
> 9. **Joseph.** This Uncle Joseph was my Godfather and lent me his name for my second one without asking me whether I wanted it or not. He was married to Julia Gough and emigrated to America (White City, Morris County, Kansas) *(p. 4)* some time between 1863 & 1870 and resumed farming but in a more extensive manner than he had been accustomed to at home. He had two daughters but what became of them I am unable to say.
>
> 10. **Mary Ellen.** A greatly beloved aunt who was Godmother to our Mary Ellen (Aunt Nell). When Aunt Mary became engaged to be married she decided to leave home and procure employment to earn money to help in setting up the anticipated house keeping. In this she suffered a grievous disappointment as her prospective partner transferred his affections elsewhere. You may have heard of this Aunt Mary as having established herself at the George Müller Orphanage at Ashley Hill.
>
> I think you probably know about Aunt Charlotte, Uncle Charles, & Aunt Hannah so I need not go into details regarding these three. Aunt Charlotte, married to Uncle Frederick William Taunt, was the mother of Cissie Taunt of Bampton, & of her brother Roland Frederick now resident in Canada – married, but has no children.
>
> I have no record of the dates of death of seven of my uncles & aunts but I think they may probably be found amongst the remnants & relics of the Baker family in Cissie Taunt's keeping at Bampton.
>
> In addition to the father of John, William, & Leah, and the father of Joseph & Amelia, my Grandfather had a brother Henry who had children Henry & Fanny (& possibly others), but I know nothing of this Henry Baker or his family. There was also the brother William (unmarried) whom I have already mentioned & a sister Leah both of whom lived to be very old. This Great Uncle William also had farm lands near Bampton, and was chiefly remembered by the "younger fry" from his habit of carrying a stock of lion shillings (William IV coinage) to distribute when *(p. 5)* visiting Nephews & nieces.
>
> My Grandfather was Church Warden at Bampton (St Mary's) for many years & during his term of office it was decided to restore the church & pay for it by public subscription, my Grandfather & another being accepted as Guarantors for payment. The amount of money collected fell considerably short of the sum required, the second Guarantor died, & my Grandfather had to bear the cost; as an outcome of this & his long service to the Church he subsequently received from the Community a grand huge Bible & Prayer book.
>
> A good deal of my Grandfather's farm land was Church property which he had held on lease for very many years until a certain Vicar by his persistent recommendations to the Eccles: Comm.rs caused the lease to be transferred to another person. This, and bad farming times, caused the decline & final extinction of farming in the Bampton Baker family. I knew this Vicar – Mr Hunt, who came to Bampton ~~from Plym[outh] … Devon~~ – I sang in his Choir for 12 months (1872–1873). The most remarkable thing about him was that he had Seventeen children. I did not like him. He had one book – full of sermons which seemed to be his whole library in this class of literature. I remember that one of these sermons introduced the Story of Androcles & the Lion; I heard it several times. I did, however, like Mr Hunt's boys & girls.
>
> I well remember leaving Bampton at the end of this long visit: Grandfather gave me two sovereigns to take to my mother one of which was to buy flannel to be made into shirts for me & the other to be taken to a famous Oxford doctor (Giles) who was to be consulted about a chronic cough which troubled me – & others. (a "Churchyarder" my brothers called it). I was proud of the shirts & glad of the rapid success of Dr Giles' ministrations.
>
> I could write a great deal more upon this *(p. 6)* subject but must now stop, if only to get breath. I will answer your question about the Acotts in another letter a little later on.
>
> Kind love to you from Ella and
> Your affectionate Uncle
> Edwin.

*(Pencil sums in the margins of pp. 1 and 6 — 1885/1849, 1870/1825 = 45, 1894… — are a later reader's age calculations, not part of the letter.)*

## Letter 2 — the Acotts (23 August 1942) — full text

*Thirty-two small numbered pages, pencil-grey ink — "Acott Pedigree, etc., as promised".*

> *(p. 1)* 15 Grasmere Avenue
> Acton. London W.3.
> 23rd August 1942.
>
> Dear Muriel,
>
> Acott Pedigree, etc., as promised:–
>
> Your Great-Grandfather **Edward Acott**, entered into this life at Coleshill, a village on the Cole river about 3½ miles W.S.W. of Faringdon, Berks. on the border of North Wilts. He was born in Nov.r 1781 & died on April 27th 1858. I have found no record of his parents. He was married on October 2nd 1809 to **Mary Weston** who was born on March 18th 1784 & died on August 24th 1851. Mary Weston's mother Jane Weston died at the age of 60 on January 5th 1812. No other records of the Weston family, whose home was at – or near – Fyfield, Berks., where Great-Grandmother Baker's family were *(p. 2)* farmers.
>
> Edward & Mary Acott established their home at Oxford in Gloucester Green – a place far different in 1809 from what it was when you may have known it.
>
> Edward Acott was a builder, house decorator, carpenter, etc., & must have been in a fair way of business – sometimes at any rate – as I have a record of his having been engaged in building a house for a gentleman in East Anglia – (Norfolk, I think – I cannot find the particulars at the moment).
>
> The children of Edward & Mary Acott were:–
> James – born August 9th 1810
> Jane – " August 9th 1812
> George, born 29.10.1814 died 21.4.1821
> Anne born 26.10.1816
> Charles " 10.12.1818 died 20.3.1825
> George " 3.5.1821
> Henry " 26.5.1823 died 4.4.1825
> *(p. 3)* And **Charles Henry** – your Grandfather – Born June 7th 1825, married May 14th 1850, died August 19th 1870.
>
> Uncle James Acott's wife was aunt to Sir Robert Buckle [*sc.* Buckell], a former Mayor of Oxford. I have not got the date of Uncle James' death but he lived to be quite old. He had one son and, I think, two girls, – the latter had no children but the son Edward who was in the Civil Service (Home Office Section) married a school mistress & had eleven children. One of these attained a good (clerical, not professional) position in the Great Western Railway Engineering Department from *(p. 4)* which he retired about two years ago, & died recently leaving no children. This was a man of excellent repute as also his father was. One other member of this group whom I knew of was in Lloyds Bank but I have not come in contact with any of the others.
>
> Aunt Jane Acott was married to Ingram Shrimpton, bookseller, printer, & publisher of Oxford, who with his family emigrated to New Zealand in 1853. Shrimpton had previously (in July 1851) sent his son (John) with workmen, type, & material, to start a newspaper; (in 1853) he sold his Oxford business to Parkers the well-known Oxford publishers. The (New Zealand) paper was *(p. 5)* called the "Timaru Herald" but it subsequently became the "Canterbury Times". Shrimpton subsequently sold his interest in this enterprise & became a sheep farmer. For many years my mother corresponded with her sister-in-law, Jane Shrimpton & her daughters. The old lady had eleven children & in one of her letters said (at the time of writing) she had one hundred descendants.
>
> I haven't much information about George Acott (the second) who was a Shoemaker, i.e. a maker of boots & shoes besides being a Cobbler. He married the daughter of a prominent Oxford (High Street) tradesman & had a son & three or four daughters; it is very many years since I heard anything of them.
>
> You probably have knowledge of the famous old Aunt Anne who was for many years *(p. 6)* housekeeper for the Vicar of Wishford (Wilts) – the Reverend Edward Hill – when her Vicar died his son (also the Reverend Edward Hill) found her a satisfactory "retiring" place at Salisbury, where she lived to a good old age.
>
> These are the sons & daughters of my father & mother:–
> *[squeezed in above the list: "Chas. Henry Acott Born May [sic] 1825 / died Aug. 1870"]*
>
> | | | Born | Died |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1. | Charlotte Jane | 6.11.1851 | 10.3.1932 |
> | 2. | Charles Henry | 5.11.1852 | 3.3.1871 |
> | 3. | Rupert Philip | 24.10.1853 | 3.2.1941 |
> | 4. | Arthur Edward | 11.3.1855 | 11.4.1879 |
> | 5. | Edgar Vernon | 17.8.1856 | 10.10.1876 |
> | 6. | Sydney Mark | 31.1.1858 | 6.9.1913 |
> | 7. | John Allan | 13.11.1859 | ? |
> | 8. | Edwin Joseph | 3.9.1861 | |
> | 9. | Mary Ellen | 23.1.1863 | 16.2.1942 |
> | 10. | Howard Spencer | 27.3.1865 | 13.3.1905 |
> | 11. | Edith Grace | 11.11.1867 | 10.9.1900 |
> | 12. | Herbert Stanley | 19.11.1868 [?] | 23.11.1868 [?] |
> | 13. | Anne Amelia | 30.4.1870 | 24.8.1870 |
>
> *(In the original the dates sit half a line above the names they belong to; aligned here by count and checked against the text — Edwin "not quite nine" in Aug 1870, Howard d. March 1905. Herbert Stanley's year is hard to read and may be 1866/1868.)*
>
> No. 1 & No. 2 were born at Long Wittenham, Berks, No. 3 at Bampton (Oxon), and all the others at Oxford.
>
> Although I was not quite nine years old when my father died I have a vivid recollection of the *(p. 7)* last four or five years of his life. Also I have three volumes of diary, notes, & memoranda relating to his life from 1839 to 1863. From these it is easy to judge that he was a man of high principle and correct conduct; a keen politician, ardently Conservative, & loyal to Queen, Constitution, Church, & State, as by law established, and with evident & ample respect for properly constituted authority, – and a very proud man withal. He was a great reader – especially of newspapers – & well versed in current events, for these exercises having constant recourse to the Oxford City Library. As Churchman he was a conspicuously regular attendant, taking with him such of his children as he considered old enough to go, & seeing that the latter attended Sunday afternoon classes.
>
> He was much addicted to walking exercise, not merely in & near Oxford City but further afield to *(p. 8)* Bampton, Fyfield, Cumnor, Bablock Hythe, etc. In these expeditions he was nearly always accompanied by one, or two, or three, or four, or five, of his children. There were Grandfather Baker's people to visit at Bampton, Grandmother Baker's & Grandmother Acott's people to visit at, or near, Fyfield.
>
> During all the years that I remember up to 1913, when our last Oxford home was broken up, we had a water colour sketch of my father which was "done" in 1828 when he was three years old. He is represented as standing in Gloucester Green (Oxford) holding a hoop & stick, with his hat lying on the ground beside him; rough herbage in front & trees (poplars) in the background. Your father had this picture when Aunt Nell came to live with *(p. 9)* me (in 1913) so you will probably remember what Grandfather Acott looked like in 1828 when he was three years old. Before sending this picture to Exeter I had a coloured photograph of it made for Nell's satisfaction – & my own, of course.
>
> At the age of 14 my father was apprenticed to Mr Thomas Combe, master of the Classical side of the University Press (Oxford). This Mr Combe was a descendant of that friend of Shakespeare to whom the latter left his sword. This Mr Combe committed the dire offence of flooding Port Meadow by work which he caused to be done at Wolvercot Paper Mill; to expiate this crime he built a Chapel to the Radcliffe Infirmary. This same Mr Combe, & his wife, were patrons & supporters of the young artist William Holman Hunt, *(p. 10)* by reason of which Mrs Combe was enabled in later years to present Holman Hunt's famous picture "The Light of the World" to Keble College. Mrs Combe was very kind & helpful to my mother when my father died as well as during his illness.
>
> My father's indentures covered a period of seven years (I have the original document) during which he gained complete knowledge of composing & printing as exercised at that time, after which he remained at the Press as a Compositor for only six months, and left with an excellent reputation.
>
> In 1847 he joined his brother-in-law Shrimpton (a much older man) in a scheme for teaching girls composing, Shrimpton then owning an extensive business known as the Crown Yard Printing Office – in Cornmarket Street, the *(p. 11)* noted Dr Giles being also a party to the scheme. Dr Giles was "Curate of the first portion of the living" – (whatever that may mean) at Bampton (Oxon) & the scheme was carried on at the Vicarage house there. Dr Giles was an Anglo-Saxon scholar engaged at that time in putting into modern English the works of William of Malmesbury, Bede's history, & the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. I have copies of each of these works & it interests me to think that my father had something to do with the supervision & printing of the first proofs of these works. The girls employed were educated at the Bampton National School & my father notes "it was astonishing with what rapidity they took to the business, soon being able to perform as much work as journeymen – with wages from 4/- to 7/- per week" (!!!). It was at this period my father & mother *(p. 12)* first met & came to certain conclusions.
>
> After a few months at Bampton the promoter of the scheme (Shrimpton) induced the parties to remove the business to Long Wittenham; he had been lodging at that place during the summer & considered it easier of access than Bampton from Oxford & therefore less expensive. At this juncture Dr Giles was paid out of the undertaking. At Wittenham the business went along satisfactorily & my father, as supervisor, had a fair amount of spare time during which he became acquainted with the leading people of the village & neighbourhood joining with them in their outdoor pursuits – as:– shooting, fishing, cricket, quoits, & hunting with the Berkshire hounds. At this time, too, he became interested in tillage, occupied an extensive *(p. 13)* garden & joined brother Shrimpton in renting several acres of heath & underwood for clearance & cultivation.
>
> In October 1849 when out shooting with his young nephew – John Shrimpton – my father had a serious mishap which left him minus his right thumb – blown off by an explosion of his powder flask through a most absurd act. Standing near a spot where rough grass was burning for clearance purposes, – fire apparently dying down – thought to liven it with a pinch of gunpowder – let some out into the mouth of his flask holding the spring tightly closed to prevent too much coming out – tipped what was free on the fire – & bang!!! Loud explosion of four ounces of enclosed gunpowder heard in a radius of three miles. Shock and loss *(p. 14)* of blood nearly cost him his life & certainly altered his whole career afterwards. He had now to give up his farming and gardening but brother Shrimpton assured him that the printing business should always be carried on vigorously & there was no reason for him to look for any other source of income. Having in mind these & many other assurances my father resolved to get married & did so on May 14th 1850.
>
> In the following year, however, Shrimpton began to take more interest in farming, occupied more land, rented a large house & farm premises all this to the neglect & detriment of the printing business; also he began to get into difficulties through "having too many irons in the fire". My father now began to express his concern at the drift of things and Shrimpton signified his intention of emigrating to New Zealand in the near future offering at the same time to furnish my father with sufficient stock to enable him to do business on *(p. 15)* his own account. The offer was accepted &, my father left Wittenham with the keenest regret & established himself as a Printer & Stationer at Bampton. The promised stock was very slow in coming along & the full complement thereof never arrived. The business was not a success; there was already an old-established printer there & not enough work for two; also Dr Giles' printing work (which was resumed) was so badly paid for that receipts barely covered the expense thereof. At this period my father conducted a night school for the village.
>
> In 1853 he became manager of a business at Faringdon leaving my mother in charge of the shop at Bampton; this engagement only lasted eight months.
>
> In April 1854 the family removed to Oxford where my father engaged himself at the Crown Yard Printing Office (Parkers). From thence he transferred his services to the University Press where he became Overseer on the Classical Side. *(p. 16)* I do not know when this last change was made but it was certainly after December 1863, probably quite soon after.
>
> I have a perfect recollection of the place where the type setting was done at the University Press; a long dark dreary room lit by primitive unclean gas which smoke-blacked the walls & ceiling, & of an office permeated with the smell of printers' impedimenta, as:– printer's ink, printer's roll, etc. fresh, & stale; intense heat in summer, & freezing in winter. – "No Factory Acts" in those "Charles Dickens" days.
>
> Another feature of Oxford life was the annual flooding of cellars, sometimes up to four feet in depth, & lasting for weeks at a time, the families above living over a stagnant pond. My father changed his home four times in a few years but *(p. 17)* never got away from these floods until he was dead.
>
> On December 20th 1856 John Shrimpton – the nephew of my father who was with him when he "gunpowdered" his thumb off – was accidentally shot by his younger brother Walter. They were out shooting (in New Zealand of course) John walking in front, & Walter behind, the latter tripped & fell. Gun discharged & lodged its load of shot in John's heart.
>
> Now what about our family? **Charlotte Jane** was Charlotte Jane because that was the name of the ship in which young John Shrimpton sailed – yes literally **sailed** – to New Zealand.
>
> **Charles Henry** was apprenticed at the University Press – and *(p. 18)* died before he was 19 years old.
>
> **Rupert Philip** in his youth wanted to be a sailor & for some time walked bow legged because he thought it was the proper thing for a sailor. He was apprenticed to Rickarby, Draper, – (Corner of Little Clarendon Street & Woodstock Road) and hated the business, especially when he had to walk out with a bale of cloth & a yard stick. I think he went from Oxford to Hurrells at Warminster, thence to Baker, Baker & Co. Bristol, & then to Exeter. While at Oxford he sang "basso profundo" at S.S. P. & J. Church. Both he & Charles Henry were educated at the Oxford City (Bluecoat) School. School Master Hughes – commonly called "Bucketty" but why so I do not know.
>
> *(p. 19)* **Arthur** was a small laddie, quiet-speaking, merry, humorous; first went to business at Badcock's Queen Street Drapery in the Counting House, & afterwards in the Office at Field's (Shillingford) Brewery. Once had to deal with a letter from Cuddesdon Palace signed "J. F. Oxon:" & addressed the reply to "J. F. Oxon Esq." Result a blazing complaint from the Right Reverend John Fielder Mackarness, Lord Bishop of Oxford, who apparently hadn't the wit to recognize an innocent mistake.
>
> As a Railway Officer I came in contact with his Lordship several times & he always seemed to me to possess a little more than proper self-respect, – rather reminded me of what the 18th Century Parson Charles Churchill wrote about Bishop Warburton. Churchill himself, however, was far from being a saint – or even an honest *(p. 20)* Parson.
>
> **Edgar Vernon** has probably revealed himself to you somewhat in those (of his) letters which you mentioned. At the age of 13 he became an amanuensis to Dr Joseph Bosworth, Anglo-Saxon Professor, Ch. Ch. Coll. Oxon, and Rector of Water Stratford, Bucks, who was engaged in compiling an Anglo-Saxon dictionary. Edgar advanced amazingly in learning & intelligence in the old gentleman's service & developed great devotional respect and affection for him. It is clear that the Doctor valued his young assistant's services for in his will he left Edgar £200 (equal to £400 or £500 of present-day's value) to enable him to go through the University. But the old Doctor died in 1875 or early in 1876 & Edgar – as you see – did not live long after him. Dr Bosworth's Estate (£30,000) was thrown into Chancery owing, I believe, to family disagreement, and the Court ordered certain amounts to be paid out to my brother during his illness which *(p. 21)* payments amounted to £30. Subsequently the balance £170 – was ordered to be paid out for equal distribution between my mother & her nine surviving children, the younger ones to receive their shares upon reaching majority.
>
> I expect you know a good deal about **Uncle Sydney** who left school on his own responsibility when he became 13 and found a post for himself at James Russell's music shop where in due time he became Manager before opening his own business at No. 126 High Street a few doors away from Russell's. Uncle Syd's adventures, sayings & early "sweethearting" would fill a Punch Almanack. His grandmother Baker used to tell him he would "go all round the orchard & pick up a crab"! At one time he considered himself engaged to a niece of Sir George Nares, the Arctic Explorer, and used to exchange letters with her by placing them behind a loose *(p. 22)* brick in a wall near the young lady's academy.
>
> I should say here that Arthur, Edgar, & Sydney were Choristers at S.S. Philip & James Church, which belonged to St John's College, by reason of which they were educated at St John's College School. This School was under the direct control of the President & Fellows of St John's and gave a super excellent training.
>
> **John Allan** as a chorister at St John's College Chapel was also educated at St John's College School. He was a good scholar, an exceptionally able vocal musician, but unfortunately unstable in other directions. After some years as a treble & tenor at St John's Chapel he transferred to Christ Church Cathedral, thence to York Minster, thence to Salisbury Cathedral, thence to the American (U.S.) Embassy Church at Paris. After that *(p. 23)* the deluge (!) – Emigration to Canada – Oblivion.
>
> Then **myself** – 8th child, 7th son & as such a grievous disappointment where a daughter was desired; a morsel composed mainly of fright & nerves for which imminent extinction was pronounced several times; rescued & revivified more than once (poor chap!) by a cunning nurse – perchance of the Sarah Gamp order. I well remember my first school – near our home at the north end of Walton Street – where on the first day of attendance I sat on my legs on a tall form in fear of the "School Marm's" cat. (There were no cats at my home then – only rabbits & white rats or mice.)
>
> Promoted thence in due time to St George's School & a seat in the Church Choir, this Church being a "Chapel of Ease" to the Church of St Mary Magdalen. The School Master was also the Choir Master – Mr Thorogood – and a thoroughly expert hand at thrashing. E.g. "I'll cut you in *(p. 24)* two, pretty near!" – but he never once thrashed me. At this School I came in contact with a Reverend Vicar (of Summertown) named Jones, then Diocesan Inspector of Schools, who was subsequently Consecrated Bishop of Cape Town, Metropolitan See of Cape Colony. This was the Bishop who deposed the first Bishop of Natal who was charged with heresy on account of his opinions as expressed in his treatise on the Pentateuch & the Book of Joshua. Dr Jones' decision was reversed & the alleged culprit was reinstated by the Court of Chancery. This Bishop of Natal was the famous Cornishman Dr Colenso of Algebraic & Arithmetic fame.
>
> "What! Shove Colenso by?
> Fifty thousand Cornishmen shall know the reason why!"
>
> After many & many a weary walk along the dreary length of Walton *(p. 25)* Street & Worcester Place, or alternatively by the other route:– Woodstock Road, St Giles, and Magdalen Street, I was sent to the farm at Bampton as I have already told you.
>
> I should say now that I don't know the present position of the Old St George's School but I do know that the last time I entered St George's **Church** I found it degraded into a very dirty, smelly Labour Exchange.
>
> I cannot remember how long I remained at Bampton on this occasion but think it must have been twelve or eighteen months in the years 1872–3. In this period there were – living at the farm – Grandfather & grandmother, Uncle Reuben, Uncle Charles, Aunt Charlotte & Aunt Hannah. The farm lands, some Glebe & some freehold, were dotted around the house within a radius of two or three miles – on the Witney road, Buckland Road, at Clanfield, & at *(p. 26)* Aston. There was a herd of cows, flock of sheep & plenty of pigs. A team – Violet, Diamond & Tom (the latter a blind horse) also Topsy & Dick – smart ponies. Farm hands:– **Shepherd** – whose name was also Shepherd & who had been with my Grandfather 56 years – **A thatcher** who was also hedger & ditcher whose name was Thatcher strangely enough, a **Carter & Stable-man** & a General labourer. There was a Malt-house connected with the farm my Grandfather being a licensed maltster.
>
> During this period I walked along the newly completed East Gloucestershire Railway (before it was opened for traffic) from Bampton to Alvescot & I saw the first train run under Brize Norton Bridge into Bampton Station on the day when it was opened.
>
> I may say that at this time Grandfather's eldest son John lived in Broad Street opposite the farm house & the prospective *(p. 27)* uncle – Frederick Taunt, the Organist, lived "round the corner" near the Church. This Uncle was engaged to my Aunt Charlotte for twelve years before they were married.
>
> I think it must have been in 1873 when I was sent home to go to the Central School which was governed by a Committee of University & City people. Dr Kitchen [*sc.* Kitchin] was the chief of these. This gentleman subsequently became Dean of Winchester & later Dean of Durham; he was married to a lady who came over in the train of the Princess of Denmark who became Princess of Wales & Queen Alexandra.
>
> My School master was C. M. Ridger [?] M.A. Balliol Coll: Second Master McLaren, also of Ball: Coll:
>
> In 1874 I gained prizes as Second boy in the Second Class, & Second boy in the First Class. *(p. 28)* In 1875 I was head boy of the School for the whole year & received two first Class Certificates & two Sovereigns, also first prize for taking the part of Brutus in the quarrel scene of Julius Caesar at the Public Prize giving in the Town Hall. This was virtually the end of my schooling.
>
> In 1876 I was "taken in tow" by the redoubtable Dr Benjamin Jowett – Master of Balliol, but in a very short time was fetched away to be appointed a junior Clerk at Oxford G.W.R. Station, in anticipation of "better prospects". There I remained from July 3rd 1876 until September 1879 when I was removed to Reading. Returning to Oxford in April 1881 I remained there until June 1889 when I went to Paddington where I soon became Staff Clerk in the Paddington Divisional Office *(p. 29)* from which Paddington Station & Yards were controlled. In December 1893 the Paddington Division was amalgamated with the London Division with myself still Staff Clerk at the larger office. Subsequently the London Division was by degrees extended to include all lines & branches from London to Marlborough, Winchester, Shrivenham (Berks), Aynho, & Fairford. I remained in this Office as Chief Staff & Wages Clerk until my retirement on September 3rd 1921.
>
> As Wages Clerk besides conducting the distribution of pay of Traffic Department Staff I dealt with the wages applicable to other Departments – such as Locomotive & Carriage, Engineering, Goods, Signalling, etc. The highest amount I dealt with in one year was over one million pounds and before "Bradbury's" came in we used the golden coin.
>
> *(p. 30)* So much for Uncle Edwin.
>
> **Aunt Mary Ellen and Aunt Edith Grace** and their histories are probably known to you.
>
> **Howard Spencer** distinguished himself at my latest Academy – the Central School – became a junior clerk on the G.W.R. at Oxford & subsequently at Reading; left the Railway & entered the service of a high class Estate & House Agent at Reading; and emigrated to the U.S.A. in 1892. In America for some years he worked "variously"; in a Gaslight Factory where gas was made from crude oil (from the Earth); travelled around with a peripatetic thrashing machine; took to farming & soon entered into cultivating on his own account. In 1891 [*sic* — perhaps 1897] his health failed (Asthma) & he *(p. 31)* returned to Railway Office work with the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fé Railway Company where he quickly climbed to the position of a Chief Travelling Auditor. He was married in 1898 to Elizabeth Reeves (otherwise Auntie Pink) and they had three children, two of whom died in infancy. On Friday March 9th 1905 he was stricken suddenly while at work in his office, carried home insensible, and died on the following Tuesday (Mch 13th) "Cerebro-spinal meningitis". Auntie Pink still lives, so does her son Melvin Edward who was born on July 15th 1899, is married, but has no family; he is well-established professionally at Hominy, Okla: U.S.A.
>
> The cause of my father's early death *(p. 32)* Phthisis, supposedly attributable to loss of blood when he lost his thumb but in my opinion really induced by the horrible conditions under which he (and millions of others) lived and worked in "the good old days". My eldest brother, Henry, also died of Phthisis; Arthur of heart disease; Edgar of tuberculosis (bowels); Edith of the same; Sydney – as you probably know – of cancer (throat) this latter the first & only cancer I have heard of in our family.
>
> Volumes more could be written around the "sketch" that I have given but I think you will have had quite enough so I will say kind love to you from Ella & I. finis, & Good bye.
>
> Your affectionate Uncle
> Edwin.

---

## Notes

- **Edwin Joseph Acott** was born on 3 September 1861 in Oxford, the eighth child and seventh son. His second name came from his godfather and uncle, Joseph Baker. He was head boy of Oxford's Central School in 1875. He then joined the GWR as a junior clerk at Oxford station on 3 July 1876, and retired on 3 September 1921 as Chief Staff & Wages Clerk of the London Division. **Ella**, who sends her love in both letters, was presumably his wife. His sister Mary Ellen ("Aunt Nell") lived with him from 1913 until her death in February 1942.
- **Rupert Philip Acott** trained as a draper in Oxford, then worked in Warminster and Bristol before settling in Exeter. He married Charlotte Rowell (1851–1921).
- **Edwin's own slips are kept and marked:** "Buckle" is Sir Robert Buckell, Mayor of Oxford, and "Kitchen" is G. W. Kitchin, later Dean of Winchester and of Durham. He also dates Howard's emigration to 1892 but his ill-health to 1891.
- **Readings still uncertain:** the Central School master's name ("Ridger"?) and the dates for the infant Herbert Stanley.
