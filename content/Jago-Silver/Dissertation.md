# Authorship: is 'software art' challenging our accepted notions?

*Jago Titcomb*

## Introduction

Software art appears to offer a different exchange between author and
audience, and apparently eschews fine art's elitist attitudes. It
provides a more democratic, 'conversational' relationship between the
creator and the viewer and blurs the boundaries between the tool and the
artwork created with it. This dissertation will attempt to look at
whether software art specifically and digital media more generally,
challenges our accepted ideas about authorship and 'the original' work
of art. It also intends to look at the ways in which software art does
nothing to challenge the ideas of authorship. For example the way in
which sites displaying these works are obscure and difficult to navigate
and how the artists themselves are anonymous. Most importantly I will
look at the way in which works themselves require the audience to have
the technological knowledge and correct equipment to view them. These
methods of restricted coding are in some ways even more pronounced than
in other areas of art, despite the apparent democracy of the Internet.
In the following discussion I will also look at the way in which viewing
software art is reliant on various 'filters'[^1] such as computers and
the software running on them (Microsoft Internet Explorer, Macromedia
Flash etc.) and whether this detracts from its validity as art.

Software art is a collective term for any piece of artwork that is made
using a programming language. It covers all forms of interactive
Macromedia Flash and Shockwave websites, as well as works created with
pure programming languages like C++, Lingo and Perl[^2]. It can also be
used to describe computer games, of both the free online variety and the
more commercial type. Software art can range from simple visual
representations of mathematical formula to highly complex interactive
environments. The websites I am concentrating on are *Potatoland.org*,
S*odaplay.com*, *Stanza.co.uk* and *Turux.org.* These sites all showcase
examples of software art in different degrees of interactivity.

*Potatoland.org* is Mark Napier's site and showcases various examples of
his work[^3]. These include interactive systems that let the viewer
create artworks, a web browser that creates new web pages out of
existing ones, and various semi-interactive journeys through pages of
web-inspired imagery. *Sodaplay.com*[^4], created by Soda Creative Ltd
gives the viewer a set of tools and rules with which to make a
'soda-creature', which can then be saved in the 'soda-zoo' for others to
see. The 'creatures' consist of geometric shapes and lines that can be
assigned different behaviours and reactions. Variables like gravity,
oscillation and speed can also be played with. *Stanza.co.uk*[^5] is a
collection of web based art works and music that tend to use imagery
very specific to digital 'computer-culture', such as application
'windows', icons, pixellated file format buttons (JPEG, GIF, HTML etc.).
The artworks here tend to be less interactive and more evolutionary or
generative[^6]. Often they will open in a new window that fills the
screen and presents you with a barrage of ever-changing imagery.
*Turux.org*[^7] is a difficult site to describe, but it appears to be a
gallery or repository of online interactive software art by a large
range of different and often anonymous artists. The work contained
within it varies widely but includes many generative and
semi-interactive artworks that build up images over time or in response
to mouse movement.

## What Is an Author?

In common usage an 'Author', for most people is the original creator of
a piece of work, as Donald E Pease says:

> "It can refer to someone who starts up a game, or invents a machine,
> or asserts political freedom, or thinks up a formula, or writes a
> book. Depending on the activity and the application, the term can
> connote initiative, autonomy, inventiveness, creativity, authority or
> originality."[^8]

There is a generally accepted idea that an Author is the sole source of
their work, and the title has also been applied to God, as The Author of
everything. Throughout history however, various philosophers and critics
have challenged this notion of the Foucaultian 'Author-God'. This
'Author-God' creates original works from their own imagination and
presents them for an audience to admire. It is a one-way flow of
information from author to audience, with the author (perhaps
arrogantly) assuming definite ownership over their creation. There is no
doubt about who is the author and who is the viewer, the difference
between them is very polarised. Lev Manovich describes accepted our
notions of the artist as author, as:

> "*A Romantic/modernist artist is a genius who creates from scratch,
> imposing the phantoms of his imagination on the world*.[^9]"

The idea of the artist creating original works from his own imagination,
works that were purely his vision, is central to the traditional views
of the author. In truth this view is a little Romantic, even in the
Renaissance when artists first needed to be recognised as craftsmen in
their own right, they were actually painting or sculpting under
patronage so it could be argued that the views and opinions of their
patrons influenced their work. The image of the divinely inspired
individual genius has been flawed from the start. Historically there has
been a need for authenticity as pieces of work or writing could only be
authenticated if they were attributed to an author. An anonymous written
report or painting had no value, regardless of its content or
accomplishment. During the Middle Ages this idea became central to the
emerging area of scientific thought. A report by a scientist was judged
not only on it's content but also by its authors name. The name of the
author was a measure of the report's value and truthfulness.

> "Text, however, that we now call "scientific" ... were only
> considered truthful during the Middle Ages if the name of the author
> was indicated. Statements on the order of "Hippocrates said..." or
> "Pliny tells us that..." were not merely formulas for an argument
> based on authority; they marked a proven discourse."[^10]

The role of the author has changed through history and by subject. In
science the importance of the author diminished as texts were
increasingly judged on the merits of their content. But in literature
and art the author has always been of paramount importance; how was one
to criticise a piece of art or writing without the background knowledge
of who the author was?

> *"...Literary discourse was acceptable only if it carried an author's
> name; every text of poetry or fiction was obliged to state its author
> and the date, place, and circumstance of its writing. The meaning and
> value attributed to the text depended upon this information. ...
> Literary anonymity was of interest only as a puzzle to be solved as,
> in our day, literary works are totally dominated by the sovereignty of
> the author."*[^11]

Roland Barthes, in 'The Death of The Author' dramatically reduced the
sovereignty of Foucault's 'Author-God' by denying the idea of a "*single
'theological' meaning*" intended by the author:

> *"We know now that a text is not a line of words releasing a single
> 'theological' meaning (the 'message' of the Author-God) but a
> multidimensional space in which a variety of writings, none of them
> original, blend and clash. The text is a tissue of quotations drawn
> from the innumerable centres of culture."*[^12]

Barthes also recognised the importance of the reader (or viewer, or
audience) in the work of the author, which is particularly appropriate
to software art with its conversational relationship between the author
and the audience.

> *"Classic criticism has never paid any attention to the reader; for
> it, the writer is the only person in literature... we know that to
> give writing its future, it is necessary to overthrow the myth: the
> birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the
> Author."*[^13]

Barthes went on to say, that with the death of the author, the critic
was also irrelevant, and that perhaps the traditional view of the author
had reigned so long because it suited the critics:

> "Once the Author is removed, the claim to decipher a text becomes
> quite futile. To give a text an Author is to impose a limit on that
> text, to furnish it with a final signified, to close the writing. Such
> a conception suits criticism very well, the latter then allotting
> itself the important task of discovering the Author ... beneath the
> work: when the Author has been found, the text is
> 'explained'---victory to the critic."[^14]

This is what has led to the elitist attitudes that surround a lot of
fine art today, after all if you can criticise a piece of work purely on
it's content then it becomes accessible to anyone. However if, in order
to appreciate the work fully, you need to know the background, politics,
race, religion and views of the author it restricts the critical
audience greatly. Software art, with its anonymous author's and lack of
apparent intentions, removes this restriction. However it does impose
other restrictions of it's own.

## Does 'software art' offer a challenge?

Wimsatt and Beardsley,[^15] who thought that anyone, who based their
criticism on biographical information about the artist rather than their
work, was committing an 'Intentional Fallacy', later challenged the
ideas of 'intention'. The 'Fallacy', is argues that in order to
criticise the artist's work you must know what their intentions were.
Software art agrees with this principle, as it is very hard to discover
anything about the intentions, background or influences of the artists.
You have to judge the work on its' own. It also proves the point of
their criticism in a slightly different way. Due to the way in which you
view software art, you can never be entirely sure that what you are
seeing is what the artist originally intended and their intention
therefore becomes irrelevant.

With the appearance of 'New Media', and 'Media Art' in the early 90's,
many previous ideas of authorship were turned on their head. Like Pop
Art before it, Media Art uses the content of commercial media as the
base for its work. The art is not related to the real world but to the
world of media, or to the way the media portrays the real world. This
secondary view of the world diminishes the role of the author, as there
becomes no definite originator to a piece of work. Instead the work is
merely the latest product in a chain of events, waiting to be picked up
by someone else and used again. As Lev Manovich says in Generation
Flash[^16], some media artists, who claim that there is no such thing as
an original work of art, have simply accepted the idea that originality
is impossible to attain.

> *"...Post-modern media artists accept the impossibility of an original
> ... their subject matter is not reality itself, but representation of
> reality by media, and the world of media itself. Therefore these media
> artists not only use media technologies as tools, but they also use
> the content of commercial media...*[^17]*"*

Software art is in many ways a return to the modernism of the Romantic
artist; it eschews media art's dependence on other media for a starting
point and instead creates something from scratch, more of which, later.
Whilst the idea of an original work is hard to define in digital terms,
the software artist's work is definitely not wholly reliant on the world
of the mass media.

This discussion has been primarily focused on *Potatoland.org*,
*Stanza.co.uk*, *Turux.org* and *Sodaplay.com*, but there are many more
sites with a way of working. All of these sites are blurring the
boundaries between what is a tool and what is an artwork, what is media
and medium. These types of artwork can be quite obscure, Black and
White[^18], at *potatoland.org* is an image-generating interface that
uses the information on the CNN website to create a swirling hypnotic
pattern. The information on the CNN site is read in binary by the
software artwork. Every '0' causes a black pixel to move horizontally,
every '1' causes a white pixel to move vertically, the black and white
pixels are also attracted to each other. This can appear to be very
mechanical but it doesn't take long to see that the scriptor has made
some aesthetic decisions. The lines drawn are not blocky and pixellated
but blurred and smooth, because of this as they start to build up they
create a wonderful sense of volume and solidity. Though the processes
used may be technological and mathematical the end result is
aesthetically pleasing, as Nate Burgos says,

"*Aesthetically driven, mathematically generated*"[^19].

Perhaps Mark Napier (the artist behind *Potatoland.org*) intended the
work to highlight the fact that we are all the authors, because the
image is generated by the news in the world that day, which we may all
have had a small part in.

The results of these works are truly 'original' pieces in that they
would be impossible to reproduce exactly, (and in this respect they are
an anomaly in the digital world). Although they have been produced as
part of an automated process, the scriptor has no control over the input
(in this case the world news on the CNN site). This makes these works
more like traditional "one off" pieces of art, in that they are
impossible to replicate, but they remain very different in that the
artist cannot control the input. What can be misleading is that it is
not always clear which is the artwork and which is the tool, who is the
artist and who is the audience? In 'The Work of Art in the Age of
Mechanical Reproduction', Walter Benjamin says:

"Even the most perfect reproduction of a work of art is lacking in one
element: its presence in time and space, its unique existence at the
place where it happens to be."[^20]

A work like 'Black and White' challenges this entire notion because it
is always changing, and though it only becomes visible when activated by
the viewer it is continually working, unobserved. This means that the
artwork is never 'finished' it is always a work in progress. The viewer
chooses to create a piece of art with it by activating it at a certain
time. Had the viewer activated it at a different time the images would
have been completely different. Similarly, the artwork is only brought
into being when it is viewed, but many people in different parts of the
world can view it simultaneously. Although this is 'reproduction' in a
sense, in some ways all the viewers are looking at the 'original'. In
theory all of these users are equally responsible for the outcome of the
artwork and therefore are joint 'authors' of it. Walter Benjamin
described 'original' pieces of work as having an 'aura' that gave the
work its authenticity:

> "The authenticity of a thing is the essence of all that is
> transmissible from its beginning, ranging from its substantive
> duration to its testimony to the history which it has experienced.
> Since the historical testimony rests on the authenticity, the former,
> too, is jeopardized by reproduction when substantive duration ceases
> to matter. And what is really jeopardized when the historical
> testimony is affected is the authority of the object."[^21]

How Walter Benjamin would reconcile these simultaneous 'originals' with
his concept of 'aura' is difficult to imagine.

These new forms of art are symptomatic of the changes in society brought
about by the internet, but traditional laws of copyright and
intellectual property will have to change if they are to be relevant in
the coming era. John Perry Barlow's essay "The Economy of Ideas" in
Wired magazine talks about the problems of copyright when applied to
digital forms of creativity. He says:

> *"If our property can be infinitely reproduced and instantaneously
> distributed all over the planet without cost, without our knowledge,
> without its even leaving our possession, how can we protect it? How
> are we going to get paid for the work we do with our minds? And, if we
> can't get paid, what will assure the continued creation and
> distribution of such work?"*[^22]

He also highlights how inadequate, current laws of intellectual property
are when dealing with digital media and the Internet, because of their
basis in the physical world:

> *"Thus, the rights of invention and authorship adhered to activities
> in the physical world. One didn't get paid for ideas, but for the
> ability to deliver them into reality. For all practical purposes, the
> value was in the conveyance and not in the thought conveyed.*
>
> *In other words, the bottle was protected, not the wine.*
>
> *Now, as information enters cyberspace, the native home of Mind, these
> bottles are vanishing. With the advent of digitisation, it is now
> possible to replace all previous information storage forms with one
> metabottle: complex and highly liquid patterns of ones and zeros...*
>
> *...Intellectual property law cannot be patched, retrofitted, or
> expanded to contain digitised expression any more than real estate law
> might be revised to cover the allocation of broadcasting spectrum
> (which, in fact, rather resembles what is being attempted here). We
> will need to develop an entirely new set of methods as befits this
> entirely new set of circumstances."*[^23]

Current laws of copyright[^24] simply don't work when applied to work at
sites like *Potatoland.org*. The installations on these sites are in
some ways more like tools than artworks, in that they give the viewer an
interface and a set of rules to play with and let *them* create the
work. Trying to establish copyright on this would be awkward, while the
copyright of the interface would definitely belong to the scriptor, what
about the images created with it? Could someone use these interfaces as
a tool to produce their own art for sale? If the copyright of the images
was granted to the scriptor it would be rather like saying that Canon
owned the copyright of every photograph taken with its cameras, or
Daler-Rowney owning hundreds of paintings that used its brushes as
tools. I think that the public's perception of tools however, is altered
by the commercial nature of software. Because companies like Adobe,
Microsoft and Macromedia are very large and well known, the fact that
their software is used as tools to create these works is far more
noticeable than that a painter might use a Daler-Rowney brush. Compared
to Flash or Internet Explorer, the brush is fairly anonymous.

This is somewhat related to the situation in France between 1857 and
1957 when photographers and filmmakers had to argue the case for their
work to be treated as culturally significant art (and therefore
copyrightable). The French Law saw their products as too 'industrial' to
be treated as 'art', because 'art' had to reflect something of the
Author's personality in it. Photographers and filmmakers like J E Bulloz
and Adam Salomon, and their critics such as Hippolyte Taine argued that:

> *"Generally it was maintained that a human factor overrode the
> mechanical process; upon close inspection, a human mark, the trace of
> a cultural act, could be discerned."*[^25]

The same could be said of software art and generative systems or tools.
Despite their technological appearance they contain an essential element
of the 'human mark' or 'cultural act'. Really their artworks are just
slightly more removed from the physical starting point of traditional
art, but although this is generally seen in a negative light, Curt
Cloninger says:

> "Digital artists sometimes claim superiority to physical/analog
> artists because digital artists work in a virtual, non-physical realm.
> This makes their art less dependent on physical matter, and thus more
> "pure" (or so the argument goes)"[^26]

It is true that when working digitally software artists have no physical
restraints and as digital media develops it could be said that the
digital artist has a distinct advantage over traditional artists because
they can create art without the restrictions of physical media. However,
even these software artists cannot really claim sole authorship of their
work, even if it is unfettered by physical realities. Someone else has
made all the software or programming languages that these software
artists use at some point. This relates to the argument over whether
there can ever be anything original other than original thought,
however, by taking this to it's logical extreme, even conceptual
artists, working only in ideas have not created their own minds, and are
therefore not the ultimate author's of their work. Whether evolution or
a god is considered to be the ultimate Author is of course another
discussion entirely.

## Individual Author vs. Collaborative Editor

Cloninger sees author's becoming more like editors as artists continue
to work further from raw materials and no longer start 'from scratch'.
Perhaps the name 'Author' no longer has any meaning when applied to
software art and the like; perhaps these works are becoming more
editorial than authorial. The degree to which the artist 'edits' rather
than 'authors' is also dependent on the tools they are using. The more
'assertive' the tools are the more editorial the work becomes, at one
end of the scale you have a pencil which will do nothing without the
direct concentration and manipulation of an artist, at the other,
generative software that will create 'artworks' with a single click of a
mouse. The 'assertive' tools that Cloninger describes are those that
have a greater effect on the outcome of the artwork, the artist has to
collaborate with the tools. For example, generative software is
collaboration between the designer of the software and the artist. The
software designer will have a far greater influence on the final artwork
than say the manufacturer of a pencil or paintbrush. Although this could
be seen to be limited to the area of software art, Cloninger argues that
it is present to a degree in many other forms of media:

> *"Editing the output of auto-generative software may seem an extreme
> example of the standard artistic process, but is it really? A
> photographer tweaks a camera's parameters and frames his picture. He
> selects which images to print from a roll of negatives. In the
> darkroom he crops, accentuates, mutes, burns, dodges. All these
> actions are primarily editorial.*[^27]*"*

In 'What Is An Author' Foucault talks about the way in which some
authors become 'initiators of discursive practices':

> *"The distinctive contribution of these authors is that they produced
> not only their own work, but the possibility and the rules of
> formation of other tests*[^28]*"*

Software artists, especially those engaged in the production or use of
generative software are very much the type of authors that Foucault was
talking about. As well as creating their own artworks they are creating
entirely new genres of art and the tools to work within them. However,
it seems that well as author's possibly resigning themselves to the role
of editors, the breakdown of the 'Author-God' also necessitates the
relinquishing of authorial control in a commercial sense.
Open-Source[^29] licensed software or artwork cannot easily make any
money, software art cannot easily be bought and sold, and it has no
obvious commercial value.

It maybe that the breakdown of authorial control of these online works
is simply inherent of the Internet's 'anarchy', but it is difficult to
imagine how the Internet could ever be institutionalised either. It is
rather like the English language, nobody owns it but everyone can use
it, and neither would be any better off for having a board of directors.
Is the Internet simply another example of the breakdown of the modernist
elite, where the artist was the one to whom the work was solely
indebted? Is this inherent of the postmodernist culture of copying that
we live in today? The much-publicised file sharing network, Napster and
similar sites, Kazaa and Gnutella[^30] are preaching the '*everything
free for everyone'* attitude of the net. Software art is in tune with
this, rather than artists saying, '*this is my masterpiece you can't
have it unless you pay me for it*' software artists actively encourage
the viewer to take part in their work for free. After all without the
participation of the viewer the artwork doesn't exist. Although this
free for all utopia sounds great for the consumers, it is difficult to
imagine how an artist can ever make a living from it.

Computer games are another form of digital software art that challenge
the notions of how the audience reacts to and interacts with the author.
The authors of computer games however, seem to have managed to retain
authorial control in a commercial sense whilst still appearing to offer
a new author-viewer relationship. The ultimate goal of many games is to
gain 'photo-realism' and with many looking more and more like films
every year; it is interesting to note the differences between to the two
mediums. Whilst watching a film the relationship between viewer and
creator is very one way. The director, who has become the nominated
'individual author' of the film, decides what you will and won't see and
hear, he has chosen the pacing, the story line and the soundtrack. The
viewer can only watch and interpret. With computer games, there is a
two-way relationship between author and viewer. Games like Bethsoft's
Morrowind essentially create a world with a set of rules and then allow
the player to make up their own mind about what they do with it. This is
very similar to websites like *Sodaplay.com*, however games go further
than this, more and more often now games come complete with editing
software. With Morrowind this software is the same as that used to build
the game. The player can do anything with it, from adding a single item
to the game world; to completely replacing the entire land mass. This
unprecedented freedom has created some interesting possibilites.

For example game 'mods' (modifications) have been created that pitch the
(highly protected) Star Wars franchise against the Star Trek one.
Players can play as a Jedi against a Klingon. These kinds of
collaborations would never be officially sanctioned by the licensees but
can be created and distributed by the online game community for free.
The cinematic equivalent would be that halfway through Star Wars Episode
II, the audience could decide to swap Obi-Wan for Captain Kirk, or that
once you've bought the DVD, you also got the tools to re-edit the movie
and distribute it for free. Other 'mods' are available that could
completely 'subvert' the character of the original game, for example
modifications that remove the clothes of the in game population, or
change their dialogue to something obscene. This is all tolerated and
actually encouraged by the game publishers. The reaction if this were
done in different media (film for example) would be very different.
However, the apparent freedom to rewrite and redistribute the game is of
course tempered by the fact that very few purchasers will actually have
the necessary skills or inclination to do so. In addition to this, you
cannot run any of the mods without already owning the original game.

This distribution of the tools of game creation is very much linked to
the 'Open-Source' movement and with software art adopting Open-Source
models of authorship the boundaries between the tools and what is
created with them are being blurred. In his essay '*Models of
Authorship'* Manovich talks about how many players of The Sims simply
use the game as a tool to create their own scenarios to share with
others.

> *"With another widely popular game, The Sims (2001), this type of
> collaboration reached a new stage. The Web site for the game allows
> users to upload the characters, the settings, and the narratives they
> constructed into the common library, as well as download characters,
> settings, and narratives constructed by others.*[^31] *Soon it turned
> out that the majority of users do not even play the game but rather
> use its software to create their own characters and storyboard their
> adventures."*[^32]

This kind of activity is actively promoted by game producers, and is
seen as an essential feature for future games to incorporate, to ensure
longevity. This is in marked difference to the way in which the music
industry clamps down on any form of copyright infringement. The game
producers however see this as more of a collaborative authorship of new
material for their games. Recently this kind of modification of a games
original intention has been used for political means, sometimes with the
result of attacking the creators of the game. As the Canadian Press
reported recently:

> *"The latest game to hit the market is the Sims Online, from
> Electronic Arts. Players have control over a character and act out
> real-life fantasies. They've built in-game restaurants, created
> several radio stations and even a newspaper. And they are not shy
> about complaining. Freelance writer Tony Walsh didn't like a deal
> Electronic Arts made to insert a McDonald's kiosk into the game, so
> he organized a protest..."*[^33]

At the emerging online community of *There.com*, gamers have already
organised online protests against the impending war in Iraq and clothed
their online avatars in peace symbols. However, this apparent virtual
freedom of speech may come to an end when the forthcoming Star Wars
Galaxies game is released.

> *"..."Somebody saying something in the game and being witnessed by
> somebody else can reflect not just on the game but on Lucasfilm and
> George Lucas," said Koster, a lead designer for Galaxies, which is
> due in April..."*[^34]

Obviously the companies who own these online games will only let gamers
say so much, and will probably seek to ban those players who insist on
using the game as a political or ideological soapbox. It is interesting
to note however, that once an author has released their work into the
world, no matter what the media, they no longer have control over how it
will be used or interpreted.

## Devices and Filters

The idea of an Open-Source license that accompanies a piece of work may
have practical uses for software artists. Jon Ippolito (Associate
Curator of Media Arts at the Guggenheim)[^35] has set up the Variable
Media Initiative, which proposes that an artist should be able to state
how their work should be exhibited. This is effectively a license
containing what can and cannot be done with the artwork. This is
especially useful for software artists because their work is so reliant
on other software.

This reliance on 'filters' or 'devices' is an area in which software art
challenges the ideas of authorship and especially the Foucaultian notion
of an "Author-God". It becomes difficult for many to separate the art
from the technology. To display most pieces of software art requires a
PC equipped with a Flash enabled Internet browser (preferably Microsoft
Internet Explorer). The fact that only when each of these devices is
combined and calibrated correctly, can the artwork be displayed, is a
major hurdle in the acceptance of software art, as a valid art form. If
all software art came with a license specifying exactly what hardware
and software were to be used to view the work it would help the artist
retain control over how their work was displayed to the public.

*Jodi.org*, a web-based collective of software artists, has found an
interesting solution to the problem of exhibiting software art. They
have an exhibition running at Plug-In, a new media gallery in Basel. The
exhibition consists of carpeted and comfortable booths and a rack of
laptops. The viewers simply take a laptop to a booth, plug in to the
provided network socket and browse jodi's online work at their leisure.
This raises interesting questions about whether the gallery space is
redundant if all it does is to reproduce the experience most people
could have at home. It also questions ideas about how a work of art is
defined, after all if a painting in a gallery is art simply because it
is in the gallery, what then happens when the gallery is online, open to
all and viewable at home. Does work in this form of gallery lose some of
its *'Benjamin Aura'*?

In reality software art and generative software has a collaborative
authorship shared between, scriptor, software publishers and the viewer.
However it seems unlikely that society is really ready to accept the
idea of collaborative authorial credit. Films are a good example of the
way that generally society seems to need to identify an individual
author as solely responsible for the work. Although films are hugely
collaborative projects they are often referred to as 'the director's'
film (for example, Peter Jackson's Lord of the Rings, which required a
cast of thousands working for several years as well as many different
technical departments). Popular culture as a whole is simply not
satisfied with collaborations and prefers to imagine that even a film
involving thousands of differently talented people is in fact the vision
and accomplishment of one person.

The reliance of the work on 'devices' also confuses the issue, when
trying to establish authorship of a piece. For example a piece of Flash
art may well have been made by one individual, but it has been created
using Macromedia's Flash 5.0 program and relies on Microsoft's Internet
Explorer 6 in order to be run. This in turn is reliant on Microsoft
Windows, which is itself being run on the compliant hardware of the
computer manufacturer. A distinct problem is that each of these devices
or filters may change the artwork in some way and so it is impossible to
know if you are ever viewing the work quite as the artist intended or
not. The same digital image can look different in different programs on
the same computer, or in the same program viewed through different
monitors or monitor settings. Also, if a vital piece of software is
missing then the artwork may not appear at all. This is reminiscent of
Heisenburg's Uncertainty Principle, which states that you cannot observe
something without changing it. When the digital file is closed it is at
it's most perfect, as soon as we open it to view it, it has been
altered. However, in reality this is something of a paradox, as the
digital artist would have to be looking at the file whilst they were
creating it and as such could never be sure that even they had seen the
'original' artwork. This kind of 'invisible original' is of no real use
in a discussion. At present all of this is of less concern to artists,
primarily because digital art tends to be used in industries
(illustration for example) where reproduction is inevitable and the
scanning and printing processes alter all works, traditional as well as
digital. But how will this be resolved when digital artworks start to
make an impact in the fine art arena? This whole area of the art world
is founded on the value of the original rather than a copy.

> "*The drawback of copies, however carefully made, is that they are
> never exact. (A copy is really an interpretation or translation.) Also
> copies are almost as time consuming and labour intensive to produce as
> the originals...*"[^36]

Digital copies have none of these drawbacks; every copy is an original
and can be reproduced almost instantaneously, in a fraction of the time
the first version took to make. It is this plurality that really sets
digital images and software art apart from traditional media.

The perception of technology is another factor here, after all,
paintbrushes, pigments and canvas are all technology but because most
people are so familiar with these things they no longer see them as
this. A lot of the apparent differences between digital and traditional
work are in fact simply differences in the perception of technology.
This could be applied to photography too, after all a photographer has
used a Nikon camera, Ilford film, and an enlarger and paper in producing
their photographs. Perhaps it is just because photography is more
established that we tend not to think so much about the tools, it has
become more divorced from its technology. A major difference though is
that with photographic tools there is a vast range to choose from, where
as a lot of software art is held under a monopoly of Microsoft and
Macromedia products. This gives the impression that the software artist
has less choice in choosing their tools and that subsequently, the work
they produce will be dictated by the software. The high profile of these
companies (especially Microsoft) also distorts the perception of their
software as tools, as do the high prices they cost.

The lack of control that a software artist has over their work is one of
the disadvantages of such an open way of working. But there are ways in
which the artist can reduce the unauthorised distribution of their work,
even on the Internet. Many of the sites I have looked at allow you to
download the source code of the work on their sites. This may seem as
though they are giving away their secrets but in fact the number of
people who could make use of the code is limited. It would be rather
like Leonardo da Vinci creating a colour by numbers book for his famous
paintings, even with these instructions few people would be able to
recreate his work. What you download is not the finished piece of work
but rather the instructions to build it. Of course to make use of this
code requires a specific knowledge of programming and the correct
software. The game-editing software discussed earlier is another example
of this. Of course a lack of control over their work is not something
limited just to software artists. Even with 'original', traditional,
one-off pieces of fine art, an artist can never retain control over how
their work will be interpreted. Marcus Harvey's painting of Myra Hindley
using child-size handprints received an extreme reaction from the press,
even though the artist's intention was entirely different to the
journalist's interpretation.[^37]

'Restrictive coding' of various types is seen throughout sites like
*Turux.org* and *Potatoland.org*, and it is not just limited to the
downloading of code. Potential visitors face a problem before they even
get to the site: how to find it. Because these types of sites are not
dealing with mainstream artworks they are not advertised or promoted.
Most of the sites I found were connected to each other, either directly
or via central site's like *Rhizome.org*. They almost seem to operate
within a 'secret circle' of websites, hard to find initially but easy to
explore once you are 'in' .The next hurdle that visitors will encounter
on arriving at the site is that they generally require the latest
versions of Macromedia's Shockwave and Flash plugins. This may not even
be mentioned on the site itself but will just appear as a message
telling you that you don't have the required plugins. These sites assume
that anyone interested in them will have the software or will know how
and where to get it. The use of Flash and Macromedia is also somewhat
dependent on the Internet browser being used, so if the viewer doesn't
have the latest version of Microsoft Internet Explorer, they may not get
very far. Sites like these often require that your monitor is set to a
certain minimum resolution (1024x 768 pixels for example), if the viewer
doesn't understand what this means then the site will probably look
rather odd and impede navigation even further. Of course another
potential problem with this is that the viewer may not even realise that
they are not seeing the site as intended.

The next hurdle for the viewer is the design of the sites themselves.
They tend not to be particularly user friendly, and don't really explain
what they are about or where you should click. Links to the artworks are
often unnamed or obscurely named so that you are not entirely sure where
you're going when you click on them. Sites like *Turux.org* have no
legible text on the homepage[^38] at all and have a navigation bar down
one side with minuscule numerical links to the artworks. This means that
you can never be quite sure what you're going to see or ever be sure
you'll see it again. It also makes it difficult to tell if the site has
been updated or new work added. *Potatoland.org* has a very anonymous
homepage[^39], which gives very little indication of where you are. It
is the antithesis of a corporate website with no logo's or branding, no
navigation bars or mission statements. These sites go directly against
all the established rules of web-design.

Sites like *Jodi.org* have taken this one stage further. The homepage of
*Jodi.org* is continually moving and changing. It has recently been
removed altogether; the address now takes the viewer to a download page
for one of their games. The only way to access the website proper is via
a search engine which will take you to articles reviewing the site,
which in turn link to pages on the site itself. The idea that people can
only reach the site via a 'second-hand' route and other peoples opinions
of it is certainly a novel one, but it must also drastically reduce the
number of visitors to the site. By using this method *Jodi.org* is
completely negating the advantage of its short and catchy website
address and forcing would be visitors to work hard to find what they
want. However, this does then mean that once again, the viewers of the
site are not merely passively being fed information by the author's of
the site. There is far more effort required from the audience, but the
result is a more reciprocal relationship between author and viewer. All
of these things combine to seriously limit the appeal of such sites to
many people. Although the artwork appears to offer a new conversational
style of author-viewer interaction, the websites them selves restrict
those viewers to the 'technological elite'.

These websites and their work are very much part of the 'free and
democratic' ethos of the Internet, you do not have to pay to view any of
the artworks and they are not affiliated with any multi-national
conglomerates. They extol the virtues of the 'open-source' movement and
seem to compliment the 'free for all' ideals of the file-sharing
programs. However, the democracy of the Net is not quite as fair and
open as it may first appear. To begin with, there is the issue of
technology; to have access to these websites and artworks requires a
relatively up to date computer and a reliable Internet connection.
Obviously the purchase of a computer is a fairly major investment and
far more so in developing countries. Secondly the availability of a
reliable telephone network and affordable ISP (Internet Service
Provider), limits the audience to predominantly those in developed
countries. Another restriction often overlooked is the issue of
language, English is the dominant spoken and written language in the
West, offline, but has become the dominant online language as well.
Sites like *Google.com* will even attempt to translate foreign language
pages into English. This linguistic imperialism probably goes unnoticed
by most of the English-speaking world but is obviously a major hurdle to
anyone browsing the Internet who is not fluent.

### Conclusion

Although the digital revolution can be compared to the introduction of
photography in the 19th Century, its implications will almost
certainly be far broader. 'Digital', is not simply a new tool, nor is it
only a new medium, as Alan Kay says:

*"The protean nature of the computer is such that it can act like a
machine or like a language to be shaped and exploited. It is a medium
that can dynamically simulate the details of any other medium, including
media that cannot exist physically. It is not a tool, although it can
act like many tools. It is the first metamedium, and as such it has
degrees of freedom for representation and expression never before
encountered and as yet barely investigated."*[^40]

In conclusion, it seems apparent that current models of authorship and
laws of copyright cannot encompass this new '*metamedium*' but although
software art challenges many of the established notions of authorship,
it has not entirely freed itself from the elitist attitudes of Fine Art.
One thing that seems to be true in all cases of software art is that
they give the appearance of offering a new democratic relationship
between viewer and author but actually hide a far more conservative
authorial model. The common factor seems to be the rather underhand way
that they go about this.

Firstly, there are the online galleries of Flash based artwork; these
appear to be open to all but actually shut out the majority of viewers
through a combination of technology, economics and language. Once the
prospective audience has found the site, navigated the gallery and
downloaded the required software they have access to the new
conversational relationship between them and the author. Then there are
the computer games that appear to give you the viewer, the power to
create and distribute your own versions and modifications to the game --
for free! But once again, there's a catch. This only applies if you have
the technical knowledge, time and patience to do so and can only be
distributed to those who already own the original. The apparently open,
democratic and 'conversational' relationship between author and viewer
operates within a closed, restrictively coded, technological elite. It
seems undeniable, however, that the new modes of authorship, prevalent
within the Open-Source, generative software art world, will have a
profound effect on the world of more traditional art, at some point in
the future. Indeed this more collaborative, interactive authorship may
simply be part of a wider trend. In recent years the sales of computer
and console games have outstripped the revenues of Hollywood. In a year
when CD sales have fallen, many more people are choosing to download
their own selections of music (i.e. become their own authors) rather
than pay for a 'pre-authored' album of tracks[^41]. The proliferation of
software art could well be the start of this new form of authorship
extending into the areas of Fine Art and other media. As the Internet
continues to expand and the world becomes ever more digital and
connected, digital imaging and software artistry may well lead the way
forward for new models of authorship.

Jago Titcomb 2003
