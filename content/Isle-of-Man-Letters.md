*← [Silver Family Archive](./)*

From [Henlow](Henlow-Letters), [David Silver](David-Silver) was posted in July 1946 to the **Isle of Man**, first to RAF Andreas and then to RAF Jurby, as an engine fitter, LAC 1587757. These eighteen letters to his parents at **Tresillian** in Exeter run through the island's long winter until March 1947, a few months before his last posting, to [Wellesbourne Mountford](Wellesbourne-Letters).

He arrives on the brand-new *Mona's Queen*, falls asleep on a train of forty-year-old carriages pulled by a little green engine with a copper dome, and sends home a pen map of the island, which he finds "a sort of combination of Cornwall and Wales". He walks to the Point of Ayre, sketches at Peel and Port Mooar, and hunts the shops of Ramsey and Douglas for Christmas presents, finding only "trinkets brooches and trash". He hates the motorcycle races and thinks Andreas "the most inefficient unit I have ever been on". He works on derelict Wellingtons in the rain with the hangars pulled down around him, flies the five miles to Jurby in one ("much better" than a Fortress), and talks German with an anti-Nazi prisoner from Chemnitz. After seeing *School for Secrets*, a film about radar, he writes that it showed "all about the work I should have done if I hadn't been shoved onto the ground". All the while he is planning his escape into architecture: the application to London University, the prospectuses, the maths books, and the History of Architecture read by the E.V.T. room fire.

**About the transcription.** The letters are transcribed with David's own spelling and punctuation. ~~Struck text~~ shows his crossings-out, [?] a doubtful reading and [square brackets] describe drawings and layout. Page breaks appear as *(p. n)*. Most of the letters are dated from what he says and from postmarks; the evidence is given in italics under each heading. One sheet is missing and a few pages were scanned at an angle. A letter from his brother Christopher, filed with these, is given at the end. The scans for each letter are in the collapsible **Scans** box beneath it.

## 1946

### 01. Saturday – Sunday, c. 27–28 July 1946 (arrival on the island)

*Undated. He has just crossed from Liverpool on the new *Mona's Queen* and reported to RAF Andreas; the next letter, written on the August Bank Holiday weekend, refers back to "last week's letter". The "Victory" stamps on two envelopes are of the 1946 issue.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1.
> 1587757 LAC SILVER
> address not known at present.
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> The Taxi, and the train to London were on time. At Euston R.T.O. I found that Exeter R.T.O had made a serious mistake in my routing. The only ship to the I.O.M. which service personnel might use was the 10.30 am from Liverpool. Euston R.T.O. routed me to Liverpool R.T.O. So I went up on the 12.30 early morning train and arrived a little late this morning at about five. There I met another person from my entry at Henlow.
> He was going to the other camp on the other side of man.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> We had breakfast in the Y.M. We left our kit bags in a corner at the other end of the room. A policeman came in and told us they would be stolen if we didn't keep them in sight all the time. The notice on the door said no civilians, but the place was full of villainous looking tramps who begged for money. We cleared out of there as quickly as possible. The R.T.O. directed us to wait for a transport at 7.0 am on No 7 platform. It never came so at 7.20 a porter advised us to go to the harbour at once by cab as there would be a queue for the boat. He was right.
> We shared a Taxi (incidentally I used the inter-station bus in London).
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2.
> At the harbour was a great queue we joined it and stood waiting till quarter to ten when the queue must have been a quarter of a mile long and six people wide. We were allowed onto the boat about a hundred at a time, after a great deal of backing and positioning and mooring of the ships. Its name was "Mona's Queen". It was about the same size as those we saw at Holyhead, one funnel and I should think a hundred yards long. (It's a new ship built this year)
> We took six cars aboard, an R.A.C. man directing operations. The owners drove them up a horrible sloping ramp very quickly
> [Drawing: a car driving up a steep ramp onto the ship's deck.]
>
> *(p. 4)*
> There was a ~~heavy~~ thick mist over the Mersey but I could see very large cargo vessels being escorted up, and down, by tugs.
> I got a seat on the lower deck looking out of the side in the stern
> [Drawing: the ship's stern deck in section, with himself seated under the upper deck, labelled "me".]
> The ship was very full of people going on ~~leave~~ holiday and about 3 other RAF men. There ~~were~~ were many small children with whom I died a thousand deaths as they appeared to be intent on finding the quickest way over the side and no one tried to stop them. Strangely enough none of them did fall over which proves why no one bothered but me
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3.
> We travelled rapidly down the river. There were three large chimneys behind some harbour buildings and not knowing we had begun to move and seeing the chimneys move in relation to the harbour buildings I called the attention of the other Henlow-ite to them. For a few moments we both thought that they belonged to some large ship till we realized it was our ship that was moving and that they belonged to a factory.
> As we sailed down the river the fog cleared a little and we passed the wrecks of boats marked by buoys and by the masts sticking out of the water. (three in all).
> The weather was very grey and England soon
>
> *(p. 6)*
> disappeared.
> There appeared on the horizon little mushrooms which on passing closer proved to be groups of six or seven towers on legs either to do with anti aircraft or else RADAR I remember seeing them illustrated in the papers.
> [Drawing: three box-like towers on splayed legs, joined by walkways: the Mersey sea forts.]
> There were four or five such groups. Then there was nothing but grey sea and grey sky and poor visibility. The boat hardly rolled at all, all the way. In fact walking along the deck it wasn't possible to be certain that it moved at all unless you watched some point on the railing move slightly in relation to the horizon. Nobody was sick.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4.
> Down below was a dining saloon bar, buffet, and sleeping berths. These however were occupied before I'd discovered their existence. As I'd managed with canteens up to now I had your provisions on the boat Mummy dear, very good they were too, and must have cost a year's rations. Two thirds of the way there, the rain began to come down heavily drenching the people on the top deck.
> Saturday. So far I got before I fell asleep. I was sitting up in bed writing and it was 9 p.m. I'll go on from where I left off.
>
> *(p. 8)*
> The ship left Liverpool at 10.45 The Isle of Man showed up at about 2.0pm or just after as the ship we came on was the fastest of three that do the trip. It was very fast, you could tell by the movement relative to land.
> Because of the mist the I.O.M. loomed through the mist, much above the horizon.
> The engines were stopped about three miles off and the boat slid in. Directly you could see anything you realised that the island was almost mountainous, not that you were approaching a fairly large place with a harbour and big blocks of buildings along the front. This was less impressive as you got nearer.
>
> *(p. 9)*
> 5.
> Sunday afternoon.
> By a very skilful manoeuvre which I should have hitherto thought impossible we came gently up to a harbour wall. There were two other steamers in the harbour one rather small. They were the "Ben Machree" and the "Manx Maid". Some R.AF. people with red arm bands took charge of the RAF people. Our kit was put on a lorry (very unusual this) and taken to the station, we walked it was about half a mile. I got a bit of a shock at the station as it's the size of Exmouth station only the platforms are only a foot high. The coaches are about forty years old, hold four a side, have no corridor and about 3 compartments of eight seats in each carriage. The engines are in the Emmett tradition.
>
> *(p. 10)*
> They are - I am so sorry I've run out of ink and it being Sunday the Y.M. isn't open yet so I'll have to make do with pencil for a while.
> I was going to say that the engines are pale green. The size of a baby Austin and have huge, copper domes highly polished like this
> [Drawing: a little tank engine with a tall polished dome, pulling a four-wheeled coach with many doors.]
> I got into a carriage there wasn't any question of whether it was the right train as it called at every stop on the island practically.
> The seats were hard wood backed and very dingy and worn upholstery to sit on.
> The train set off at about four miles an hour it was the 3.40 by the way.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> 6.
> It didn't sway and it didn't roll but it did seem to have square wheels. I just remember passing up a rather pretty wooded valley with little whitewashed cottages with slate roofs as in Cornwall and in Kit's photograph in Anglesey.
> [Drawing: a whitewashed cottage with a big chimney, "This type of thing".]
> Then I fell asleep. I missed most of the ride I suppose we called at Peel but I was asleep. Anyway the next thing I knew we were on the North West side of the island on the top of some cliffs right at the cliff edge looking down in just the way one would look down at Bedruthan Steps with the mist and damp almost steaming and the wet beach looking dark below.
>
> *(p. 12)*
> After crossing a number of ravines like that one at Bossiney we went inland at Kirkmichael. We were now in the north of the island in the flat part and it reminded me of Cornwall again. On the right however the hills towered above us like the Welsh mountains did. One more stop Ballaugh and then we reached Sulby Glen which I find is a much cracked up beauty spot. It would have been lovely only the clouds obscured the tops of the hills. At Sulby Glen the rest of the RAF disembarked for RAF JURBY or as I thought it was - JURGY. I went on to Ramsey as instructed.
> There was no transport waiting so I caught a bus to camp? the bus met the transport coming down
>
> *(p. 13)*
> 7.
> I surveyed it coldly. It was half an hour late. I reported to the guard room who gave me blankets and a quarter of an hour walk with them to my billet a temporary one. The transport brought my kit down from the guard room where I'd left it till later, which was also unusual. I had something to eat in the Y.M. and guess what it was? They had great trays of egg sandwiches real eggs. I noticed a bowl like our washing up bowl sitting casually on a counter full of eggs (in their shells uncooked). Stop, Wiggs I know what you are going to say THERE ARE NOTICES EVERYWHERE FORBIDDING THE EXPORT OF FOOD BY AIR, BOAT, OR POST.
> Any way they are rationed over here just like at home only
>
> *(p. 14)*
> the bread ration doesn't begin till next week.
> So I made my bed and started this letter and fell asleep and now the Y.M's open so I'll see if there's any ink before going further.
> Here we are - some terrible looking stuff called "Lion brand"
> Well that was Friday night, I overslept missed breakfast and reported to P3 movements at 9.30, the sergeant there looked at his watch (It should have been 8.30) raised an eyebrow and said that as it was the AOCs inspection that morning, he couldn't do anything for me so I might as well come back on Monday. Everybody was in a panic about the AOC as last time he came here they were all on
>
> *(p. 15)*
> 8.
> Christmas grant and they could hardly even dig up a meal for him let alone a parade or inspection. (Just like an AOC to expect everybody to be in camp on Christmas day). Any way when I arrived I was a bit mystified as all the occupants of the huts were in the ablutions scrubbing their belts and webbing equipment. Something I've never seen before in the RAF.
> I found out to my joy that they finished work at 12.0 on a Saturday and the week end was free. It was a typical Treknow day. Everything wet with fine rain and a cool wind. I went to Ramsey, the nearest town, by bus.
> I spent the afternoon looking round, it's about the same size as
>
> *(p. 16)*
> Exmouth's shopping centre without the residential part of Exmouth. Very narrow streets filled with toy shops and postcard shops. I sent you some kippers, supposed to arrive in good order by Friday for breakfast on Saturday. Eight were the least I could send I keep wondering how you'll manage two each. I hope they won't be bad They'll be dispatched I think on Wednesday.
> In the evening I went to the smaller of the two cinemas and sat in the best seats for 1/3. I saw Waterloo Bridge (not Waterloo Road) it was old and not terribly good.
> I returned by bus and did a sketch of the range of hills
>
> *(p. 17)*
> 9.
> which lie to the West of here, just as it was getting dark.
> This morning (by the way yesterday I had for supper in a café fried real egg, fried potato and fresh peas hog!) this morning there being no buses on a Sunday morning from camp I walked to Sulby Glen about six miles. Then since it was lunch time I went into the only place where I could get lunch, a hotel marked RAC. I was put at one corner of a table laid for six. Two flight lieutenants were placed one opposite and one at the end of the table. I wished they hadn't been. However we all kept offering each other bread and biscuits and cheese
>
> *(p. 18)*
> and pepper and salt and apart from the fact that ~~all the~~ every body spoke in whispers and most in north country ones at that, it was all right.
> Then I caught the first bus to Kirk Michael which I expected to be very nice. It would have been judging by the bus drivers information about it as we sat there just before the bus turned round. It was driving with rain, sheets and sheets. The hills were out of sight and have been for the rest of the day. The bus took me back to Ramsey were I had tea in a small tea room on the front with plastic table cloths. I was impressed by a small boy who came in to have tea with his parents
>
> *(p. 19)*
> 10.
> and seeing the table cloth said "plastic". They sat at the next table to me and had sandwiches some of which had crab in them. His mother said to the father "which are which"? and the little boy said "I expect if you smell them you'll be able to find out". He was only about six.
> Then I caught the bus and here I am now writing this to you in the Y.M. reading room while the rain drives down. There are a good many people on holiday over here.
> It's a beautiful place I'd like you to see it. It's very much like Cornwall. Apparently it's an awful camp as regards discipline and work but I haven't met much of either yet.
>
> *(p. 20)*
> I did a rotten sketch to show you what the cottages are like which I'll enclose. It was done with a restricted pallette of five colours. I shall probe the leave question on Monday - tomorrow but I don't hold out very great hopes as there are all sorts of restrictions, as for instance you may only start leave on a Friday and it's not easy to get being Training Command. Most people here seem to have only recently joined the RAF or else to have been four years overseas.
> Well that's about the end of volume I on the Isle of Man.
> with lots of love your loving son David
> [Bottom left, in capitals:] 1587757 LAC SILVER / MAINTENANCE H.Q. / RAF ANDREAS / NR RAMSEY / I.O.M. will find me.

*Notes:* He numbers the sheets 1–10. The "towers on legs" are the Mersey sea forts. The Manx Northern Railway engines with polished copper domes are "in the Emmett tradition" (Rowland Emett, the *Punch* cartoonist). Exeter's R.T.O. had routed him wrongly; the A.O.C. is the Air Officer Commanding.

### 02. Sunday 4 – Tuesday 6 August 1946 (August Bank Holiday)

*Headed "Sunday", "Bank Holiday Weekend" (Monday 5 August 1946), continued "Monday" and "Tuesday". With it went an "Electrolux technical supplement" for mending the vacuum cleaner he had rebuilt at home.*

### 06. Saturday 14 – Wednesday 18 September 1946 (moving to Jurby; a flight in a Wellington)

*Headed "Saturday" and continued "Monday", "Tuesday morning" and "Wednesday": "Saturday was Open day for Andreas" (Battle of Britain day, 14 September 1946), and the main party left Jurby for the mainland at 4 a.m. on the Tuesday.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1 [In other ink:] This address is O.K. now → use it if you like.

>
> *(p. 4)*

> *(p. 5)*
> 3.

The driver had forgotten to remove the radiator muff and travelling at such a speed the engine had boiled. We went on to the bomb dump which lay peacefully under its camouflage netting with never a flicker of flame.
> The fire officer came up in a van. apparently an alarm had come
>
> *(p. 6)*
> from married quarters at the same time but the bomb dump being more important we had gone there first. It turned out that the married quarters one caused by a little boy pushing a button had in some mysterious way affected the Bomb Dump indicator.
> The next night the warning went at 10.30 pm I was in bed or just about to get in and the others were all in bed we had to get up and run to the fire section - it was only a practice.
> Every morning this week I have had to get up early, push a Spitfire out of a hangar start it up and await the pilot to take it on a weather test flight
>
> *(p. 7)*
> At Andreas the hangars are being dismantled and the camp is soon to return to the state in which I should like to see all aerodromes - ploughed up.
> One of the men working on one of the last few aeroplanes over there told me of a civilian private aeroplane a Proctor (single engine four seater) which came down at Andreas. It was red and green and very smart and there were two men and two women in it who were flying from Ireland. They asked where they were when they got out and had no idea that they were on the Isle of Man. It was foggy
>
> *(p. 8)*
> and they had no wireless.
> Although I have been on duty all the week and not able to go out at the week end I don't get a day off next week. Everybody else has had one so far. The Warrant Officer will not let me or the other chap who was on fire picquet have one because he never realized before that they were having a day off although they have had it for years! He said he didn't have one when he was an airman (that must have been twenty years ago). I think that's a poor reason and nearly got very angry with him but fortunately didn't
>
> *(p. 9)*
> which was perhaps just as well as I might now be looking at the outside world through bars.
> I don't wonder they can't get any volunteers. All this stuff you see in the papers about no more parades etc is all bosh we still have them.
> The difficulty after doing this duty fitter business and seeing the Spitfire off is to get any breakfast. Everybody else has finished by then. They tried to palm me off with a plate of cold beans and a piece of bread, so I asked them if I could use their 'phone to ring up the orderly officer. I got corn flakes beans and bacon and
>
> *(p. 10)*
> bread and butter after asking to do this. I will be very glad to be demobilized. The tea a few days ago was black and everybody had pains inside after drinking it so we made an awful row about it. It stayed the proper colour for a few days and now it's getting black again.
> However, enough of this - I haven't been out of camp so I haven't a lot to occupy my mind. I am drawing a lot of cross sections of window sills but I do wish there was some one to explain them to me.
> I am still reading Henry
>
> *(p. 11)*
> Williamson's "Tales of a Devon Village".
> Isn't it awful about Woodbury Common, Dartmoor, and Woolacombe I think it's time the public protested, why can't they use some out of the way spot in the north of Scotland where there is plenty of room? and not many people live.
> First food, then clothes, then houses, petrol and cars and now the country.
> I think I'll go and live in New Zealand soon at this rate.
> Whatever happened at these elections? I can't think how Labour did so well.
>
> *(p. 12)*
> 4.
> I met Orchard to-day at Flying Control. I had to go there in response to a Tannoy call for the Duty Fitter.
> He recognised me directly I came in and we exchanged greetings rather to the amazement of two Wing Commanders who had just landed and wanted their plane seen to. I quite liked him much better than when he was at Norwood. He and I went to get some concrete blocks to picquet the plane down with, in the Flying Control van (the blocks not the plane). They live at Exmouth now. He asked me if I'd like to come down to Ramsey some evening with him. I said that I would but don't mean to as I have
>
> *(p. 13)*
> no civilian clothes and it would be a bit thick for him to have to go out with an LAC.
> He is officer in charge of Flying Control. He hopes to be demobbed in February 52 group. He's a pilot and a Flight Lieutenant. He hopes to be an airfield controller in civilian airways when he gets out.
> Do you know anything about his family as I have nothing to talk about when I meet him. His sister is at the "D+E" as he put it I took about half an hour working this out as the Devon + Exeter Hospital so didn't know whether to look glad or sorry when he said it. She's a nurse.
>
> *(p. 14)*
> I don't seem to be able to find much in the way of Christmas presents over here. Can any one give me any suggestions please? There aren't any little books over here, Mummy.
> Well that's about all for now
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> I am sorry about these horrid envelopes but I haven't any others at the minute.

*Notes:* The order of the later pages is reconstructed from the text. As Duty Fitter he pushed out a Spitfire every morning for its weather-test flight. Woodbury Common, Dartmoor and Woolacombe were wanted by the services for training.

### 09. A Sunday, c. late November 1946 (Christmas parcels)

*Undated ("Sunday"). Christmas parcels are on their way, with one marked "C.M.S. birthday" (his father's birthday was just before Christmas); envelopes postmarked DOUGLAS 20 and 29 NOV 1946 fit.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 LAC Silver D.R.
> Hut 8 R+I Section
> RAF JURBY
> Isle of Man
> Sunday
> Dear Mummy + Daddy
> Shortly you should receive two parcels, one will be addressed to me and one to Lavender.
> The one addressed to Lavender should be opened at once. It will NOT contain eggs or any rationed goods so please don't think it's a lovely surprise of two dozen eggs that will go bad, because it isn't.
> The one addressed to me should not be opened except in the event that I am not at home before 23rd December.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> It should then be opened and inside will be found a number of very roughly tied up parcels with the initials of the future owner written on the outside. The one marked "C.M.S. birthday" of course should be removed before the 23rd.
> This parcel consists of some of the members of the family's presents. A later parcel may contain the rest.
> I am sorry Wiggy but I shan't be able to do anything about that pen as I find that there are no fountain pens available either in Ramsey or Douglas, perhaps somebody else will know where to lay hands on one.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> My idea of giving you a thermometer Daddy has been shattered by the fact that there are only those spring Rototherm ones available and they are in horrid cases.
> I will stick to your request and try to get "Tono Bungay"
> The shops are not very good on books. I wondered if you and Mummy would like the "Listener" for Christmas between you. (for a year I mean - not one copy!)
> I can hear the sea roaring in the distance. For the past week I have been going over to Andreas by day to try to get four Wellingtons serviceable
>
> *(p. 4)*
> enough to fly over to Jurby.
> They are in a fearful state of decay. I am enjoying H.G. Wells's book that Kit sent me. This paper is Hieratica Bond which I believe you use don't you? it's quite nice I think.
> That 'phone must have been better my end than yours. I got the information I required anyway.
> Yesterday after 'phoning you I went by train (steam) to Douglas. A bus took me to Sulby Glen to catch it.
> I did quite a lot of shopping and came back on the 9.0 pm which got to Sulby Glen at 10.20
>
> *(p. 5)*
> There was no 'bus back so I had to walk I got in at about 11.20 it's about four miles. It was a very dark night and I was glad that I wasn't living in the days of footpads and highwaymen
> While I was working Orchard came past and seemed to be quite pleased to see me, I was all oily and dishevelled and not very pleased to see him.
> He was looking for a portable search-light which is on his inventory.
> An aeroplane engine I have been working on had not been run since last April and it's been out in all weathers ever since.
>
> *(p. 6)*
> James' car is nothing to this it has white fur growing in the top of its cylinders and the crankshaft has seized up (Possibly? not known yet)
> I doubt if I'll ever get it going. I am enjoying myself although it's very cold working outside on my own but I am left to my own devices.
> I have worked on three standard most used British Engines since I have been here and do more ~~[struck]~~ advanced work than I used of which I am glad.
> You'd have been surprised and so would the N.C.Os to see me putting on a starter motor the other day by a method not seen before in the RAF I'll tell you when I see you
> That's about all for now with lots of love your loving son David

*Notes:* On Hieratica Bond paper. *Tono-Bungay* is H. G. Wells's novel; Kit had sent him another Wells.

### 10. Wednesday – Thursday, c. early December 1946 (Henry Swain's watercolour)

*Undated ("Wednesday", continued "Thursday dinner hour"). Plans to be free from 23 December; *Theirs is the Glory* (on general release in late 1946); the Christmas number of the *Illustrated London News*.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1
> 1587757 LAC Silver D.R.
> Hut 8 R+I Section
> RAF JURBY
> Isle of Man.
> Wednesday.
> My dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you for your letter, Daddy. I was fortunate enough to be on the spot when the letters were delivered and so there was no delay at this end. I got the letter at 2.45 pm yesterday Tuesday and since you posted it on Sunday I think that it was quite quick and must have come by air. Sometimes the letters take much longer and that is when I think the air service is cancelled. As you thought the weather has been the same over here howling winds and driving rain.
> For about the past fortnight I have been going over to Andreas by RAF bus or lorry at 8.30 in the morning returning ~~[struck]~~ at 12.0 and going back at 1.30 till 4.40. I have been working outside in the wet as the hangars with the exception of the one stacked with furniture have been pulled down. It's not so bad except when the lorries
>
> *(p. 2)*
> are open.
> I saw Orchard a week ago yesterday on the bus from Ramsey. He suggested that we should go down to Ramsey later in the ~~[struck]~~ week (sorry the wireless is distracting me). I was to ring him up on Thursday or Friday. I rang him up at Flying Control from Andreas ~~[struck]~~ on Friday and we went down to Ramsey together and had supper and then went to the cinema, it was a terrible film so we came out, he had already seen the good one at the other cinema. I saw it the next evening Saturday, it was "Piccadilly Incident" a British film and well worth seeing. British films are much better than American now. This week it is "Theirs is the Glory" the film about Arnhem which I think you said was on in Exeter a week or two ago.
> I can't think why I am writing so small.
> To resume the Orchard Incident which I seem to have left unfinished.
> I quite enjoyed seeing him and he seemed to have kept up with all the Norwood-ians of my time and knew all about them.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2./ Henry Drew is a qualified Brewer in Exeter Henry Swain is at the Architectural Association School in London, Michael Paynter's brother is in an Exeter Bank. Mooney is in the Fleet Air Arm. Wasn't it extraordinary I went back to camp and next morning in the reading room was looking at the ~~[struck]~~ Architect and Building News when I saw some water colours on the centre page from the Arch. Assoc. exhibition.
> There was a very good water colour of a destroyer or cruiser in dry dock by Henry T. Swain. Wasn't it a coincidence I know it's his as I remember the T. was for Thornton. It was a jolly good water colour, I was green with envy and infuriated that I am stuck here.
> It's been so wet that it's been impossible to get out at the week ends as it has with you as well. This is being written in the dinner hour and as I see every one is going to work I must stop for now.
> . . . . . .
> Thursday dinner hour.
> Last night I went to "Theirs is The Glory"
>
> *(p. 4)*
> 3. I enjoyed it very much. It was so good because it was true and nobody in it was an actor.
> How stupid to bring that Exeter School Milk Drinking competition up in Parliament.
> Thank you for that "Listener" I am not sure whether I did before. I have read most of it and was glad to see that Rocket Motorcycle broadcast wasn't missed out. I enjoyed reading about the first Men, the article about people before the Ice Age I had no idea there were any. I also enjoyed The King's Pictures. This morning I was doing E.V.T. and reading about Hellenic Architecture. During break I noticed that there is a thick Christmas Number of The Illustrated London News with stories and lovely pictures in ~~[struck]~~ the reading room.
> It has a wonderful pre-war cover most Christmas-sy.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> Now here is the advanced and not necessarily very reliable news about time off for Christmas. I think it's official but may be changed.
> At present we are free to go after duty on the 23rd That means that if they can get it we shall cross on the midnight boat on the night of the 23rd if not, by the 9.30 am boat on the 24th.
> That means that I should be in Liverpool either at about 8.0 am or at about 2.0 so in either case I should probably catch the 2.50 pm on the 24th arriving at Exeter at about eleven I think - my usual train.
> So I should have Christmas day Boxing day and on the next day the Friday I should have to leave at 10.0 pm to catch the Saturday morning boat across to the Island. What I think is so stupid is that we are to have Saturday Sunday & Monday off
>
> *(p. 6)*
> following Christmas but may not stay at home for it.
> Well that's what we are supposed to be getting so far and I'll let you know if there are any changes. So I'll be at home from late on Christmas Eve until the evening after Boxing day, three days.
> I hear to-day that the Exe has burst its banks I wonder where, near Cowley bridge I daresay. To-day it's not raining (sensation!) and the sun came out for a quarter of an hour at about half past twelve.
> I hope you enjoy the melon and that it hasn't gone bad before you eat it. I made the box for it I expect you guessed that. The straw was originally round aircraft parts. That's all for now
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> P.T.O.
>
> *(p. 7)*
> I can't think how I've not written on the back of that other sheet I've just noticed it.

*Notes:* Henry Drew, Henry Swain, Michael Paynter and Mooney were his schoolfellows at Norwood. The melon he sent home had a box he made himself, packed with straw from aircraft parts.

### 11. Wednesday, c. 11 December 1946 (Christmas leave granted)

*Headed "Wednesday 6.0 pm". He now comes home "on the Saturday night … before Christmas" for eight days; there had been a severe gale on the Sunday night.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1
> 1587757 LAC Silver. D.R.
> Hut 8 R+I Section
> RAF JURBY
> Isle of Man
> Wednesday
> 6.0pm.
> My dear Daddy & Mummy
> Better news about Christmas. There was rather a fuss about that wasted week end and we also suggested that we should forgo our December 48 hr pass. (The 48 hr pass only consists of Saturday morning as we have the afternoon and Sunday anyhow, 48 or not) The result of this has been that in view of our rather far off position we have been granted special extra leave for Christmas which just shows what a fuss will do.
> So now I come home on the Saturday night at about 11 pm before Christmas and have at
>
> *(p. 2)*
> home providing the trains are not altered until the following Friday evening which I think you will agree is very good, as from this end it means being absent from camp for 8 days.
> Uncle Basil's jokes are so subtle, (I am thinking of the postal order) that I don't think them jokes at all but am compelled to laugh at their stupidity so in a rather round about way he has made me laugh after all.
> Since Christopher's sweets and the glowing report returned to him about them I have become rather suspicious and so I hope if I may be forgiven if I still
>
> *(p. 3)*
> wonder very slightly whether that melon might have been a little too soft at the tapered end. Nothing will convince me unless Daddy gives me a report. Forgive me for being so sceptical but I can't forget what horrid sweets those Indian ones were and how old Kit was led to send some to Lynette after by the report. Please thank Wiggy for sending me that letter which made the melon sound like something from the Arabian Nights.
> I wish I could send you a kipper but I fear they won't post them. I am afraid that I am pig and not unselfish like everyone else who says they don't really want anything much for Christmas as I do want lots of things I will enumerate them at the end of the letter but Mother only one
>
> *(p. 4)*
> thing is intended per person so don't let me find that the feminine side of the family has given me the lot.
> The articles should be obtainable but if not obtainable from the first shop tried don't hunt for them.
> If very much more expensive than the figures stated please don't buy them as I should only be disappointed that they had cost such a lot for so little.
> This letter seems to be full of tickings off, warnings, injunctions threats and commands - I am sorry if it seems so to you.
> I am very tired - in fact I could fall asleep on my bed while I write. To-day has been a bit grim. As you probably heard the gale was very strong on the island on Sunday night. I thought at one time while crossing the camp that I shouldn't be able to stand up in it.
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3
> However it didn't get as bad as that. All the hangars at Andreas have been pulled down now and so we work exposed to the driving rain now. We have great heavy covers like sails with which we cover the engines and parts on which we are working. The water gets into everything. Yesterday a great storm broke in the afternoon, it was miserable sleet and slush but fortunately didn't last too long. I keep getting very wet but it doesn't seem to hurt me, fortunately except for occasional cramp. Funnily enough although it's so cold outside I find cold indoors much more aggravating. I have been wrestling with a generator to get it onto a "Master" aircraft to-day and it's taken me the whole day. It was a cold north wind with great heaped up snow clouds
>
> *(p. 6)*
> and now again a beautiful yellow sun with lots of long shadows and trees like beautiful skeletons, only I can't imagine a beautiful skeleton.
> Please tell me is this an epigram? "The air-force makes me physically strong but mentally weak." I keep reading of people making epigrams and I don't know what they are. I don't think it is an epigram perhaps if I had said "the airforce is an organization which makes a man physically strong but mentally weak" it might have been one, anyway it's true whether it's an epigram or not. Reading it through I still don't think it's an epigram.
> We have sometimes a closed lorry now that rolls fearfully. The other day it rolled into the parapet of a bridge but
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4
> we were all right and didn't even stop it lost some paint and gave us a bit of a turn in so doing. To-day a different driver put it into a horrible skid on the way back that made even the stoutest hearts quaver. We all fell in a heap in the back but he corrected the skid.
> The roads are very narrow and rough and they insist in driving like maniacs which to us in the back is rather inconsiderate as we have to cling on like limpets, excuse me scribbling I see a lot of limpets holding on like people in a lorry.
> [Drawing: a limpet-like figure clinging to the tailboard of a speeding lorry, saying "Steady!!"]
> Fortunately there is hardly any traffic and from our point of view the lorries are very large and strong.
> We ticked the drivers off when we arrived at our destinations very loudly and extra fiercely in relief at
>
> *(p. 8)*
> our being in one piece (each)
> Andreas looks funny now that the hangars have been taken away. I want to see the aerodrome ploughed up or with sheep on it as I'd like to see all aerodromes. This aerophobia will be a mania just as long as I am in the air force I expect. Everybody is awfully disappointed about the demob news particularly as Mr Attlee seems to be quite content to let the unequal demobbing go on. There is a sailor here, one of the fleet air arm who has only just been in the navy over a year and expects to be out in February he's 66 group. Dinah should be out of the Wrens soon I imagine, or is she out all ready? I can't seem to remember.
> I think that's about all for tonight with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* The last two sheets were scanned in the wrong order. Uncle Basil sent a joke postal order. Dinah was in the Wrens. Mr Attlee was Prime Minister.

## 1947

### 12. Saturday 4 January 1947 (back from Christmas; his 23rd birthday)

*Dated "Saturday 4th". Written after his Christmas leave and his birthday (2 January) and New Year's Day.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1. 1587757 LAC Silver D.R.
> Hut 8 R&I Section
> {which means:- Repair and Inspection Section}
> RAF Jurby. Isle of Man
> Saturday 4th
> My dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you very much for all the good wishes and lovely letters. Thank you also very much for all the trouble you took to try and let me know about the forty eight hour extension. I am so sorry that you spent such a rotten night. I was afraid that you might be sitting up all night waiting for me, so while you were trying to get me back, I was sitting in the train speeding North and trying to decide whether I would have been right or wrong to get off at Bristol. Fortunately for my peace of mind it never occurred to me that after 'phoning Bristol you might have guessed that the extension did not apply to me.
> However to begin at the beginning, I settled myself in the carriage with the soldier, and began to read "Mr Polly".
>
> *(p. 2)*
> He had such a horrible cough and made no attempt to control it. He asked me if the train were going to Shrewsbury. So realizing that I should have to be in the carriage most of the night with him and also that he didn't want the windows open, I got out at Taunton and got into a more comfortable carriage, a corner seat again. I went to sleep after Bridgwater until the train pulled into Temple Mead station. How glad I am that I didn't have my bicycle with me it would have been an awful bother from now on.
> I heard the loudspeaker say "Lac ...... of the R.A.F. Travelling from Exeter to ...." and I wondered what the rest of it was and stuck my head out of the window. It was evidently the second time however for the loudspeakers remained silent.
> I asked two people if the name had been "Silver" and they got very excited and said yes yes it was and that I must go to the office of platform nine.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> 2. Just then I saw an airman who came from Jurby and he confirmed what they said. I got out and finding that the train was already ~~out~~ at platform nine I walked down to the luggage and guards vans and asked the guard if I had time to go to find out about this message. He said I had and that he'd look out for me. I went down to the end of the platform and met a man who said quite excitedly "is your name SLIVER?" I corrected him and he said I had an extension of leave but should see Mr Somebody or Other in the office. Mr Somebody also thought my name Sliver but was very affable and gave me your message adding that they had had to open up the broadcasting system specially for me as it had closed for the night. They (there were two of them) also called me 'Sir', so many times that I thought they were after a tip but didn't give them one.
> They gave me the time of the next train to Exeter
>
> *(p. 4)*
> two o'clock, and I left them after thanking them.
> Outside however I didn't take the news at its face value and wondered whether I had time to get to the R.T.O and confirm the news officially before the train went. You see I had no right to leave the last train that could get me back in time without official information. I knew you probably didn't know what time the pass ended officially and it can make quite a difference. I still hadn't heard the text of the broadcast and so knew no dates for certain.
> I remembered the Jurby airman I'd seen and knowing that he came from Bristol wondered why he had come to the station if he had an extension. So I went to look for him.
> I found him at the front of the train and asked him if knew about the extension, he said he did and that the R.T.O. at Bristol had said that it did not apply to us as they had phoned the BBC in Bristol
>
> *(p. 5)*
> 3. for a confirmation of the dates 29th and 30th. So I got into the train just as it started to move.
> In a crowded overheated smoky carriage with glaring lights we threshed the problem over and over again. You see we both thought that the Air Ministry had meant the extension to apply to us but did not think of anybody having to be back at 2359 on the 28th as most stations would make it 0800 hrs on the 29th.
> The civilians in the carriage joined in and tried to get us to turn back then everybody went to sleep, except me. They all slept with their mouths open and snored and their heads lolled all over the place till I felt that I was looking at some picture by Graham Sutherland or Henry Moore.
> I kept thinking of you expecting me in about 3.40 am and wondering what sort of scene had
>
> *(p. 6)*
> taken place after the broadcast.
> I felt less easy about it than the other airman as I knew him to be a blockhead of the first order but I thought he couldn't have got the R.T.Os message wrong.
> So "Mr Polly" and I went on towards Crewe for I could not sleep. At Hereford and Shrewsbury the R.T.O, the telephone and the telegraph all seemed inaccessible because of the shortness of the stops we made.
> It wasn't until I got to Crewe that I woke up the soldier in the R.T.Os office and found the extension didn't apply to me It was almost a relief, I had come so far and it would have cost so much to return. I sent the telegram because I thought that to phone at that hour (five am) would be useless
>
> *(p. 7)*
> 4. I slept the hour from Crewe to Liverpool, had a horrid breakfast in the Y.M. and walked through the still-dark streets to the docks. The trams were packed with poorly dressed men and women, the men with cloth caps and mufflers and the women with shawls on their heads. Here and there people ~~[struck]~~ were sweeping out shop doorways, laying newspapers out on window ledges weighted by stones and letting up blinds. I couldn't get onto the ship until a quarter to nine and when I did, I made my way below to get a bunk. This I succeeded in doing but was turned out an hour later by a steward who had to turn them all into seats during the day time and was being tipped to get some customers seats. I unreasonably disliked them, they made me think
>
> *(p. 8)*
> of the wife of somebody who used to say "Icey-creamio" which is supposed to be a very guarded way of letting you know who I mean. I felt that they had had a good night's sleep. However as I say it was unreasonable and for all I know they might have come from Penzance.
> So after sitting as in a railway carriage for a while the heat and the motion of the ship which was rolling heavily was too much for me and I came up above.
> The sun was out, the white horses were racing past, the sea was blue and there was a keen wind and I felt much better. So much better that I was soon able to stand going below to have a shave and wash and then having lived on your provisions and Mr Polly's entertainment all night I found that the dining room
>
> *(p. 9)*
> 5. was open and that lunch was being served. This I had and the ship rolled abominably.
> I was intrigued by the fact that I couldn't draw my chair up to the table as it was fixed to the floor. The lunch was rather like a railway one. I felt better and came on deck (by the way while shaving I noticed that my face was dead white with a pink spot in each cheek like Petrouchka)
> ~~There~~ I finished "Mr Polly" and finding another tenant of my billet, spent the rest of the trip with him and also went with him to "Great Expectations" on arrival at Douglas. It was well worth seeing as I hope you found.
> I went to sleep directly my head touched the pillow that night
>
> *(p. 10)*

> In the evening as the next day was my birthday and the film was only on for the first half of the week I went to "Caesar and Cleopatra" in Ramsey. I'd seen it before about two years ago but I enjoyed it again.
>
> *(p. 11)*
> 6. On my birthday I got all your letters in the afternoon and Wiggy's book and James' purse. I did Architecture for E.V.T. all the morning and it was a sunny day with blue sky so it was quite nice.
> The book is jolly good on houses and the purse is the admiration of every one who sees me draw it out every time I pay for anything. It's also jolly useful as I've got holes in my trouser pockets.
> I got a registered letter.
> It's roaring today and the wind is howling. You can work the foregoing remark out if you think. Here's another, what a horrible scene enacted by L.T. qui squatte.
> This afternoon I shall write letters after letter it's a good
>
> *(p. 12)*
> thing it's raining.
> That's about all for now except thank you for everything
> with lots of love
> your loving son
> David.
> P.S. Mummy

> Also John O' London have advised me as they put it that you will receive the first copy on January 15th or thereabouts.
> (Look Sackville West up in my Artists and Authors year book please)
> My leave will be on February 7th I can't get it any earlier.

### 13. A Sunday, c. 12 January 1947 (rheumatism)

*Undated ("Sunday"). His leave is due on 6 February, "just over three weeks from now".*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 LAC Silver. D.R.
> Hut 8 R+I Section
> RAF Jurby
> Isle of Man.
> Sunday.
> My dear Mummy & Daddy
> I have a streaming cold so I can sympathize with Lavender. Furthermore my right knee is bandaged so tightly that I can't bend it. I shall just get through this letter and then lie on my bed all the afternoon. On Thursday night I found that having had a little rheumatism in my right arm it had somehow got into my right knee. I had been kneeling on this while working. I had worn waterproof trousers but the damp must have got through. In the morning I could scarcely bend my knee. As I had rather a job to walk I thought I'd better see the M.O. He said there was some fluid on my knee and put me on Light Duties for four days
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I had some stuff called Lin. Meth. Sal rubbed onto my knee and a "Jones" bandage lots of cotton wool and bandages. I was able to hobble along very slowly. I went to the hangar and there the flight sergeant who has been replaced but seems to be taking his time about going, told me that Light Duties meant that I was excused station parades and fire picquet, but must keep at work. Normally one is put on office work or something of that sort but he packed me off to Andreas as usual. I refused to stand in the back of the lorry and had his seat in the cab. It rained all day and I wore the waterproof clothes. They have no ventilation so must be unhealthy. In the evening I couldn't walk fortunately I had a forty eight for Saturday and Sunday. I couldn't sleep on Friday night I've come to the conclusion that I've never had rheumatism properly before this was like fire. I took some aspirins knowing they were salicylic acid.
>
> *(p. 3)*
> At about three o'clock after some more aspirin I got to sleep and yesterday morning woke much better to find my knee bendable. I stayed in bed all Saturday morning and got up in the afternoon and walked about a little, I found that I'd got a cold.
> My leg's all right now but I keep getting it in my left elbow and my cold's a real snorter. However I feel quite cheerful although it's such a bitter tale of woe. I read Stalky & Co yesterday. I am going to embark on the Moonstone to-day I hope. I heard that bit of Beau Geste when they joined the Foreign Legion.
> I'll see if I can get any Brylcreem, now and again it comes in, I believe. I'd forgotten about that cow in Great Expectations I think its voice was meant to be in his imagination but I agree that it might well have been left out. I am glad you saw it anyway.
>
> *(p. 4)*
> I hope to come on leave on Feb 6th arriving at home on Friday night the 7th just over three weeks from now I think. I shall only have the inside of a week at home and must be back in camp on the Thursday night thus giving me at home from very late Friday night till nine thirty on Wednesday - less than I had at Christmas. I will be glad to be demobbed.
> I wonder how James is getting on with the inquiries. I thought of a good joke to draw but am infuriated because I've forgotten it. Well that's about all for now, if I were you I'd burn this letter my ~~[struck]~~ cold is the infectious kind I am sure
> Lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

*Notes:* "Lin. Meth. Sal." is methyl salicylate liniment. He reads *Stalky & Co* and *The Moonstone*.

### 16. Sunday 2 March 1947 (a sketch at Ballaugh)

*Dated "Sunday 2nd March" (a Sunday in 1947).*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 LAC Silver.
> Hut 8, R&I Section
> R.A.F. Jurby.
> Isle of Man.
> Sunday 2nd March
> My dear Mummy and Daddy,
> Last Sunday I walked along the road to Ballaugh (pronounced Bal-lakk) until I came to a cottage which was falling to pieces. I intend to enclose the results of the sketch I started on there. I ~~was standing~~ stood in the road with the board resting on the top of a granite wall. I had three colours brown, blue and yellow, and a Gibbs toothpaste tin to mix them in. While I was drawing a man came up the hill pushing a bicycle. He suggested that I ought to draw the cottage from the back. Seeing no point in doing so as the cottage was in the shade at the back, I
>
> *(p. 2)*
> asked him why the back of the cottage would make a better sketch than the front. He replied that the field ~~in front~~ behind the cottage was what I should have drawn. He said that a few days before an Austrian professor had finished excavating in this field having found the remains of an old Celtic(?) chieftain and various urns and flints etc, about 2000 years old. Why I had to sketch the field I don't know. Then he told me to visit Ballaugh church as he said it was 14th Century. (None of this may be accurate, he didn't look as if he'd be very reliable). He said that the doorway was interesting as there were two stone "pillows" leaning in toward each other: thus:- [tiny sketch: two slanting stones]
> I nearly laughed when he said
>
> *(p. 3)*

> We were called out on Tuesday night, it was blowing a blizzard. We were told that we should have to patrol the camp all night one hour on, three hours off, measuring the snow and reporting drifts to the snow plough team. Fortunately they cancelled the order, I went to bed and later they got some other
>
> *(p. 4)*
> unfortunates to shovel snow at 3.0 am as the snow plough was stuck. They carried on doing this all night and never got time off, so I am glad I didn't get called out.

> Yesterday afternoon was not bad but not as nice as to-day. I did a little drawing. To-day It is lovely and of course I may not go out of camp so I had to content
>
> *(p. 5)*
> myself by going down to between two hangars out of the wind but in the sun and, coatless, sitting on a pair of steps sketching a view across the aerodrome. Once again in 3 colours, this one has I think more attractive colour combinations. It looks better from about 6 feet away than right close up. I was rather pleased as I thought with the exception of the pen work it looks rather more grown up than my recent efforts. > Demob news gives me cause to think; 50 and 51 groups getting
>
> *(p. 6)*
> out in March. If they continue at two groups a month I would be out in May which might be inconveniently early, if however they resume one group a month that brings me out in July which would be very nice, I think, if I start my term in October. I have written to Birmingham and Manchester for prospectuses. I can't remember which it was we agreed you would do and which I would, so I have written, in case I'd said I would. I must write to London University about the exams.

> With lots of love
> your loving son
> David.

### 17. A Wednesday, c. early March 1947 (to his mother, unwell)

*Undated ("Wednesday"), sending the sketches of the last two weekends, so a few days after the 2 March letter.*

> *(p. 1)*
> The 1587757 LAC Silver.
> Hut 8 R&I Section
> RAF JURBY
> I.O.M.
> Wednesday
> My dearest Mummy
> I am so sorry to hear that you are not very well and hope that you'll soon be better. I am sending the sketches I did during the last two weekends so you'll be able to look at them while you are in bed. It's a pity that I can't collect any aubretia and lobelia flowers and put them in a tiny vase for you like I did when I was small, but apart from its being the wrong time of year I am not at home- I expect to be demobbed sometime during the summer between May and July, which is such a nice time of the year to be at home in; eight weeks leave with pay!
> I hope you are not freezing
>
> *(p. 2)*
> I think I did hear on the wireless that it was going to be warmer and rain in the Southwest. The sun comes out every day now and is quite warm and when I went out to sketch at the week end I could hear lambs bleating and rather to my surprise there was a lark too. (not bleating, of course!!)
> I do hope you are quite well soon again. I shall be getting leave towards the end of April I hope and I might get a few days at Easter.
> With tons of love
> your loving son
> David.
> [Bottom left:] P.S. I sent Lynette a beautiful blue rabbit, very soft with a white tail like a powder puff, the inside of its ears were blue suède [tiny drawing of the rabbit]

### 18. A Tuesday, c. mid-March 1947 (to his father and Wiggs)

*Undated ("Tuesday"), to "Daddy & Wiggs" while his mother is in the Devon & Exeter Hospital; the March 1947 floods in the Cambridge area are on the BBC; leave provisionally fixed for 14 April.*

> *(p. 1)*
> 1587757 LAC Silver D.R.
> Hut 8. R+I Section
> R.A.F. Jurby
> Isle of Man.
> Tuesday
> My dear Daddy & Wiggs
> I am glad the trouble with Mother is not as serious as you had been afraid it might be. Thank you very much for keeping me posted with information as I am sure it must have been a very trying time for you and not one at which letter writing would be easy.
> I am glad in a way that Mummy went to the Devon & Exeter Hospital as apart from the hands she's in it was better that she should have company, to occupy her mind.
>
> *(p. 2)*
> What a pity the death watches didn't stay away.
> The letter you re-addressed to me was another appeal for the War Memorial Fund for K.S.B. I shall have to give something but I can't afford to do their covenant scheme over seven years. I knew 25 of the 56 people given on the list as killed in the war. Five of them were in the studies at the same time as me.
> The Cambridge area seems to be suffering from the floods and St Ives was mentioned on the B.B.C. Is Barbara still there? I enjoyed the time I was at Oakington, and it seems nice to be able to visualize the country when it's mentioned on the wireless. I have a pamphlet (up-to-date) on the Isle of Man
>
> *(p. 3)*
> It says that Income tax is charged like this on the Isle of Man in the £
> income not exceeding £500 — 3s 0d
> " exceeding £500 but not exceeding £1500 [sic] — 3s 9d
> income exceeding £1000 but not above £1500 — 4s 6d
> Personal allowances Married £225.
> I thought that it seemed nice and low
> Rates vary between Ramsey 11/5¾d, Douglas 9/8¾d and Lezayre 4/8½d.
> We have milder weather now but last week end the trains from Douglas on Saturday evening were snowed up.
> I have Manchester University prospectus and also Birmingham. Birmingham is no good but Manchester
>
> *(p. 4)*
> looks quite promising except that ~~entry~~ students are selected from the applications by their qualifications (academic) and there isn't a lot of room.
> Please give me any news you can about when not to have any leave etc. I have provisionally fixed it for the 14th April but it looks as if I might have to move it on. I daren't move it too far for fear of losing it as you can't get leave if you are soon to be demobbed. I expect now to be demobbed any time after the beginning of June. I have been wondering whether to put it off for a month as it's such a long time till October if I can get accepted.
> With lots of love
> from
> David.

*Notes:* The King's School, Bruton, war memorial appeal listed 56 old boys killed; he had known 25 of them. He has prospectuses from Manchester and Birmingham Universities.

## A letter from Christopher

A letter from David's brother Christopher (Kit) to their parents, on Radcliffe Infirmary, Oxford, notepaper, filed with these (c. autumn 1946): his plans for hospital jobs at the Brompton, Newcastle and the Queen Elizabeth Hospital for Children in Poplar.

> *(p. 1)*
> THE RADCLIFFE INFIRMARY, OXFORD [printed heading]
> Dear Mummy & Daddy
> Thank you very much for the two letters which I enjoyed. I am glad the polys are picked & to hear the grass is cut.
> This week I have only been outside the hospital on Sunday & Monday of this week but I have done rather less work than before as there is no waiting list & only got up once in the night. Wilson came to Oxford on Friday: do you remember he came to supper with us? He now has something to do with nutrition in Germany and is in RAF as a Squadron leader & expects to be out in December.
> My plan of action is now carefully arranged so that I don't get more than one job at once:—
> About Dec 1st I expect to apply to the Brompton Hospital for a job beginning Feb 1st
>
> *(p. 2)*
> about 31 Dec I shall apply to the General Hospital, Newcastle on Tyne, for a children's job beginning 1 Feb & if that doesn't bring something then I shall apply for one beginning 1 March at the Queen Elizabeth Hospital for Children in Poplar. But probably this won't work out according to plan. I met a man who was an H.P. in Sheffield on Thursday when I was.
> I can't find any bottles of wine anywhere which look worth having. I expect Childs next weekend. I must hurry now to catch the post. All the shares have looked up so much that this must be a bad time to buy. I enclose something that you may already have seen. I should be home in about 6 weeks now.
> with lots of love
> Christopher.
